@ 26 | The Supreme Court
## Establishment and integrated judiciary
- The Constitution sets up an **integrated (single) judicial system** with the Supreme Court at the top and High Courts below it.
- This single judiciary system was adopted from the Government of India Act, 1935.
- The Supreme Court of India was inaugurated on **28 January 1950**. {1}
- The Supreme Court was established by the **Constitution** (Part V, Chapter IV, Article 124); it succeeded the Federal Court of India set up under the Government of India Act, 1935. {2}
- **Articles 124 to 147** (Part V) deal with the Supreme Court: establishment, composition and jurisdiction.
- The Supreme Court is the highest judicial forum and the final court of appeal.
- The **Sapru Committee** Report (1945), led by Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru, made recommendations on appointment of Supreme Court and High Court judges. {3}
- The Sapru Committee was appointed by the Non-Party Conference in November 1944 to address minority issues; its report had 21 recommendations on constitutional questions.
- The Constituent Assembly appointed an Ad hoc Committee on the Supreme Court for recommendations on the judiciary.
- Its members: S. Varadachariar (former Federal Court judge), Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, B.L. Mitter, K.M. Munshi (eminent advocates) and B.N. Rau (Constitutional Adviser, former high-ranking judicial officer).
- Its report (21 May 1947) dealt with the Supreme Court's jurisdiction and proposed two alternative methods of appointing its judges.
## Number of judges
- Article 124(1): Supreme Court = Chief Justice of India and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, not more than **33** other judges (originally 7).
- The power to increase the number of Supreme Court judges is vested in **Parliament**. {4}
- The original Constitution of 1950 provided a Chief Justice and 7 puisne judges.
- In the early years all the judges sat together to hear cases; the number was raised as work and arrears grew.

## Appointment and qualifications
- Article 124(2): every Supreme Court judge is appointed by the **President** by warrant under his hand and seal. {9}
- The President appoints after consulting such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as he deems necessary; the Chief Justice is appointed after such consultation.
- For appointing a judge other than the Chief Justice, the **Chief Justice of India** shall always be consulted. {23}
- Consultation with the CJI was made obligatory by a decision of 1993.
- In 1998 the Supreme Court said CJI's consultation means consultation of the collegium = CJI and four other senior-most judges.
- Article 124(3) qualifications: citizen of India, and High Court judge for at least 5 years (one or more courts in succession), or High Court advocate for at least **10 years**, or a distinguished jurist in the President's opinion. {8}
- No minimum age is prescribed for appointment as a Supreme Court judge.
- A person appointed as a Supreme Court judge makes and subscribes an oath or affirmation before the President.
- The Third Schedule has 10 forms of oaths or affirmations: for Union and State Ministers, MPs, members of State Legislatures, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and the CAG.
- The oath "bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution… uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India… perform the duties of my office… uphold the Constitution and the laws" is for the **Chief Justice of India** and the CAG. {93}
## Collegium and NJAC
- The **Collegium System** for appointing judges was adopted by the Supreme Court in **1993**. {24}
- It has its origin in three Supreme Court judgments together called the Three Judges Cases.
- The Supreme Court Collegium = Chief Justice of India and **4** senior-most judges. {25}
- The Collegium is a five-member body headed by the CJI; judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts are appointed only through the collegium system. {26}
- The Collegium considers elevation of judges to the Supreme Court and of High Court judges as Chief Justices; in a disagreement the majority opinion prevails.
- The collegium system of appointment and transfer of judges evolved through Supreme Court judgments, not by an Act of Parliament or a constitutional provision.
- It arose because the Constitution requires consultation with the CJI before judicial appointments.
- After the Second Judges Case (1993) the collegium at first had the CJI and the two senior-most judges.
- Second Judges Case (1993): "consultation" means concurrence, binding the President to the CJI's consultation.
- The collegium was not introduced by Justice P.N. Bhagwati in the First Judges Case.
- The **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)** was brought by the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** to replace the collegium system.
- The Supreme Court struck down the NJAC in **2015** as unconstitutional; the collegium system continues, and India has no NJAC at present.
- NJAC composition: CJI (ex-officio Chairperson), two senior-most Supreme Court judges, Union Minister of Law and Justice, and two eminent persons. {27}
- The two eminent persons were to be nominated by a committee of the Prime Minister, the CJI and the Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha (or leader of the single largest opposition party).
- The **Second Administrative Reforms Commission** recommended appointing judges of higher courts with participation of the Executive, the Legislature and the Chief Justice of India. {28}
## Tenure, resignation and removal
- A Supreme Court judge holds office until the age of **65 years**; High Court judges retire at 62. {15,16}
- A judge resigns by writing under his hand addressed to the **President**. {10,11}
- Article 124(4): a judge is removed only by an order of the **President** after an address by each House of Parliament. {12,14}
- The address needs a special majority in each House: majority of total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
- The address must be presented to the President in the same session.
- Grounds of removal: **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**.
- These removals can be declared unconstitutional and illegal in extraordinary conditions.
- The Constitution gives the grounds of removal but does not define "proved misbehaviour" or "incapacity".
- The details of the removal process are in the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**; the removal motion needs the special majority in each House. {13}
- Under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 the Speaker of Lok Sabha can reject the motion to remove a judge.
- The word "impeachment" is used only for removing the President; for Supreme Court and High Court judges the Constitution uses the word "remove", though the process is impeachment-like.
- Supreme Court judges are not removed by the Chief Justice of India.
## Salary, independence and after retirement
- Article 125(1): salaries (and other allowances) of Supreme Court judges are determined by **Parliament** by law; until then as in the Second Schedule. {17}
- Safeguards of Supreme Court autonomy: the President must consult the CJI in appointing judges, and judges' salaries are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (not voted). {18}
- Consulting the CJI curtails the absolute discretion of the Executive and keeps political or practical considerations out of judicial appointments.
- The CJI appoints officers and servants of the Supreme Court without executive interference and can prescribe their conditions of service.
- Supreme Court administrative expenses (salaries, allowances, pensions of officers and servants) are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India; those of a High Court on the Consolidated Fund of the State. {19}
- Article 124(7): a person who has been a Supreme Court judge cannot plead or act in **any court** or before any authority in India. {20}
- Article 220: a former permanent High Court judge cannot plead or act in any court or authority in India except the Supreme Court and the other High Courts.
## Acting, ad hoc and retired judges
- Article 126: the **Acting Chief Justice** of India is appointed by the **President** when the office is vacant or the Chief Justice cannot perform his duties. {21,22}
- The Constitution provides **ad hoc judges** in the Supreme Court; there is no provision for additional judges in the Supreme Court. {29,31}
- Article 127(1): ad hoc judges are appointed when there is **no quorum** of judges to hold or continue a session of the Court. {30}
- The CJI, with the previous consent of the President and after consulting the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned, requests in writing a High Court judge qualified to be a Supreme Court judge to sit as ad hoc judge for the needed period.
- Article 128: the CJI, with the previous consent of the President, may request a retired judge of the Supreme Court or Federal Court, or a retired High Court judge qualified for the Supreme Court, to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge. {32}
- Article 215: every High Court is a court of record with power to punish for its contempt; as a court of record it can also review and correct its own judgments, orders or decisions.
## Seat, benches and language
- Article 130: the Supreme Court sits in Delhi or at such other place as the CJI appoints with the **approval of the President**. {46}
- At least **5 judges** must hear a case involving a substantial question of law on interpretation of the Constitution; this is the Constitutional Bench. {33,34}
- Benches generally have an odd number of judges (3, 5, 7, 9, 11) to avoid a tie of votes or views.
- Largest bench so far = 13 judges, Keshavananda Bharati (1973).
- Second largest bench = 11 judges, **Golaknath vs State of Punjab (1967)**. {51}
- **Article 348(1)(a)**: all proceedings in the Supreme Court and every High Court shall be in the English language. {127}
## Court of Record and contempt
- **Article 129**: the Supreme Court is a Court of Record with all powers of such a court, including power to punish for contempt of itself.
- Court of Record = its judgments and proceedings are recorded for perpetual memory, have evidentiary value and cannot be questioned in any court. {41}
! For "Court of Record implies", both evidentiary value and power to punish for contempt are correct; the U.P. Commission took evidentiary value as the answer.
- Both the Supreme Court (Article 129) and the High Courts (Article 215) are Courts of Record. {42}
- Article 124 = establishment and constitution of the Supreme Court; Court of Record is Article 129, not Article 124. {43}
- The **H.N. Sanyal Committee** was set up in 1961; on its recommendation the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** was passed. {44}
- The Constitution does not define "contempt of court" (civil or criminal); it is defined in the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971.
- Parliament has the power to make laws on contempt of court.
- Section 2(b), Contempt of Courts Act, 1971: wilful disobedience of a judgment, decree, direction, order or writ of a court = civil contempt.
- **M.L. Sachdev vs Union of India**: wilful non-compliance with court orders held to be contempt of court under Article 32. {45}
## Original jurisdiction
- The Supreme Court has **original, appellate and advisory** jurisdiction. {54}
- Collegium system, appellate jurisdiction and original jurisdiction are all concerned with the Supreme Court. {55}
- **Article 131** (original jurisdiction): disputes between the Government of India and one or more States; between the Centre and State(s) on one side and State(s) on the other; between two or more States. {35,36,37,38}
- This jurisdiction is exclusive, and applies only if the dispute involves a question of law or fact on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.
- It does not extend to disputes arising from pre-Constitution treaties, agreements, covenants, engagements or similar instruments that continue in operation or that exclude such jurisdiction.
- Election disputes of Parliament or State Legislatures and disputes between the Centre and a Union Territory are not in the original jurisdiction.
- As a federal court, the Supreme Court decides disputes between units of the Indian federation.
## Appellate jurisdiction
- **Articles 132 to 136** deal with the appellate jurisdiction of the Supreme Court; it is the highest court of appeal.
- All cases on interpretation of the Constitution can be brought to the Supreme Court under its **appellate jurisdiction**. {57}
- Constitutional cases: **Article 132 read with Article 134A** gives the appellate jurisdiction. {56}

- **TDSAT** orders: on telecom, broadcasting and airport tariff matters appeal lies to the Supreme Court (only on substantial questions of law); on cyber matters appeal lies to the High Court. {99}
- TDSAT (Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal) was set up when the TRAI Act, 1997 was amended in 2000, to adjudicate disputes and dispose of appeals.
## Review, curative petition and Article 142
- **Article 137**: the Supreme Court can review (change) any judgment or order made by it, subject to Parliament's law and rules under Article 145. {59,60,61}
- **Curative Petition** evolved in Rupa Ashok Hurra vs Ashok Hurra (2002); it can be filed in the Supreme Court under **Article 142**. {58}
- Article 142(1): the Supreme Court may pass any decree or order needed for **complete justice** in a matter before it, enforceable throughout India as Parliament's law or the President's order prescribes.
- Limits in ordinary laws cannot limit Article 142 powers = the Supreme Court is not constrained by laws made by Parliament in using these powers. {62}
- **Article 141**: law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts in India.
- **Article 139A**: the Supreme Court can transfer any case anywhere in India (send any case to any court). {94}
- If cases with the same substantial questions of law are pending in the Supreme Court and High Courts, it may withdraw and dispose of them itself, on its own motion or on application by the Attorney-General or a party.
- **Article 138**: Parliament, by making a law, can enlarge the jurisdiction and powers of the Supreme Court on any Union List matter. {84,85}
## Advisory jurisdiction
- **Article 143**: the **President** may seek the Supreme Court's opinion on a question of law or fact of public importance; the Court advises only when he seeks it. {86,89,90,91}
- Second ground: disputes arising from a pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant, engagement or similar instrument.
- In the first case the Court may give or refuse its opinion; in the second case it is bound to give its opinion.
- A full bench (5 members) hears a reference; the opinion given is **not binding** on the Government. {88}
- Under **Article 143(1)** the President can refer the process of appointment and transfer of Supreme Court and High Court judges. {87}
- In August 2002 President Dr. Abdul Kalam sought advice under **Article 143** on the Election Commission - Government controversy over Gujarat elections. {92}
- Issues referred: limits of Election Commission powers under Article 324, impact of Article 174 on them, and whether the Commission could recommend President's rule in a State.
## Writs and fundamental rights
- **Article 32** = Right to Constitutional Remedies; the Supreme Court issues directions, orders or writs to enforce Fundamental Rights (Part III). {83}
- Enforcement of Fundamental Rights lies with both the Supreme Court (Article 32) and the High Courts (Article 226). {82}
- Issuing writs is therefore **not an exclusive power** of the Supreme Court. {39}
- High Court writ jurisdiction under Article 226 is wider: for fundamental rights, other constitutional rights and other purposes.
## Guardian of the Constitution
- The Supreme Court is the final authority to interpret the Constitution and is the **Guardian / Custodian** of the Constitution. {76,77,78,79,80}
- The Supreme Court is the highest court, a federal court, guarantor of citizens' fundamental rights and final interpreter of the Constitution. {75}
- The Constitution is the supreme law of India and a living document; it sets the political principles, structure, procedures, powers and duties of government institutions, Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and duties of citizens.
- The Supreme Court does **not** have exclusive jurisdiction on the constitutional validity of central laws; High Courts also have it. {81}
! On the assertion "Supreme Court has exclusive jurisdiction on constitutional validity of central laws", UPPSC took both assertion and reason as true with reason explaining it, which is wrong.
## Judicial review
- **Judicial review** = power of the judiciary to interpret the Constitution and declare any law or order of the legislature or executive void if it conflicts with the Constitution. {63,64,65}
- The doctrine originated and developed in the USA; first propounded in **Marbury vs Madison (1803)** by Chief Justice John Marshall.
- In India the power of judicial review is with both the **Supreme Court and the High Courts**. {67}
- The Supreme Court has declared judicial review a part of the basic structure.
- Judicial review exists in both **India and the USA**; in the UK Parliament is supreme and its law cannot be challenged in court. {69}
- **Article 13** provides for judicial review: pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with Part III are void, and the State shall not make a law that takes away or abridges Part III rights. {66}
- Articles establishing judicial review: 13, 32, 136, 137, 226 and 227.
- Judicial review in India is based on **procedure established by law** (and supremacy of the Constitution). {72}
- A Constitution (Amendment) Act can be struck down if it removes the Right of Equality before law from Part III and puts it elsewhere (Article 13(2)). {74}
- The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 barred High Courts from deciding constitutional validity of central laws; the 43rd Amendment Act, 1977 restored the original position.
- A High Court can invalidate a central law, and the Supreme Court can question a constitutional amendment that violates the basic structure. {68}
- The scope of judicial review is limited in India; the Constitution is "a bag of borrowings"; both true but the second does not explain the first. {70}
- Three reasons for limited scope: review mainly when a law affects the basic structure; the Court has outlined the division of sovereign power among legislature, executive and judiciary; the huge size of the Constitution.
- **Due Process of Law** in essence = the principle of **natural justice**; it ensures the law-making procedure is free from arbitrariness and irrationality. {71}
- Natural justice = fairness, reasonableness, equity and equality; a Common Law concept, like the American procedural due process.
- Two principles of natural justice: "Nemo judex in causa sua" (no one a judge in his own cause; rule against bias) and "Audi alteram partem" (hear the other party; no one condemned unheard).
- Natural justice is grounded in Articles 14 and 21; the Supreme Court recognised Due Process of Law in the Maneka Gandhi case.
- Judicial process in India is based on procedure established by law, the Constitution, conventions and the due process of routine law, all together. {73}
- Article 14: the State shall not deny equality before law or equal protection of laws; equal protection is from the American Constitution, equality before law from the British Constitution.
## Basic structure
- **Keshavananda Bharati vs State of Kerala (1973)**: the Supreme Court propounded the Doctrine of Basic Structure; it is not from Indira Sawhney, Shankari Prasad or Rudal Shah cases. {47,48}
- A 13-judge bench, by 7:6 majority, held Parliament can amend the Constitution under Article 368 only so far as it does not damage or destroy the basic structure.
- Constitutional amendments are subject to judicial review; a substantive non-legislative check on Parliament's amending power.
- The source of the Basic Structure doctrine is **judicial interpretation**; it is not found in the Constitution. {50}
- The Keshavananda view was approved in the Minerva Mills case, 1980.
- **Articles 32, 226 and 227** were declared part of the inviolable basic structure in Keshavananda Bharati. {49}
- Judicial review by High Courts (Article 226) and the Supreme Court (Article 32) is an integral and essential feature; Article 227 = High Court's superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory.

## Public Interest Litigation and judicial activism
- **PIL** = Public Interest Litigation. {114}
- PIL was introduced by **judicial initiative**; it is a judicially developed process. {112}
- PIL agrees with Article 39A: prompt social justice with the help of law.
- Before the 1980s only the aggrieved party could approach courts; after the Emergency the Supreme Court let any member of the public or an NGO seek remedy where public interest is at stake.
- Justice **P.N. Bhagwati** and Justice **V.R. Krishna Iyer** were among the first judges to admit PILs; they are known for initiating PIL.
- Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer (15 November 1915 - 4 December 2014) was a Supreme Court judge, never Chief Justice of India; he is a progenitor of PIL. {113}
- PIL can be filed in both the **High Courts and the Supreme Court**. {115}
- First PIL case: Hussainara Khatoon vs State of Bihar (1979), on a petition by Kapila Hingorani, accepted by Justice P.N. Bhagwati; the CJI then was Y.V. Chandrachud.
! For "who was CJI when PIL was introduced" none of the given options (Hidayatullah, Ahmadi, Bhagwati, Anand) is appropriate.
- PIL widened locus standi, giving weaker sections effective access to justice; but PIL is **not essential** for the rule of law, it only strengthens it. {116}
- Rule of law rests on basic structure, equality (Article 14), Article 32, Article 226 writs and judicial review, which existed before PIL.
- PIL lets public-minded citizens reach court to seek justice for a person, group or society unable to reach it. {117}
- The term and concept of PIL originated in the **United States**. {118,119}
- In the USA public interest law grew from 19th-century movements as part of the legal aid movement; the first legal aid office opened in New York in 1876.
- **Judicial activism** in India is related to Public Interest Litigation; it began in the mid-1970s on the Supreme Court's initiative. {120}
- **Justice A.S. Anand** said judicial activism should not become judicial adventurism. {121}
- Judicial activism arose mainly from failure of the executive and legislature to act; judicial adventurism is its extreme form.
