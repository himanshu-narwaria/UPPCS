@ 29 | The High Court
## High Court: place and Articles
- The High Court occupies the top position in the judicial administration of the State.
- The Constitution provides a High Court for each State.
- The **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956** authorised Parliament to set up a common High Court for two or more States, or for two or more States and a Union Territory.
- **Articles 214 to 231** in Part VI deal with the organisation, independence, jurisdiction, powers and procedures of the High Courts.
- High Courts are instituted as constitutional courts under Part VI, Chapter V, Article 214.
- The institution of the High Court originated in India in **1862**; High Courts were set up at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras.
- In **1866** the High Court of Judicature for the North-Western Provinces came into existence at Agra; its seat was shifted from Agra to **Allahabad in 1869**.

- **Court of Record**: the judgements, proceedings and acts of the High Court are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony.
- These records have evidentiary value and cannot be questioned when produced before any subordinate court; they are recognised as legal precedents and legal references.
- A Court of Record also has the power to punish for contempt of itself.
## Judges: number and appointment
- Article 216: the **President** decides the number of judges of a High Court; the judges must not exceed the maximum number the President fixes by order for that Court. {1}
- Article 217(1): every Judge of a High Court is appointed by the **President** by warrant under his hand and seal; the Governor does not appoint them. {2}
- The President appoints them after consulting the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State and (for judges other than the Chief Justice) the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned.
- Qualifications of a High Court Judge: citizen of India; and held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least **ten years**, or been an advocate of a High Court (or High Courts in succession) for at least ten years.
- Article 223: the President can appoint a High Court Judge as **acting Chief Justice** when the office of Chief Justice is vacant, or the Chief Justice is temporarily absent or unable to perform his duties.
- **Article 224**: additional and acting Judges are appointed in the High Court. {4}
- Article 224(1): on a temporary increase in business or arrears of work, the President may appoint duly qualified persons as **Additional Judges** for a period not exceeding two years.
- Article 224(2): when a Judge other than the Chief Justice is absent, unable to perform duties or acting as Chief Justice, a duly qualified person may act as a Judge until the permanent Judge resumes duties.
- Article 224(3): no additional or acting Judge holds office after attaining 62 years of age.
- Justice **H.R. Khanna**, Chairman of the 8th Law Commission, recommended in its 80th Report ('The Method of Appointment of Judges') that at least one-third of the judges of a High Court should be from other States. {34}
## Judges: transfer, tenure, salary and removal
- Article 222: the President can transfer a judge from one High Court to another after consulting the Chief Justice of India.
- **Third Judges case (1998)**: for a transfer, the Chief Justice of India must consult the Collegium of the four seniormost Supreme Court judges and the Chief Justices of the two High Courts concerned; his sole opinion is not consultation.
- A High Court Judge holds office until the age of **62 years**; fixed under Article 217(1) by the **15th Constitutional Amendment, 1963**; before that it was 60 years. {7,8}
- The 114th Amendment Bill (2010) aimed to raise the High Court retirement age from 62 to 65 years; it could not be passed.
- Article 217(1)(a): a High Court Judge resigns by writing addressed to the **President**. {29}
- Article 217(1)(b): a High Court Judge is removed by the President in the manner provided in Article 124(4) for a Supreme Court Judge; the mode of removal is the same. {28}
- Grounds of removal: proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
- The President can issue the removal order only after an address by Parliament is presented to him in the same session, passed by a special majority.
- Special majority = majority of the total membership of each House and not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting in each House.
- Article 220: a former permanent High Court Judge cannot plead or act in any court or before any authority in India, except the Supreme Court and the other High Courts.
- Article 202(3)(d): salaries and allowances of High Court Judges are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State**. {5}
- Article 112(3)(d): the pension of High Court Judges is charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**. {6}
- Justice **Soumitra Sen**, former Judge of the Calcutta High Court, was charged with misappropriation of funds; Rajya Sabha passed an impeachment motion against him in 2011. {30}
- The motion was to come before Lok Sabha on 5 September 2011, but he resigned on 1 September 2011.
- Shri **Rajeev Gupta** became Chief Justice of the Chhattisgarh High Court on 2 February 2008; before that he was Chief Justice of the Uttarakhand High Court. {33}
- The current Chief Justice of the Chhattisgarh High Court is Justice Ramesh Sinha.
## Number of High Courts and their territory
- At present there are **25 High Courts**; **7** of them have jurisdiction over more than one State / Union Territory.
- Punjab, Haryana and the Union Territory of Chandigarh have a common High Court; the NCT of Delhi has its own High Court. {9}
- Delhi, Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh are the Union Territories with a High Court: Delhi has its own; Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh share a common High Court.
- The NCT of Delhi is the only Union Territory with a separate High Court.
- The other Union Territories fall under the jurisdiction of different State High Courts.
- Parliament can extend the jurisdiction of a High Court.
- In 2008 there were 21 High Courts; at present there are 25.
- Excluding Union Territories, **three** High Courts have jurisdiction over more than one State: Gauhati, Bombay, Punjab and Haryana. {14}
- **Bombay High Court** has jurisdiction over more than one State / Union Territory. {13}
- The High Courts of **Meghalaya, Manipur and Tripura** were established by the Government in January 2013 and started working in March 2013. {16}
- Before March 2013 Manipur had no High Court of its own; it was under the Gauhati High Court.

## Jurisdiction and powers
- Main jurisdictions of a High Court: original, appellate and supervisory.
- **Advisory jurisdiction** (Article 143) belongs to the Supreme Court, not to High Courts. {18}
- The second appeal to the High Court is within its appellate jurisdiction.
- High Courts may accept a petition of Public Interest Litigation.
- Article 227: every High Court has superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory, except those relating to the Armed Forces.
- Article 235: the High Court has control over all other subordinate courts in the State.
- The Chief Justice of a High Court acts in a dual capacity: judicial and administrative.
- In his administrative capacity the Chief Justice of a High Court is subject to the **writ jurisdiction of any other Judge** of the High Court. {19}
- The Chief Justice controls, observes, superintends and administers the lower courts.
- Wilful disobedience or non-compliance of court orders and use of derogatory language about judicial behaviour amount to **Contempt of Court**. {24}
- Judicial activism cannot be practised without punitive powers to punish contempt; this is true but does not explain the contempt rule.
- The Babri Mosque / Ramjanmabhoomi dispute came before the Allahabad High Court as a **Title Suit**: two communities claimed the same land and property. {31}
- **Allahabad High Court**, in Rahmatullah vs State of UP (1994), held that divorce by saying 'Talaq' three times is illegal; it violates Articles 14 and 15. {32}
- The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019: any pronouncement of talaq by a Muslim husband upon his wife, spoken, written, electronic or in any other manner, is void and illegal.
## Writs
- Under Article 32 (Supreme Court) and Article 226 (High Court), five writs are issued: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto.
- The Supreme Court's writ power is limited to Part III (Fundamental Rights); a High Court can issue writs for Part III rights and for **any other purpose** (legal rights). {26}
- So High Courts are in a better position than the Supreme Court to protect the rights of citizens. {27}
- **Certiorari**: issued by a higher court to a lower court to send up the record of a case, with all files, evidence and documents, for review; usually to overrule the lower court's judgment. {20}
- Certiorari examines the functioning of a subordinate court. {22}
- Certiorari is one of the mechanisms that protect the fundamental rights of citizens.
- **Prohibition** means "to prohibit, prevent, forbid or restrain": a superior court orders a lower court or tribunal to stop proceedings beyond its jurisdiction or contrary to natural justice. {21}
- Prohibition is issued when the procedure is pending in court; when a lower court hears a case outside its jurisdiction, the upper court takes over the case. {23}
- Prohibition applies only to judicial and quasi-judicial bodies; it cannot be enforced against administrative agencies, statutory authorities, or private persons or enterprises.
- **Mandamus** will not lie against a private organisation unless it is entrusted with a public duty; it will lie against a government company. {25}
- Any public-minded person can be a petitioner for the writ of **Quo Warranto**.
## District Judge and subordinate courts
- Article 233(1): appointment, posting and promotion of **District Judges** are made by the Governor in consultation with the High Court having jurisdiction in that State.
- The District Judge is the highest judicial authority in the district; he has judicial and administrative powers and supervisory powers over all subordinate courts in the district. {37}
- Article 236(a): 'district judge' includes judge of a city civil court, additional district judge, joint district judge, assistant district judge, chief judge of a small cause court, chief presidency magistrate, additional chief presidency magistrate, sessions judge, additional sessions judge and assistant sessions judge.
- 'District judge' does not include a **Tribunal judge**. {36}
## Advocates, prisons and custody
- The **Advocates Act, 1961** aims to create a single class of legal practitioners called 'advocates'.
- Government law officers, legal firms, corporate lawyers and patent attorneys are all recognised as advocates.
- The Act provides for autonomous Bar Councils, one for all India and one for each State; the Bar Council of India lays down rules on legal education and recognition of law colleges. {38}
- 'Prisons / persons detained therein' is a State subject under **entry 4 of the State List**.
- Prisons are governed by the **Prisons Act, 1894**, which gave Provincial Governments (now States) the power to make rules on prisons; so prison management vests solely with State Governments. {39}
- The Ministry of Home Affairs has finalised a comprehensive 'Model Prisons Act, 2023' as a guiding document for States to adopt.
- **Police custody**: police have physical custody of the accused, who is lodged in a police station lock-up.
- **Judicial custody**: the accused is in the custody of the concerned Magistrate and is lodged in jail.
- When police take a person into custody, the Cr.P.C. applies and they must produce him before a Magistrate within **24 hours** of arrest.
- During judicial custody the police officer in charge cannot interrogate the suspect, unless the court allows it as necessary. {40}
- Cr.P.C. Section 167, Gian Singh vs State (Delhi Administration) 1981: the Magistrate can direct the place and manner of interrogation; interrogation by permission does not change the nature of custody.
- A person can generally be detained under **Preventive Detention** for a maximum of 3 months; under certain conditions it may exceed 3 months.
## Lok Adalat, Gram Nyayalaya and mobile courts
- **Lok Adalat** (People's Court) is an alternative dispute resolution mechanism; it settles cases pending in court or at the pre-litigation stage.
- A Lok Adalat can take up civil cases (including marriage and family) and compoundable criminal cases.
- A Lok Adalat is presided over by a sitting or retired judicial officer as chairman, with two other members, usually a lawyer and a social worker; the composition is not fixed. {41}
- The consent of both parties is the most important factor; a matter cannot be forced into a Lok Adalat.
- Once the parties agree to go to the Lok Adalat, neither party can walk away from its decision.
- Money claims are easier to settle in Lok Adalats, as mostly only the quantum is in dispute.
- The **Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008** sets up Gram Nyayalayas at the grass-root level to give citizens access to justice at their doorstep.
- Section 11: a Gram Nyayalaya has both civil and criminal jurisdiction, only for disputes and cases specified in the Act's Schedule.
- Section 27: the district court, in consultation with the District Magistrate, prepares a panel of village-level social workers (as conciliators). {42}
- Mobile courts were first imagined by former President Dr. **A.P.J. Abdul Kalam**. {43}
- The first mobile court of India was set up in August 2007 in Mewat district, Haryana; it was inaugurated by the then Chief Justice K.G. Balakrishnan.
- Mobile courts aim to take civil and criminal justice closer to people in remote areas, saving them the cost of travelling to distant courts.
