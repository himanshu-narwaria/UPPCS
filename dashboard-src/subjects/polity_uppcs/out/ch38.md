@ 38 | Constitutional Amendment
## Article 368 and the power to amend
- Any constitution, however wisely made, needs amendment with time and circumstances.
- Indian Constitution is a mixture of rigidity and flexibility; it is flexible and adaptable.
- **Article 368** in Part XX gives Parliament's power to amend the Constitution and the procedure for it. {4,5}
- Article 368(1): Parliament, in its constituent power, may amend any provision by **addition, variation or repeal**; it is the supreme legislative body elected by the people. {1,2}
- But Parliament cannot amend provisions forming the 'basic structure' (Kesavananda Bharati case, 1973).
- India's process of constitutional amendment is taken from the Constitution of South Africa.
## Three ways of amendment
- The Constitution can be amended in three ways: simple majority; special majority; special majority plus ratification by at least half of the State legislatures.
- Amendments by simple majority are not treated as amendments under Article 368.
- Simple majority covers: admission or creation of new States; change of names, areas, boundaries of States; creating or abolishing Legislative Councils; salaries of President, Governors, Supreme Court and High Court judges.
- Article 368 itself mentions two methods: special majority, and special majority with ratification by half of the States.
- Special majority (Article 368(2)) = majority of total membership of each House and at least **two-thirds of members present and voting**.
- Most Articles of the Constitution can be amended by special majority of Parliament.
## Procedure for an amendment Bill
- A Constitution Amendment Bill can be introduced in **either House of Parliament** (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha), not by State Legislatures or the President. {6,7,8}
- A Constitution Amendment Bill must be passed by each House separately by special majority. {3}
- There is no provision for a joint sitting on a Constitution Amendment Bill; if the Rajya Sabha rejects it, the Bill is dropped. {17}
- No prior recommendation of the President is needed; the President must give assent and cannot veto the Bill. {18,19}
- After the President's assent the Constitution stands amended as per the Bill.
- **24th Amendment** took away the President's veto over amendment Bills by using the words 'shall give his assent'. {20}
- Article 368 was amended by the 24th Amendment Act, 1971 and by the 101st Amendment Act, 2016 (to bring in GST). {21}
- 24th Amendment Act, 1971 (passed 5 November 1971): affirmed Parliament's power to amend any part including fundamental rights; made President's assent compulsory.
- Article 74: the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers.
## Ratification by States
- Proviso to Article 368(2): some amendments also need ratification by legislatures of not less than **one-half of the States**, before the Bill goes to the President.
- Matters needing ratification: election of President, executive power of Union and States, Supreme Court and High Courts, federal provisions, Seventh Schedule Lists, representation of States in Parliament, Article 368 itself. {10,11}
- Articles named in the proviso: 54 (election of President), 55 (manner of election), 73 (executive power of Union), 162 (executive power of State), 241 (High Courts for UTs), 279A (GST Council). {12,13}
- Chapters named: Chapter IV of Part V (Union Judiciary), Chapter V of Part VI (High Courts), Chapter I of Part XI (legislative relations between Union and States).
- Article 279A was added to the proviso after the 101st Amendment in 2016.
- Ratification is by half of the States, not all States, even for changes in federal character. {14}
- Formation of new States, change of borders or names, and abolition of a Legislative Council do not need State ratification.
- Article 158 (conditions of Governor's office) is not in the proviso; no State ratification is needed.
- Qualification, conditions of office and tenure of the President are not covered by the ratification requirement. {15}
- A State Assembly can pass or reject the ratification resolution, but cannot propose any amendment to it. {9}
- **Third Amendment, 1954** was the first amendment ratified by State Legislatures under Article 368(2). {16}
- Third Amendment re-enacted entry 33 of the Concurrent List in the Seventh Schedule.
## First Amendment, 1951
- The first Bill to amend the Constitution was presented in **1951**. {22}
- First Amendment was passed in 1951 by the Provisional Parliament, whose members had just drafted the Constitution in the Constituent Assembly.
- First Amendment amended Articles 15, 19, 85, 87, 174, 176, 341, 342, 372 and 376.
- First Amendment added Articles 31A and 31B and the **Ninth Schedule**. {24,25}
- It was for the protection of agrarian (land) reforms in certain States. {23}
- Object of the Ninth Schedule: protect certain Acts and regulations from being declared void for violating fundamental rights (Part III).
- First Amendment was widely believed to be enacted to overcome judicial interpretations of the Fundamental Rights. {26}
- Immediate cause: Supreme Court and High Court rulings holding parts of criminal, press and public safety laws incompatible with free expression.
- It added clause (4) to Article 15: special provisions for backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- It added restrictions in Article 19: 'public order', 'friendly relations with foreign states', 'incitement to an offence'.
- Constitution as adopted on 26 November 1949 had a Preamble, 395 Articles and 8 Schedules.
- Sixth Schedule: administration of tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram.
- Seventh Schedule: three lists (Union, State, Concurrent) fixing legislative authority.

## 42nd and 44th Amendments
- 42nd Amendment, 1976 amended the Preamble, 40 Articles and the Seventh Schedule, and added 14 new Articles and two new Parts.
- Its main objective: overcome difficulties from the Supreme Court's Kesavananda Bharati decision of 1973.
- It added **10 Fundamental Duties** (not eight) in Part IV-A, Article 51A, on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation. {35,36}
- Fundamental duties were 10 earlier and are 11 at present; derived from the Constitution of the Soviet Union.
- It is not correct that the 42nd Amendment added Articles 39A, 43A and 47 as new Directive Principles.
- 42nd Amendment gave the President power, in consultation with the Election Commission, to disqualify members of State Legislatures.
- 42nd Amendment amended Article 74 to state that the President shall act in accordance with the advice of the Council of Ministers.
- 42nd Amendment gave Directive Principles preference over the fundamental rights in Articles 14, 19 and 31.
- **44th Amendment Act, 1978** was brought by the Janata Party Government (1977-79) to undo several changes of the 42nd Amendment.
- Under the 44th Amendment the President can return the Council of Ministers' advice for reconsideration only **once**, then must act on it. {37,38}
- 39th Amendment (1975) removed the Supreme Court's authority over election petitions of President, Vice-President, Prime Minister and Lok Sabha Speaker.
- 39th Amendment was nullified by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978; the 44th did not place the PM's election beyond judicial review.
- Article 31 was repealed by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978.
## Anti-defection, education and Council of Ministers
- Anti-defection law (ban on floor crossing) came by the 52nd Amendment Act, 1985 adding the Tenth Schedule; amended by the 91st Amendment Act, 2003.
- Tenth Schedule: disqualification of MPs and State legislators on the ground of defection.
- 58th Amendment answered the general demand for an authoritative Hindi text of the Constitution including all later amendments.
! The book prints the 61st Amendment Act as 1988 in one place and as 1989 in another.
- 61st Amendment gave unrepresented youth a chance to express their feelings and join the political process.
- 104th Amendment Act, 2019: nomination of Anglo-Indians in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies not extended beyond 25 January 2020.
- 93rd Constitution Amendment Bill = free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14; enacted as the 86th Amendment Act, 2002. {53}
- It added Article 21A (Fundamental Rights), amended Article 45 (Directive Principles) and added Article 51A(k) (Fundamental Duties).
- Article 21A: State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of 6 to 14 years as the State may by law determine.
- Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 implements this right.
- Both the 86th Amendment and the RTE Act came into force on **1 April 2010**. {54}
- Right to Education aims at 'Education for All'; earlier free and compulsory education was in Part IV, Article 45 of the original Constitution. {59}
- Right to Education became a fundamental right by the 86th Amendment, not the 91st.
- Till 2003 the Constitution did not fix the number of ministers; it depended on the Prime Minister or Chief Minister.
- 91st Amendment Act, 2003 amended Articles 75 and 164 and the Tenth Schedule, and inserted new Article 361B.
- Article 75(1A): ministers including the Prime Minister not to exceed 15% of Lok Sabha strength; in force from 1 January 2004.
- Article 164(1A): same 15% limit for a State's Council of Ministers including the Chief Minister.
- Objective of the 91st Amendment: strengthen the anti-defection law.
- A member disqualified under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule is also disqualified to be appointed a Minister.
## Co-operatives, land boundary, GST, later amendments
- 97th Amendment was notified on 12 January 2012 to give constitutional status to co-operatives.
- It added 'Co-operative Societies' in Article 19(1)(c) (Part III), Article 43B (Part IV) and Part IX-B (Articles 243ZH to 243ZT).
- These provisions cover voluntary formation, professional management, functioning and operation of co-operative societies.
- Part IX-B is now operative only for multi-State Co-operative Societies.
- Article 243ZJ: number of directors of a co-operative society is fixed by the State Legislature, maximum **21**. {70}
- 99th Amendment Act, 2014 formed the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC).
- Supreme Court struck down the 99th Amendment and NJAC as violating independence of judiciary and separation of powers. {39,40}
- A five-judge bench headed by Justice J.S. Khehar declared NJAC and the 99th Amendment void (dates printed: 15 October 2015 and 16 October 2015).
- 100th Amendment Act, 2015 ratified the 1974 Land Boundary Agreement between India and Bangladesh.
- India transferred **111 enclaves** to Bangladesh; Bangladesh transferred **51 enclaves** to India (not the reverse). {71}
- Under it 6.1 km of undemarcated boundary was demarcated and territories of four States changed.
- 101st Amendment Act, 2016 introduced a national GST on 1 July 2017.
- It added Article 246A (legislative power for GST), Article 269A (inter-State GST) and Article 279A (GST Council).
- 103rd Amendment, 2019: EWS = citizens other than classes in Article 15(4), 15(5) and Article 16(4); 10% reservation in government jobs and education.
- President Ram Nath Kovind gave assent to the 105th Amendment Act, 2021 on 18 August 2021. {74}
- 105th Amendment amends clauses 1 and 2 of Article 342A, adds clause 3, and amends Articles 366(26C) and 338B(9).
- Under it every State or UT may by law keep its own list of socially and educationally backward classes, which may differ from the Central List.
## 106th Amendment (women's reservation)
- Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 = 'Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam'; the latest amendment.
- It reserves one-third of directly elected seats for women in Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and Delhi Assembly, including seats reserved for SCs and STs.
- One-third of the seats reserved for SCs and STs are reserved for SC and ST women. {87}
- It takes effect after delimitation following the first census after the Act's commencement; not from the 18th Lok Sabha.
- It stays in force for 15 years from commencement; reservation may continue till a date Parliament fixes by law.
## Basic structure and related cases
- **Golaknath vs State of Punjab (1967)**: Supreme Court limited Parliament's power to amend the Constitution for the first time. {76}
- Order: Golaknath case (27 February 1967) → 24th Amendment (5 November 1971) → Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) → 42nd Amendment (1976). {75}
- **Kesavananda Bharati vs State of Kerala (1973)**: Supreme Court gave the doctrine of 'basic structure' of the Constitution. {77,78,79,80,81}
- In Kesavananda Bharati the Supreme Court reversed its Golaknath decision and upheld the 24th Amendment.
- **Parliament** can amend the Constitution including Fundamental Rights, but not the basic structure, under Article 368. {82}
- A Fundamental Right that is part of the basic structure cannot be restricted or removed by Parliament.
- Basic structure theory: certain features of the Constitution are so essential that they cannot be abrogated. {83}
- Basic structure = inviolable features that Parliament cannot amend or alter.
- Minerva Mills case, 1980: giving all Directive Principles preference (42nd Amendment) is unconstitutional; preference only to Article 39(b) and 39(c) over Articles 14 and 19 is valid. {85}
- That preference came from the 42nd Amendment, 1976, not the 44th Amendment, 1978.
