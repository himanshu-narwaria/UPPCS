@ 13 | Fundamental Rights
## History and nature of Fundamental Rights
- First explicit demand for Fundamental Rights (FR) = "Constitution of India Bill, 1895", inspired by Bal Gangadhar Tilak.
- **Motilal Nehru Report (1928)** demanded inalienable Fundamental Rights and favoured including them in the Constitution of India. {3}
- Congress adopted the resolution on Fundamental Rights at the **Karachi Session, 1931**, presided over by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel. {2}
- Karachi session was held on 26 to 31 March, 1931, after the Gandhi-Irwin Pact and the execution of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru.
- At Karachi the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (Delhi Pact) was endorsed, the goal of Purna Swaraj was reiterated, and resolutions on Fundamental Rights and the National Economic Programme were adopted.
- Fundamental Rights are in **Part III**, **Articles 12 to 35**; Part III lists 6 Fundamental Rights. {10}
- Part III is regarded as the Magna Carta of India.
- Fundamental Rights are compared with, and are like, the Bill of Rights in the U.S. Constitution; in philosophical and conceptual sense they are similar to it.
- Fundamental Rights are defended and guaranteed by the Constitution; they are a guarantee against arbitrary State action and are listed in Part III. {9}
- Socio-economic justice is found mostly in the Directive Principles (Part IV) and to a little extent in the chapter on Fundamental Rights and certain other provisions.
- Fundamental Rights are **justiciable**: enforceable by the Courts, subject to certain restrictions. {6}
- Fundamental Rights are natural and non-transferable rights.
- They are called Fundamental Rights because they are written in the Constitution, are essential for personality development, and Parliament cannot make laws against them (they are part of the basic structure). {7}
- Fundamental Rights can be suspended only during an emergency, under **Articles 358 and 359**: enforcement is suspended by an order of the President during a national emergency; rights under Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended. {8}
- **Article 358**: suspension of provisions of Article 19 during emergencies.
- **Article 359**: suspension of enforcement of Fundamental Rights except Articles 20 and 21 during emergencies (after the 44th Amendment Act).
- If National Emergency is declared on grounds of war or external aggression, rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended.
- After the 44th Amendment (1978), the right to life and personal liberty (Article 21) cannot be suspended even during a National Emergency. {66}
- **Jawaharlal Nehru**: "A fundamental right should be looked upon, not from the point of view of any particular difficulty of the moment, but as something that you want to make permanent in the Constitution." {4}
- **Dr. S. Radhakrishnan** ('philosopher king'): Fundamental Rights are "a pledge to our people and a pact with the civilized world". {5}
- Patanjali Sastry (former Chief Justice of the Supreme Court): the whole object of Part III is to protect the freedoms and rights in it against arbitrary invasion by the state.
- In a liberal democracy, **separation of powers** is the best safeguard of liberty; it works as a vehicle for restraint of powers. {1}
- Liberal approach: all power is rooted in the will of the people; rule of law is a basic foundation of democracy; individual rights and freedoms are a prerequisite of dignity.
- A committed judiciary will never go against the government's policies.
- Centralization of power concentrates decisions in a few leaders at the top and may lead to authoritarian tendencies.
- An elected government is expected to ensure liberty but is not its best safeguard.
- **Article 13**: pre-constitutional and post-constitutional laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights (Part III) are void to the extent of the inconsistency; it secures paramountcy of the Constitution regarding Fundamental Rights. {11,12}
- Article 13 gives the power of Judicial Review to the Supreme Court and High Courts when Fundamental Rights are violated.
- Article 245 = extent of laws made by Parliament and State Legislatures.
- Article 326 = elections to Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of adult suffrage.
## List of Fundamental Rights
- Originally the Constitution gave seven Fundamental Rights: Equality, Freedom, against Exploitation, Freedom of Religion, Cultural and Educational, Property, Constitutional Remedies.
| Fundamental Right | Articles |
| Right to Equality | 14–18 {13,14} |
| Right to Freedom | 19–22 {42} |
| Right against Exploitation | 23–24 {72} |
| Right to Freedom of Religion | 25–28 {120} |
| Cultural and Educational Rights | 29–30 {121} |
| Right to Constitutional Remedies | 32 |
- Articles 14, 15 and 16 give the Right to Equality; Article 13 does not.
- 'Right to protest' is not among the six Fundamental Rights. {148}
- The Constitution gives Right to Equality (Art. 14-18), Right to Freedom of Religion (Art. 25-28) and Right to Liberty/Freedom (Art. 19-22), but no 'Right of Equal Shelter'. {149}
## Right to Equality: Articles 14 to 18
- **Article 14**: the State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India. {16,17}
- Article 14 puts a limitation on the legislative power. {15}
- Article 14 rights belong to every person, citizen or foreigner.
- Concept of 'Equal Protection of the law' is taken from the American Constitution.
- Equal protection means: equal treatment in equal circumstances, in privileges conferred and liabilities imposed; same laws applied alike to all similarly situated; likes treated alike without discrimination.
- A law giving vague, wide (unguided, uncontrolled) discretionary power to an administrative authority may be declared ultra vires Article 14, Article 19 and other provisions. {18}
- Courts control delegation of discretionary powers by testing the constitutionality of the delegating law against Part III rights.
- Article 14 guarantees equality but courts permit reasonable classification; even under a valid law, a discriminatory action still violates the equality clause.
- The State can treat unequals differently to create a level playing field in social, economic and political spheres; among equals the law should be equal and equally administered. {27}
- Equal protection under Article 14 does not mean all laws must be general in character.
- **Rule of Law**: creation and enforcement of laws are legally regulated, so no one, even the highest official, is above the law; one act and one judiciary for all. {19}
- Under Rule of Law the government is subject to existing laws as much as citizens are.
- Equality before law: no legal person enjoys privileges not extended to all, and no person is immune from legal sanctions.
- **Article 15**: prohibition of discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth; it falls under the Right to Equality. {20}
- **Article 15(4)** was added by the First Amendment (1951) after State of Madras Vs. Champakam Dorairajan; it amended the right against discrimination. {21}
- Article 15(4): nothing in Article 15 or Article 29(2) prevents the State from making special provision for socially and educationally backward classes or for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- **Article 15(5)**: State may make special provisions for reservation in admission to educational institutions, including private ones, aided or unaided, other than minority institutions under Article 30(1). {22}
- 93rd Amendment Act, 2005 enabled 27% reservation for Other Backward Classes in Government and private educational institutions.
- OBC reservation rests on **Article 15(4)** and **Article 16(4)**. {23}
- **Article 16**: equality of opportunity in matters of public employment.
- **Article 16(1) and 16(2)** ensure equal opportunity for all citizens in employment or appointment to any office under the State. {25}
- **Article 16(2)**: no citizen shall, on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence or any of them, be ineligible for or discriminated against in any employment or office under the State. {24}
- **Article 16(4)**: State may reserve appointments or posts for any backward class of citizens which, in its opinion, is not adequately represented in State services.
- Supreme Court (Indra Sawhney Vs Union of India 1993; M Nagaraj Vs Union of India 2006): reservation under Article 16(4) must be read with Article 335, which provides for "maintenance of efficiency of administration". {28}
- **Article 335**: claims of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes to be considered, consistently with efficiency of administration, in appointments under the Union or a State; it does not define 'efficiency of administration'.
- **77th Amendment Act, 1995** inserted clause (4A) in Article 16: reservation in promotion for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. {29}
- Right to equality includes equality before law (Art. 14), social equality (Art. 15) and equal opportunity (Art. 16); it does not include economic equality. {26}
- 'Equal pay for equal work' was deduced as a Fundamental Right from Articles 14 and 16 read with the word 'Socialist' in the Preamble and Article 39(d) (Randhir Singh Vs. Union of India). {30,31}
- Randhir Singh: the principle applies to unequal pay scales based on no classification or irrational classification, for identical work under the same employer.
- **Article 17**: untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden; enforcing any disability arising from it is an offence punishable by law. {32,33,34,35,36}
- Article 17 is classified under the Right to Equality. {38}
- Article 17 is a "statement of principle" to remove humiliation and harassment of Dalits and ensure their fundamental, socio-economic, political and cultural rights. {37}
- Untouchability can be abolished by making laws, improving education and public awareness; giving jobs/services is not a proper solution. {39}
- Parliament declared the practice of untouchability a punishable offence by the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955.
- Structural violence = preventable harm where no actor commits the violence; it arises from unequal distribution of power and resources, built into the structure; forms include racism and untouchability.
- Untouchability is an extremely harsh social punishment on the lowest castes on the purity-impurity scale and has religious approval, so it is the worst form of structural violence. {40}
- **Article 18**: abolition of titles, except military and academic. {124}
## Right to Freedom: Article 19
- Article 19(1) guarantees all citizens six rights.
| Clause | Freedom |
| 19(1)(a) | Speech and expression {45} |
| 19(1)(b) | Assemble peaceably and without arms |
| 19(1)(c) | Form associations or unions or co-operative societies |
| 19(1)(d) | Move freely throughout the territory of India |
| 19(1)(e) | Reside and settle in any part of the territory of India |
| 19(1)(g) | Practise any profession, or carry on any occupation, trade or business |
- Freedom to own, acquire and dispose of property was Article 19(1)(f) in the original Constitution; the 44th Amendment, 1978 removed it. {43}
- Equality of opportunity in public employment (Article 16) is under the Right to Equality, not under Article 19(1). {44}
- Freedom of the Press is not given directly; it is included in freedom of speech and expression under **Article 19(1)(a)** (Sakal Papers Ltd. Vs. Union of India, 1961). {46,47,48,49}
- Supreme Court: hoisting the National Flag atop private buildings is a fundamental right of every citizen under Article 19(1)(a). {50}
- Supreme Court: Right to Information lies in freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), though it is not mentioned in the Constitution. {150}
- Bijoe Emmanuel vs. State of Kerala: Supreme Court overruled the High Court; expelling children for their conscientiously held religious faith violated Articles 19(1)(a) and 25(1); no provision of law obliges anyone to sing the National Anthem. {151}
- **Article 19(2)** grounds to restrict speech and expression: sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence. {52,53}
- 'Unbecoming criticism' and 'sedition' are not grounds listed in Article 19(2).
- Under Article 19(3) the State can impose reasonable restrictions on the freedom to assemble (Article 19(1)(b)) in the interest of public order; gherao of officials is not part of this freedom. {51}
- Articles 19(2) to 19(6) allow reasonable restrictions by law only for specified objectives: sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, public order, decency or morality, protection of interests of Scheduled Tribes, professional/technical regulations.
- Reasonableness of restrictions must safeguard the interest of the general public and take account of prevailing social values and social needs. {54}
## Articles 20 and 22: protection of accused and arrested persons
- **Article 20**: protection in respect of conviction for offences; it covers ex-post facto law, double jeopardy and self-incrimination. {79}
- Article 20(1): no conviction except for violation of a law in force at the time of the act, and no penalty greater than that under the law then in force.
- **Article 20(2)**: no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once = Doctrine of Double Jeopardy. {81}
- Double jeopardy aims to avoid harassment by successive criminal proceedings for one crime; maxim: Nemo Debet Bis Vexari (no man shall be put twice in peril for the same offence).
- **Article 20(3)**: no person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself (self-incrimination). {80}
- Article 20(3) rests on the presumption that the accused is innocent till guilt is proved; the prosecutor must establish guilt.
- Prohibition against torture is not part of Article 20; protection from torture is a fundamental right under Article 21. {75}
- **Article 22**: protection against arrest and detention in certain cases; safeguards the rights of an arrested person. {82,123}
- Article 22(1): no arrested person shall be detained without being informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds of arrest, nor denied the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of his choice.
- **Article 22(2)**: every arrested person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours of arrest, excluding journey time to the magistrate's court. {83}
- **Article 22(4)**: no preventive detention law shall authorise detention for longer than **three months**. {84,85}
- Article 22(5): the authority must tell a preventive detainee the grounds of the order as soon as possible and give the earliest opportunity to make a representation; so the detainee gets a legal opportunity.
- **Article 355**: duty of the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure State government runs according to the Constitution. {86}
- Prevention of Terrorism Act, 2002 (repealed in 2004): a confession made before a police officer and recorded in writing or on a mechanical or electronic device was admissible in trial under the Act.
- Parole = release of a prisoner, temporarily for a special purpose or completely before the sentence ends, on promise of good behaviour (word of honour in the parole order).
- Parole is not recognized as a right in India; a prisoner's claim is not absolute and prison authorities have considerable discretion; State governments have their own parole rules and guidelines. {87}
- Several international accords and organizations consider parole a right for prisoners.
- Grant of parole is governed by rules made under the Prison Act, 1894 and the Prison Act, 1900.
- Prisons (Bombay Furlough and Parole) Rules, 1959 were made under section 59(5) of the Prisons Act, 1984.
## Article 21 and Article 21A
- **Article 21**: protection of life and personal liberty. {55}
- Article 21 text: no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law; the due process doctrine is read in this Article. {56}
- **Maneka Gandhi Vs. Union of India, 1978**: right to travel abroad is a fundamental right secured by Article 21; Satwant Singh Vs. Union of India also held it a fundamental right. {57}
- Maneka Gandhi judgment: delivered on 25 January 1978, unanimously by a 7-Judge bench; it widened the scope of Article 21.
- Maneka Gandhi: Article 21 cannot allow an arbitrary or irrational process; 'procedure established by law' is by and large the same as procedural due process in the U.S.A.
- Maneka Gandhi: Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive; 'Right to life' includes the right to live with dignity.
- The statement that the petitioner bears the burden of proving the procedure arbitrary is not part of the Maneka Gandhi interpretation. {58}
- Rights brought within Article 21: clean water, clean air, freedom from noise pollution, livelihood, speedy and fair trial, food, clean environment.
- Chameli Singh Vs. State of U.P. 1995: 'Right to Livelihood' under Article 21 includes 'Right to Shelter'; shelter means all infrastructure needed to live, not a mere roof. {59}
- Govind vs. State of M.P. (1975) and Malak Singh vs. State of Punjab and Haryana (1980): right to privacy is implicit in Article 21; it is the right to be let alone.
- Intrusive surveillance that seriously encroaches on privacy can infringe freedom of movement under Article 19(1)(d) and Article 21; privacy is thus available under Article 19(1)(d) read with Article 21. {60}
- **Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) vs. Union of India**: nine-Judge Constitution Bench on 24 August, 2017 unanimously ruled privacy a fundamental right, an intrinsic part of life and personal liberty under Article 21 and of the freedoms of Part III. {61,62,63}
- Lata Singh v/s State of U.P. 2006: right to marry is a component of the right to life under Article 21; Hadiya case: right to convert and choose a partner is implicit in Article 21. {64}
- Bachan Singh Vs. State of Punjab: capital punishment (sentencing discretion in section 302 IPC) does not violate Articles 14, 19 and 21, as it is given only for serious crimes; so it is outside the purview of Article 21. {65}
- Parmananda Katara Vs. Union of India 1989: medical aid to the injured by a doctor falls under Article 21.
- Vishakha Vs. State of Rajasthan 1997: sexual harassment of women at the workplace falls under Article 21.
- Subhash Kumar Vs. State of Bihar 1991: pollution of the quality of air or water falls under Article 21.
- 'Right to Die' is not a Fundamental Right under Article 21. {126}
- **Unnikrishnan vs. State of Andhra Pradesh, 1993**: citizens have a fundamental right to education flowing from Article 21; free education for children up to 14 years. {67}
- Unnikrishnan: the right to education is not absolute; its content must be determined in light of Articles 41 and 45.
- **86th Amendment Act, 2002** added **Article 21A**: free and compulsory education for all children aged 6 to 14 years is a fundamental right, in such manner as the State may by law determine. {68,70}
- Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009 is the law envisaged under Article 21-A; it came into effect on **1 April, 2010**. {69}
- Education is a fundamental human right, indispensable to the right to development as a human right; it promotes individual freedom and empowerment of children. {71}
## Right against Exploitation: Articles 23 and 24
- **Article 23**: prohibits traffic in human beings and begar and other similar forms of forced labour; contravention is an offence punishable by law.
- Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Ordinance, 1975 was promulgated by the President on 25 October, 1975; the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Bill was passed by both Houses and became an Act in **1976**. {162}
- **Article 24**: no child below 14 years shall be employed in any factory or mine or any other hazardous employment; it relates to exploitation of children. {73,76,77}
- The prohibition in Article 24 is an absolute prohibition. {74}
- Article 24 of the ICCPR (International Convention on Civil and Political Rights) protects child rights; Article 24 of the Indian Constitution also does. {78}
- M.C. Mehta Vs. State of Tamil Nadu, 10 December 1996: Supreme Court held employing children below 14 in hazardous work unconstitutional and prohibited it; child labour in hazardous work is barred by the Constitution, this judgment and the UN Charter. {163}
- United Nations Charter: childhood is entitled to special assistance for full and harmonious development; a child should grow up in a healthy environment.
## Freedom of Religion: Articles 25 to 28
- **Article 25**: all persons are equally entitled to freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation of religion; the State may make laws for social reform. {88,90}
- Wearing and carrying of 'Kirpans' by Sikhs is deemed part of freedom of religion by Explanation I of Article 25. {89}
- Right of religious bodies to convert people is not given under Article 25.
- In Article 25, 'Hindus' includes persons professing the Sikh, Jain or Buddhist religion, but not Parsis. {91}
- Article 25 rights are limited on grounds of public order, morality and health; humanism is not a limitation. {92}
- **Article 26**: every religious denomination or section may establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes, manage its own affairs in religion, own and acquire movable and immovable property, and administer it according to law.
- Article 26 (freedom to manage religious affairs) is subject to public order, morality and health, but not to the other Fundamental Rights restrictions. {93}
## Cultural and Educational Rights: Articles 29 and 30
- **Article 29**: protection of interests of minorities; citizens with a distinct language, script or culture have the right to conserve it.
- **Article 29(2)**: no citizen shall be denied admission to any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving State aid on grounds of religion, race, caste, language or any of them. {122}
- **Article 30(1)**: all minorities, whether based on religion or language, have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice; this is not Article 29 or Article 31. {94,95,96,97}
- Article 30(2): in granting aid, the State shall not discriminate against an educational institution because it is managed by a religious or linguistic minority.
- Article 30 does not define 'minority'. {98}
- The Constitution recognizes both religious and linguistic minorities. {99}
- A community given national minority status can establish and administer exclusive educational institutions and benefit from the Prime Minister's 15-Point Programme; the Constitution gives minorities no special representation. {161}
- Article 31 was for compulsory acquisition of property; it was repealed by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978.
- Article 31(1) (former): no person shall be deprived of his property save by the authority of law.
## Constitutional Remedies: Article 32 and writs
- **Article 32** gives constitutional remedies against violation of Fundamental Rights; the Supreme Court issues writs under it only to enforce Fundamental Rights. {106,111}
- Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Article 32 (Right to Constitutional Remedies) "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it", because it gives an effective remedy against violation of Fundamental Rights. {107,108,109}
- Ambedkar: Article 32 is the most important Article, "without which the Constitution would be a nullity".
- Article 32 lets individuals approach the Supreme Court directly and makes protection of Fundamental Rights itself a fundamental right.
- The Preamble is also at times called the Soul of the Constitution, but per Ambedkar it is Article 32.
- Article 32(2): Supreme Court has extensive original jurisdiction to issue directions, orders or writs (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, certiorari) to enforce Fundamental Rights.
- **Article 32(3)**: Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise, within the local limits of its jurisdiction, all or any of the Supreme Court's powers under clause (2). {110}
- High Courts under **Article 226** issue the five writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose (legal rights also).
- Supreme Court (Art. 32) and High Courts (Art. 226) enforce Fundamental Rights and may declare any law void or unconstitutional for contravening them. {100}
- The **Judiciary** (Supreme Court and High Courts) is the guardian of Fundamental Rights, not the President. {101,103,105}
- The most significant Fundamental Right is the right to constitutional remedies under Article 32 (High Courts' jurisdiction is under Article 226).
- The **Supreme Court** is the protector of Fundamental Rights (through Article 32) and Guardian of the Constitution; it interprets and enforces the Constitution. {102,104}
- K.M. Munshi was a member of the Drafting Committee; the Constitution was adopted by the Constituent Assembly; Panchayati Raj was recommended by the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee Report, 1957.
- **Habeas Corpus** ('to have the body of' / 'let us have the body'): detained person is produced before the court and set free if detention is illegal; greatest safeguard of personal liberty and bulwark of individual liberty against arbitrary detention. {112,113,114}
- Habeas Corpus can be issued against both public authorities and private individuals.
- **Mandamus** ('we command'): command by the court to a public official, public body, corporation, inferior court, tribunal or government to perform official duties it has failed or refused to perform. {118,119}
- Mandamus ('we command' in Latin) is one of the prerogative writs in the common law, sometimes called mandate; a superior court uses it to compel a lower court or Government officer to perform mandatory or purely ministerial duties correctly.
- **Prohibition** ('to forbid'; writ to subordinate courts): issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to stop it exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping a jurisdiction it does not have. {115}
- **Certiorari** ('to be certified' / 'be more fully informed'): issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to transfer a pending case to itself or to quash its order. {116}
- Certiorari is issued on grounds of excess of jurisdiction, lack of jurisdiction or error of law.
- Certiorari is known as 'postmortem': the Supreme Court and High Courts re-examine the decision given by subordinate courts. {117}
! 'Postmortem' writ: the Bihar commission keyed Certiorari, but the book says both Certiorari and Quo Warranto have a sense of postmortem, so "more than one of the above" would be the appropriate answer.
- **Quo-Warranto** ('by what authority or warrant'): court enquires into the legality of a person's claim to a public office; prevents illegal assumption of public office.
## Amendability of Fundamental Rights and basic structure
- State of Madras Vs Champakam Dorairajan (1951): in a conflict, Fundamental Rights prevail over the Directive Principles of State Policy. {127}
- In Champakam, Madras ordered community-based reservation of Medical College seats in furtherance of Article 46; it was challenged under Articles 15(1) and 29(2) and cancelled by the Supreme Court.
- Shankari Prasad Vs. Union of India (1951) and Sajjan Singh Vs. State of Rajasthan (1965): Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights. {129}
- Golaknath Vs. State of Punjab (1967): reversed the earlier view; Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights.
- **Kesavananda Bharati Vs. State of Kerala (1973)**: Parliament's amending power is wide but not unlimited; it may amend Fundamental Rights but not destroy the Basic Structure; this case propounded the Basic Structure doctrine. {128,130,131}
- The Basic Structure doctrine was approved in Minerva Mills Vs. Union of India (1980).
- Order of judgments: A.K. Gopalan v/s State of Madras (1950) → Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda Bharati (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980). {132}
- Fundamental Rights can be amended but not removed, because they come under the basic structure.
- Fundamental Duties were added by the 42nd Amendment. {125}
- The Indian Constitution is not a complete federal constitution but a quasi-federal one.
## Right to Property
- The **44th Amendment Act, 1978** deleted the Right to Property from the list of Fundamental Rights: it omitted Article 19(1)(f) and inserted Article 300-A. {136,140,141,142,143,144,145}
- Right to Property is now a **legal (statutory) right** under **Article 300-A** in Part XII, available to any person; it is not a Fundamental Right. {133,134,135,137,138,139,146,147}
- Article 300-A: no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law.
- The 44th Amendment was made during the Janata Party Government, not a Congress Government.
- Article 301 relates to freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, not to the right to property.
## Rights of citizens and of all persons
- Fundamental Rights available only to citizens: Articles **15, 16, 19, 29 and 30**; all others are available to citizens and non-citizens. {153}
- Freedom of speech and expression (Art. 19) and cultural and educational rights of minorities (Art. 29) are available only to Indian citizens, not to aliens. {152,154}
- Rights given to both citizens and non-citizens: Art. 14 (equality before law), Art. 20, Art. 21, Art. 21-A, Art. 23 and 24, Art. 25, 26, 27 and 28.
- Equality before law or equal protection of the laws (Article 14) is available to all persons. {155}
- Right against discrimination (Art. 15, 16, 17), right to move freely throughout India [Art. 19(1)(d)] and right to contest elections are available only to citizens. {156}
- Right to constitutional remedies is available to non-citizens too; freedom of speech, free movement and acquiring property are only for citizens. {157}
- A British citizen (foreigner) in India cannot claim freedom of trade and profession under **Article 19(1)(g)**; equality before law, life and liberty, and freedom of religion are available to him. {158,159,160}
! Question on which Article belongs to a different category (14, 15, 16, 19): two answers are possible, so no single key is given.
- Article 19 is under Right to Freedom while 14, 15, 16 are under Right to Equality; or Article 14 is for all persons while 15, 16, 19 are only for citizens.
## Rights, duties and Right to Service
- Sir Ernest Barker: "Rights without duties are like men without shadow". {164}
- In India, Rights and Duties are correlative; they are two faces of the same thing and give effect to each other. {165}
- Holland: every right implies active or passive forbearance by others; that forbearance is called a duty.
- Supreme Court: a rule may impose an obligation yet not be enforceable in court; it is still a legal rule as it prescribes a norm of conduct.
- Rights are claims of the citizens against the State; they restrict State power and are essential for complete human development. {166}
- Concept of 'Right to Service' originated in **Great Britain (U.K.)**. {168}
- Right to service = laws guaranteeing time-bound delivery of public services by Government to citizens.
- Right of Citizens for Time-bound Delivery of Goods and Services and Redressal of their Grievances Bill, 2011 tried to bring such a law in India but could not be passed.
- Madhya Pradesh was the first state to implement the Right to Services Act, on 25 September, 2010; Bihar was second, on 15 August, 2011.
- Order of enactment of Right to Service Act (by Official Gazette dates): Punjab → Assam → Gujarat → Maharashtra. {167}
- Citizen's Charter aims: quality and time-frame service delivery, citizen-friendly governance, accountable government; creating an effective hearing system of public demands is not its aim. {169}
- Citizen's Charter = document stating commitments of a government or public service organization to give efficient, transparent and accountable services; its basic objective is to empower citizens in public service delivery, such as better quality and standard services with the taxpayer's money.
