@ 31 | Emergency Provisions
## Types of emergency
- Emergency provisions are in **Part XVIII**, Articles **352 to 360**; they enable the Central Government to meet any abnormal situation effectively.
- The Constitution envisages **three types** of emergency. {1}
| Article | Emergency |
| Article 352 | National Emergency: war, external aggression or armed rebellion |
| Article 356 | President's Rule: failure of constitutional machinery in a State {27} |
| Article 360 | Financial Emergency: threat to the financial stability or credit of India |
- The President can declare an emergency for external aggression (Article 352), failure of constitutional machinery in States (Article 356) and financial crisis (Article 360). {8}
- During an emergency the Central Government becomes all-powerful; the Union's executive power extends to directing any State on how its executive power is to be exercised.
- **Article 355**: duty of the Union to protect States against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure every State Government is carried on as per the Constitution. {2,3}
- Article 365 deals with the failure of a State to comply with directions given by the Union.
- **K.M. Nambiar**, opposing the President's Rule provisions, called the emergency power of the President "a fraud on the Constitution". {39}
- The Indian Constitution is regarded as federal, and as a combination of unitary and federal elements; failure of State constitutional machinery is dealt with by Article 356, not Article 352. {25}
## National Emergency: grounds and proclamation
- **Article 352** deals with the Proclamation of (National) Emergency by the President. {4,13}
- Grounds: security of India or any part of it is threatened by **war, external aggression or armed rebellion**. {5,6,7,9,10}
- The proclamation may cover the whole of India or the part of the territory specified in it.
- A proclamation can be made even before the actual occurrence, if the President is satisfied there is imminent danger; an emergency may take place at any time, but that does not explain Article 352. {12}
- The **44th Amendment Act, 1978** replaced 'internal disturbance' with **'armed rebellion'**.
- 'Internal disturbance' was the original third ground; the expression was too vague and had a wider connotation.
- So a National Emergency can no longer be declared on the ground of internal disturbance, as was done in 1975.
- Article 352(3): the President cannot issue or vary a proclamation unless the decision of the **Union Cabinet** is communicated to him in writing. {11}
- National Emergency has been proclaimed 3 times so far: **1962, 1971 and 1975**.
- On **25 June 1975** President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed declared an emergency under Article 352 on the ground of 'internal disturbance'. {24}
- That Emergency lasted 21 months, from 25 June 1975 until it was withdrawn on 21 March 1977; Indira Gandhi was Prime Minister.
## National Emergency: approval and duration
- The proclamation is laid before each House and ceases after **one month** unless approved by resolutions of both Houses of Parliament. {15,16}
- Originally the period was two months; the 44th Amendment Act, 1978 reduced it to one month.
- Resolutions approving the proclamation or its continuance need a **special majority** (Article 352(6)): two-thirds of members present and voting; it can be revoked by a simple majority. {14}
- If Lok Sabha is dissolved when the proclamation is issued, or within the one month without approving it, the proclamation survives until 30 days from the first sitting of the reconstituted Lok Sabha, provided Rajya Sabha has approved it meanwhile.
- It ceases unless the new Lok Sabha also approves it within those 30 days.
- Once approved by both Houses, the emergency continues for **6 months** from the passing of the second approving resolution.
- It may be extended for further six-month periods by resolutions of both Houses passed by the special majority.
## National Emergency: effects
- During a National Emergency, Parliament can make laws on any subject in the State List.
- Such laws become inoperative six months after the emergency has ceased to operate.
- The President can also issue ordinances on State subjects if Parliament is not in session.
- **Article 354(1)**: during a National Emergency the President can modify the distribution of revenues (Articles 268 to 281) between the Centre and the States. {17}
- This modification lasts only up to the end of the financial year in which the proclamation ceases; every such order is laid before each House of Parliament.
- Article 83(2): during a National Emergency, the life of Lok Sabha may be extended beyond 5 years by a law of Parliament, **one year at a time**, any number of times. {22}
- The extension cannot continue beyond six months after the emergency has ceased to operate.
- Proviso to Article 172(1): **Parliament** may by law extend the term of a State Legislative Assembly during an emergency, one year at a time, not beyond six months after the proclamation ceases. {23}
## Fundamental Rights during emergency
- Articles 358 and 359 describe the effect of a National Emergency on Fundamental Rights.
- **Article 358**: Article 19 rights are automatically suspended when emergency is declared on the ground of war or external aggression; no separate order is needed.
- After the 44th Amendment, Article 19 is not automatically suspended in an emergency due to armed rebellion; the President may suspend it under Article 359.
- **Article 359**: the President may by order suspend the right to move any court to enforce the other Fundamental Rights (Part III), except Articles 20 and 21. {18}
- Rights under **Articles 20 and 21** cannot be suspended in any case. {20,21}
- Under Article 359 the President can suspend enforcement of other Fundamental Rights, including Article 21A.

## President's Rule (Article 356)
- Article 356: if the President, on a report from the **Governor** or otherwise, is satisfied that the State Government cannot be carried on as per the Constitution, he may impose President's Rule. {26}
- The President can act even without the Governor's report.
- President's Rule can be proclaimed on two grounds: one in Article 356 itself and the other in Article 365.
- The President may take over all or any of the powers of the State Government.
- The President (actually the Governor on his behalf) dismisses the State Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister.
- The President removes the Council of Ministers and runs the administration with the help of the Chief Secretary of the State.
- The State Legislature is either suspended or dissolved; the President can dissolve, suspend or prorogue the Assembly; dissolution of the Assembly and of local bodies are not necessary consequences. {30}
- The powers of the State Legislature become exercisable by or under the authority of **Parliament**; Parliament passes the State's bills and budget. {29}
- President's Rule has no effect on the Fundamental Rights of citizens.
- Parliament can delegate the power to make laws for the State to the President or any authority specified by him.
- The President can promulgate an ordinance under Article 123, but it is subject to the approval of the House.
- **S.R. Bommai vs Union of India, 1994**: a proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review; failure of constitutional machinery must be real, not fictitious. {31}
- S.R. Bommai: the State Assembly cannot be dissolved with the proclamation until both Houses of Parliament approve it within two months.
- A proclamation of President's Rule must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within **two months** of its issue.
- If approved, President's Rule continues for **6 months**.
- It can be extended, with Parliament's approval every six months, for a maximum of **3 years**. {32}
- Resolutions approving President's Rule or its continuance are passed by either House by a simple majority.
- 44th Amendment Act, 1978 / Article 356(5): extension beyond 1 year needs a National Emergency in operation and an Election Commission certificate that Assembly elections are difficult to hold.
- President's Rule under Article 356 was first imposed in **1951** in the former Punjab and in 1953 in **PEPSU** (Patiala and East Punjab States Union). {34}

- Because of J&K's special status (Article 370), Governor's Rule was also provided under Section 92 of Part 6 of the Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir, in addition to President's Rule.
- The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 removed J&K's separate constitutional system; J&K is now a Union Territory.
- Manipur, Mizoram and Meghalaya have no provision for Governor's Rule.

## Financial Emergency (Article 360)
- **Article 360**: the President may proclaim a Financial Emergency if the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened.
- The proclamation ceases after **two months** unless approved by resolutions of both Houses of Parliament. {35}
- Once approved by both Houses, it continues indefinitely till revoked.
- During a financial emergency the Centre acquires full direction-based control over the States in financial matters.
- The President can direct States to reduce salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of the State.
- The President can direct reduction of salaries and allowances of persons serving the Union, including Judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts.
- Money Bills or other financial Bills passed by a State Legislature may be reserved for the President's consideration if he so directs; it is not necessarily required.
- Financial Emergency has **never** been declared in India; Article 360 has not been used so far. {37,38}
