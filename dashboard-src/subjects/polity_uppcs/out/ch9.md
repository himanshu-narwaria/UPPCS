@ 9 | The Governance System
## Government and its forms
- **Government** = institution or system made of a group of people that takes care of or manages a country or a State.
- Every Government has its own Constitution or set of fundamental principles to ensure effective governance.
- **Autocracy** = supreme power or rule in the hands of one individual or entity.
- Autocracy includes absolute monarchy, where a family or a group of families rule a country; now it also includes dictatorship.
- In an absolute monarchy the post of monarch is inherited.
- Monarchy is one of the oldest forms of Government.
- **Dictatorship** = an individual possesses absolute power without any effective limitations.
- **Democracy** = most widely adopted system of governance at present; final power resides with the people.
- Democracy: a state where people are supreme, power vests in an elected head, government formed by elected representatives; supreme power vested solely in one person like a king is not democracy. {72}
- Democratic governments are classified into two categories: Unitary and Federal.
## State and sovereignty
- **State** (Garner) = community of persons, more or less numerous, permanently occupying a definite territory, independent or nearly so of external control, with an organized government to which the great body of inhabitants render habitual obedience. {1}
- The term 'State' is central to the study of Political Science.
- State has four essential elements: Population, Territory, Government and Sovereignty; **Sovereignty** is the most important element. {2}
- People = the community of persons permanently living there.
- Territory = demarcated boundary that separates one state from another.
- Sovereignty = independent say in all domestic and external decision-making.
- India before 1947 was not a State: it had population, territory and government, but sovereignty was missing because of British control.
- 'Sovereign' means India is neither a dependency nor a dominion of any other nation, but an independent state.
- In 1947 India declared continuation of full membership of the Commonwealth of Nations and accepted the British crown as head of the Commonwealth; this extra-constitutional declaration does not affect India's sovereignty in any manner. {3}
- Idea of the Preamble borrowed from the Constitution of the **USA**; language of the Preamble borrowed from the Constitution of Australia. {4}
## Parliamentary system
- Mainly two types of systems of governance: Parliamentary and Presidential.
- India follows the **Parliamentary** form of government, influenced by the Parliamentary system of the United Kingdom. {5}
- India is a Parliamentary Democracy; it is not Presidential, not a titular monarchy, not an aristocracy. {29}
- Presidential form of Government is not a feature of the Indian Constitution; Parliamentary Government, Independent Judiciary and Federal Government are its features. {11}
- In India the President is only the formal (nominal) head of the executive; the real executive is the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister; India also adopted a secular State and a federal system. {12}
- Modern concept of Parliamentary government emerged in the Kingdom of **Great Britain**; Prof. Pushpesh Pant in his book 'Vishwa ka Itihas' calls the UK its birth-place. {8}
- In a Parliamentary system the Executive is controlled by the Legislature. {6}
- In a Parliamentary system, members of the executive are taken from the legislature and are accountable to it; the Government is responsible to Parliament and can be removed by it. {7}
- In a Parliamentary system the executive derives its democratic legitimacy from the Legislature.
- Main advantage of the parliamentary form: the executive remains responsible to the Legislature, which gives a responsible Government. {15}
- India has a parliamentary system because the Council of Ministers (executive) is responsible to the Lok Sabha (House of the People). {14}
- **Article 75(3)** (Union): Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the House of the People (Lok Sabha). {13}
- **Article 164(2)** (States): Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly.
- Collective responsibility of the executive to the Lower House is the central characteristic of parliamentary government.
- Parliamentary system is also called the Cabinet form of government, responsible government, or the Westminster model of government.
- The government is responsible to the popular House of the legislature and remains in power as long as it has majority support in that House.
- Cabinet form of Government = a mechanism of parliamentary democracy to ensure collective responsibility of the Government to the people (House of the People). {18}
- In the Parliamentary system the Union Council of Ministers exercises executive authority.
- **Article 75(5)**: a Minister who is not a member of either House of Parliament for six consecutive months ceases to be a Minister at the end of that period.
- Implied in India's Parliamentary Government: Cabinet members are Members of Parliament; Ministers hold office while they enjoy the confidence of Parliament. {16}
- In India the Cabinet is headed by the Head of the Government, the Prime Minister; the Head of the State is the President.
- Features of the parliamentary form: Executive responsible to legislature, majority party rule, collective responsibility, nominal and real executives, political homogeneity, double membership, leadership of Prime Minister, dissolution of lower House, secrecy of procedure. {17}
- The Constitution sets up the Parliamentary system at the Centre and in the States; the Constitution is the source of authority for both Central and State governments. {23}
- Parliamentary System in India shifted from Centralism to Federalism in the 1970s (1971-80), when the Congress Party lost power in many Indian States. {83}
## India and Britain compared
- Judicial Review was adopted from the U.S. Constitution; it does not apply in the British Parliamentary System, so it differentiates the Indian system from the British one. {9,40}
- Real and nominal executive, collective responsibility and bicameral legislature are common to both the Indian and British Parliamentary systems.
- Parliamentary sovereignty is a principle of the UK constitution: Parliament is the supreme legal authority and can create or end any law.
- In the UK, courts generally cannot overrule Parliament's legislation, and no Parliament can pass laws that future Parliaments cannot change.
- With no written Constitution, the British Parliament is a sovereign body; the Indian Parliament is subject to the Constitution and its powers are not absolute.
- India follows a constitutional government: Parliament's power to legislate is limited by the Constitution, and constitutionality matters are referred to a Constitution Bench. {10}
- Basic Structure principle lets the Judiciary curb any action of the Legislature and the Executive found contradictory to it.
- Supreme Court has the power of judicial review: it can declare any legislative action inconsistent with the Basic Structure null and void.
- Constitution bench is provided under **Article 143** and **Article 145(3)**.
- The Chief Justice of India is constitutionally authorized to constitute a constitution bench and refer cases to it.
- A constitution bench has at least five judges; it decides substantial questions of law on interpretation of the Constitution.
- The Constitution explicitly establishes Judicial Review in Articles 13, 32, 136, 137, 226 and 227.
## Presidential system
- In the Presidential form the President is the head of the executive branch; the best example is the Government of the USA.
- In a Presidential Government all executive powers are vested in the President, who is both head of the State and head of the Government. {19}
- Basic feature of Presidential Government = **single Executive**; power is centralized. {31}
- Presidential form: President is Head of the state and the executive, appoints his Council of Ministers, and cannot dissolve the Legislature. {32}
- Presidential form of Government first emerged in **America**; the American Presidency is regarded as the oldest and purest form of Presidential Government. {33}
- In the American system the President is elected indirectly by the people for a fixed period.
## India and the USA compared
- Pocket Veto = indirect veto of a legislative Bill by an Executive, by keeping the Bill unsigned until adjournment of the legislature.
- Both the President of India and the American President possess a pocket veto; this is the feature common to India and the USA. {80}
- American President must return a Bill within 10 days if he refuses assent; the Indian Constitution sets no such time limit for the President of India.
- Residuary powers: vested in the Centre in India, in the States in the USA.
- All members of the upper House of the USA are directly elected; in the upper house of the Indian Parliament 12 members are nominated by the President.
- The government system of America is federal: the USA is a federal republic of 50 States. {56}
## Democracy, republic and Indian polity
- India is a Democratic Republic with a Parliamentary form of government; supreme power is vested in the people of India. {20}
- The Indian political system does not provide for a unified authority; it divides powers between the Union and the States.
- According to the Constitution, India is a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular and Democratic Republic.
- The Constitution provides for a welfare State through the Directive Principles of State Policy; India is a democratic polity, sovereign state, democratic society and welfare state. {21}
- India has the Rule of Law and a democratic Government, which is the opposite of an authoritarian Government. {22}
- Every five years the people of India elect representatives who form a Government following the Constitution.
- India is a democratic country: important decisions of governance are made by the elected representatives of the people.
- India has a written constitution, but that does not explain its democracy: the United Kingdom is democratic without a written constitution, and non-democratic countries also have some form of constitution. {24}
- The Constitution begins with "We the People of India"; the **people** are the source of political power in India. {30}
- Democratic republic = a state in which supreme power is held by the people and their elected representatives.
- India's democracy rests on the people's right to elect representatives who form the Government; people are supreme and free to choose and change the government at elections. {34}
- Democracy's virtue: it builds on the wisdom (intelligence) and character of common people. {35}
- Democracy needs people's participation; participation develops awareness, and democracy's success depends on the quality of decisions made by the people.
- **Republic** = supreme power held by the people and the Head of the state is elected; there is no hereditary ruler. {36,37}
- In India the Head of the State is elected by indirect election, through elected representatives.
- President of India is elected indirectly because the Parliamentary system in India is combined with republicanism (Head of State = President). {49}
- In a Republic all representatives and some other authorities are elected by direct or indirect election by the people.
- Political parties are the life-blood of democracy: they help form government, bring people together to gain control of Government and develop policies in people's interest; people usually blame them for bad governance. {50}
- Both **Mahatma Gandhi** and **Jay Prakash Narayan** supported party-less democracy in India. {51}
- Democracy protects individual rights and raises the self-respect and dignity of women, poor, Dalits and minority groups; they are the biggest stakeholders of democracy in India. {54}
- From the Mauryan period to the modern era, specific elements of the Indian political system still exist; it shows continuity and change, with elements of modern style, traditional style and saintly style. {53}
- The Indian Constitution is the longest written Constitution in the world.
! Question on why the Indian Constitution is the bulkiest: no single option is the reason; all four reasons given are true, so no answer is keyed.
- Reasons the Constitution is bulky: it uses the experience of many Constitutions, has detailed administrative provisions, governs a large country, and contains the Constitution of both Union and State Governments.
## Welfare state and philosophy of the Constitution
- **Welfare State** = State plays a key role in protecting and promoting the social and economic well-being of its citizens; it aims at management of welfare of weaker sections. {26}
- Welfare State rests on equality of opportunity, equitable distribution of wealth, and public responsibility for those unable to get the minimum necessities for a good life.
- "The State is everywhere: it leaves hardly a gap" explains the concept of a welfare state. {27}
- Philosophy and ideals of the Constitution are reflected in the Preamble.
- India is a democratic republic committed to equality of opportunity and status.
- The Preamble describes a socialist state as an ideal to be realised by India.
- For political equality India adopted the principle of 'Adult Franchise'.
- A Communist state is not the philosophy of the Constitution of India; welfare state, socialist state and political equality are. {28}
## Federal and unitary features
- In a Unitary Constitution all powers are concentrated in a Central authority and the States are subordinate to it.
- In a federal Constitution power is distributed between the Centre and the States; States are not subordinates of the Centre.
- Federal government = powers divided between central and state governments by the constitution itself; both operate independently in their own jurisdictions.
- The Indian Constitution is called Federal because the **7th Schedule** distributes powers between the Union and the States. {38}
- The Constitution introduces a federal system as the basic structure of Government, with a strong admixture of unitary bias; distribution of legislative powers is in the Seventh Schedule and Articles 245-255. {47}
- Only the Union is sovereign, not the States: State legislatures have no power over Foreign and Defence matters, which lie with the Union Parliament.
- Judicial power is not divided between the Union and the States; there is only a hierarchy in the judicial system.
- The Indian Constitution has both Federal and Unitary features.
- Federal features: two (dual) governments, written Constitution, rigid Constitution, supremacy of the Constitution, division of powers between Centre and States, independent judiciary, bicameral legislature.
- Unitary features: strong Centre, single Constitution, **single citizenship**, flexibility of the Constitution, integrated judiciary, appointment of Governors by the President, All India Services, Emergency provisions, Parliament's power to implement international agreements. {44}
- Unitary elements also include President's approval on state bills and constitutional crisis (emergency) provisions, along with appointment of Governor. {45}
- Unitary elements also include unequal representation of States in the Rajya Sabha, along with appointment of Governors and All India Services. {46}
- **Article 250**: Parliament can legislate on any State List matter during an Emergency.
- **Article 249**: Parliament can legislate on a State matter in the national interest.
- Independence of the judiciary is an essential condition of a federal system, because the judiciary has the power to interpret the constitution. {43}
- Characteristics of a unitary form of government: strong Centre, single Constitution, flexible Constitution, Emergency provisions, single citizenship, integrated judiciary, All-India Services, integrated audit machinery, Parliament's authority over State List, appointment of Governor, integrated election machinery, veto over State Bills.
- It cannot be said outright that unitary government is ideal for large countries; that depends on a country's will. {48}
- **Article 1** declares India a Union of States; India has 28 States and 8 Union Territories.
- India's federal design aims to weave a nation out of its many diverse parts and protect national integration; accommodating diversities has built a stronger nationhood; more powers are given to the Union. {52}
- Features of Indian federalism: written Constitution, distribution of powers, supremacy of the Constitution, independent judiciary, unequal representation of states in Rajya Sabha, dual governments.
- Indian federalism is not the result of an agreement among the federating units. {57}
- Dr. Ambedkar on the word 'Union': the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement by the units, and the units have no freedom to secede from it.
- Framers adopted the federal system for two main reasons: the large size of the country and its socio-cultural diversity.
- Institutions needed for 'unity among diversity' in Indian federalism: Inter-State Council, National Development Council, Finance Commission, Regional Council, Unitary Judicial System, All India Services. {82}
- Inter-State Council: formed under **Article 263**; discusses or investigates policies, subjects of common interest and disputes among states.
- National Development Council: apex body for decision-making and deliberations on development matters; presided over by the Prime Minister.
- Finance Commission: established under **Article 280**; defines financial relations between the Central Government and the State Governments.
- India has a Unitary Judicial system with the Supreme Court at the top.
- **Article 312** provides for the constitution of All India Services.
## Nature of the Constitution: views of scholars
- Constitutional experts have no unanimous view on the nature of the Constitution.
| Scholar | Description of Indian federalism / Constitution |
| Granville Austin | Cooperative Federalism {61} |
| K.C. Wheare | Quasi-Federal {62,63} |
| Morris-Jones | Bargaining Federalism {64} |
| Ivor Jennings | Federalism with Centralising Tendency |
| D.D. Basu | Neither purely federal nor unitary, but a combination of both {59} |
| Dr. B.R. Ambedkar | Constitution has not been set in a tight mould of federalism; it has both federal and unitary features {66} |
- K.C. Wheare in his book 'Federal Government' called the Indian system "a quasi-federal state – a unitary state with subsidiary federal features"; he is cited as originator of the concept of Quasi-federalism.
- Quasi-federal = constitution with both federal and unitary features, but with comparatively greater powers vested in the Centre.
- Quasi-federal also means the government is generally federal, but the Constitution lets the Federation turn into a unitary state, generally during an emergency.
- Indian federalism is called quasi-federal; an independent judiciary with Judicial Review is a crucial feature of the Constitution, but it is not the reason for the quasi-federal label. {58}
- K.C. Wheare: the Indian Constitution strikes a good balance between rigidity and flexibility, because the amending process has variety (some provisions by simple majority, some by special majority). {65}
- Ambedkar: the Constitution can be both unitary and federal as time and circumstances require; in normal times it works as a federal system, in war or Emergency as a unitary system. {41}
- India is not a complete Federal State because some provisions give it a unitary character; many experts still accept it as a federal state because powers are divided between Union and States. {42}
- The Indian Constitution is federal in nature but unitary in spirit. {60}
- Some experts call the Indian Constitution unitary because it gives a strong centre in emergency; in reality it is federal in nature with some unitary features.
- The Indian Constitution is partly rigid and partly flexible: some provisions amended by simple majority, some by two-thirds majority under **Article 368**, and some also need ratification by at least 50% of State Legislatures. {39}
## Constitutional government, rule of law, liberty and equality
- A Constitution establishes the structure and nature of the government and the roles and duties of the legislature, the executive and the judiciary.
- The chief purpose of a Constitution is to define and limit the power of government. {74}
- The Constitution is the source of power of a Constitutional Government; it imposes limitations on the Government in the interest of the people (individual liberty). {75}
- A constitutional government is, by definition, a **limited government**. {76}
- Constitutional government or Constitutionalism = a government's authority is determined by a body of laws or constitution; a government limited by the terms of the Constitution. {77}
- Constitutionalism is sometimes regarded as a synonym for limited government, but that is only one interpretation and not the most prominent one.
- Constitutionalism evolved to protect public interest and liberty from autocratic power of Government; constitutional governments have institutional mechanisms to protect citizens' liberties.
- **Rule of Law** was propounded by A.V. Dicey, a British jurist, with three elements: limitation of powers, equality before law, liberty and civil rights. {73}
- Limitation of powers = absence of arbitrary power; no man can be punished except for a breach of law.
- Equality before law = equal subjection of all citizens to the ordinary law of the land, administered by ordinary law courts.
- Liberty and civil rights = primacy of the rights of individuals; the Constitution is the result of individual rights as defined and enforced by courts.
- Law and liberty complement each other; liberty comes only from established laws: if there are no laws, there is no liberty. {68}
- Liberty = absence of restraints on individuals' activities and, at the same time, opportunities to develop individual personality; the positive definition is the opportunity to develop oneself fully. {78}
- Protection against tyranny of rulers and absence of restraint are negative views of liberty; doing whatever one likes relates to anarchy and lawlessness.
- The Constitution's liberty provisions (reservation, protection of minority rights, no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth) create an enabling atmosphere for full self-development.
- Equality in society implies the absence of **privileges**; the principle of equality is largely about removing special privileges. {79}
- Calls for formal equality were first made to break the hierarchy of ranks and orders surviving from feudal times.
- Privileges given to one class on grounds of gender, colour, creed or religion are seen as irrational prejudices against the principle of equality.
## Good governance and administration thinkers
- Features of good governance: accountability, transparency, rule of law; **Red Tapism** (excessive regulation that hinders action or decision-making) is not one. {25}
- Second Administrative Reforms Commission Report 12, 'Citizen-Centric Administration: The Heart of Governance', identifies barriers to good governance in India and describes pre-conditions for good governance. {81}
- **Herbert A. Simon** rejected the 'principles of administration' as myths and proverbs; Dwight Waldo, Frank Marini and F.W. Riggs accepted them. {67}
- **Fred W. Riggs** gave the concept of Development Administration: organised efforts by large-scale organisations, notably governments, to carry out policies and plans to meet development objectives. {69}
- Development administration is a dynamic concept that brings socio-economic and political-economic changes in society.
- **Henri Fayol** = father of modern management theory; gave 14 universal principles of management; favoured similarities in public and private administration. {70}
- **B. Donham**: "If our civilization fails, it will be mainly because of a breakdown of administration." {71}
