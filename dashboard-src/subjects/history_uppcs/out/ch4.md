## Birth and early life of Buddha
- Gautama Buddha, founder of Buddhism, was born in **563 BC**. {1}
- He was born at **Lumbini** near Kapilavastu (now in Nepal), in the Shakya Kshatriya clan of Kapilavastu. {4}
- Father: Shuddhodana, chief of the Shakya clan.
- Mother: Mayadevi (Mahamaya), of the **Koliya clan**. {3}
- Childhood name: Siddhartha.
- His mother died a few days after his birth; he was raised by his aunt Prajapati Gautami.
- Married at 16 to Yashodhara, daughter of the Shakya clan; later Buddhist texts also call her Gopa, Bimba, Bhadkachhana.
- Their son was Rahul.
- Ashoka's **Rummindei Pillar inscription** (Mauryan dynasty) shows that Lumbini was Buddha's birthplace. {6,7,8}
- As per this inscription Ashoka came to Lumbini after the 20th year of his coronation and worshipped the place where Shakyamuni was born.
- Ashoka freed Lumbini from Bali tax; it had to pay only an eighth share of the produce.
- Buddha is also called 'Tathagata' and 'Shakyamuni' (also Siddhartha, Gautama); Parth and Gudakesh were names of Arjuna in the Mahabharata; Mihir and Prachhanna were not names of Buddha. {5}
! On 'another name of Buddha' the intended answer is 'none of these', but the Chhattisgarh commission gave 'Prachhanna' in both its initial and revised keys.
- **Tathagata** means 'he who has won truth'; the exact meaning is uncertain and Buddhist commentaries give more than one meaning.
- Buddha used 'Tathagata' for himself after enlightenment (bodhi); it became one of his stock epithets.
- **Shakyamuni** = 'Sage of the Shakyas', as he was born in the Shakya clan; Nayaputta is not his epithet. {91}
- Nayaputta (Son of Nayas) is Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara of Jainism; the name refers to his clan of origin.
- Gautama Buddha is known as the **'Light of Asia'** (Light Beam). {89,90}
- Edwin Arnold wrote 'The Light of Asia' (subtitle 'The Great Renunciation') on Buddha's life; first edition July 1879; based on the contents of **'Lalit Vistara'**. {95}
## Renunciation and enlightenment
- Four sights deeply affected Buddha: an old man, a sick person, a dead body and an ascetic/monk.
- Siddhartha left home at age 29, leaving wife and child while they slept, on his horse "Kanthaka", with Channa.
- Leaving home is called **'Mahabhinishkramana'** in Buddhist texts. {2}
- He met **Alara Kalama**, Acharya of Sankhya philosophy, near Vaishali; Alara Kalama was a teacher of Buddha, famed for spiritual strength. {20}
- Buddha learnt much from Alara Kalama, including meditation, and equalled him; Alara Kalama then asked him to stay and teach his students with him.
- At Rajgriha Siddhartha met another Dharmacharya, Rudrak Ramputta.
- After six years of effort he got enlightenment at age 35, on Vaishakh Purnima night, under a Pipal (Bodhi) tree at **Bodh Gaya** (Bihar); the Mahabodhi temple was built there. {19,56}
- After enlightenment he was called 'Buddha'.
- Bodh Gaya (Uruvela) is on the bank of the river **Niranjana**. {58}
- Present Bodhi Vriksha at Mahabodhi temple is not the original; as per Hiuen Tsang, king Shashank destroyed the original tree in the 7th century.
- The present tree was planted on Alexander Cunningham's orders and is called the **fifth generation** tree; it is a heritage site and only its fallen leaves may be touched and picked. {57}
! Another source says the tree was planted twice by Ashoka, once by Pushyamitra Shunga, once by Purvaverma and last by Cunningham, making it the 6th generation; Bihar Tourism calls it 5th; BPSC deleted the question from its key.
## Preaching life
- From Uruvela (Bodh Gaya) Buddha first went to Rishipattan (Mrigadava / Deer Park; present **Sarnath**, Varanasi) and gave his first sermon to five Brahmin sanyasis. {21,22,23,24,26}
- The first sermon is called **'Dharmachakrapravartana'** (Pali: Dhammachakkhapavathana). {25}
- The first sermon was about suffering, the cause of suffering and the path to end suffering.

| Birth | Lumbini |
| Enlightenment | Bodh Gaya |
| First preaching | Sarnath |
| Death | Kushinagar {51} |
| Event | Symbol |
| Birth | Lotus & Bull |
| Great Departure (Mahabhinishkramana) | Horse |
| Enlightenment (Nirvana) | Peepal (Bodhi tree) |
| First Sermon (Dharmachakrapravartana) | Wheel |

- In Indian art the wheel with deer stands for the first preaching. {52}
- After Sarnath he went to Koshala, Kapilavastu, Vaishali and Rajgriha to spread his teaching.
- Of the kingdoms, Buddha was linked with **Koshala and Magadha**; he visited both to preach. {14}
- He spent the 2nd, 3rd and 4th rainy seasons at Rajgriha.
- Bimbisara, ruler of Magadha, built the Mahavihara 'Venuvana' for his stay.
- From Rajgriha he reached Vaishali, capital of the Lichchhavis, and spent the 5th rainy season there.
- Lichchhavis built the famous 'Kutagrasala' in Mahavan for his stay.
- Amrapali, royal courtesan of Vaishali, became his disciple and gave her mango grove (Amravatika) for the monks.
- In the 8th year of enlightenment Buddha let women enter the Sangha as Bhikshuni at **Vaishali**, on the advice of his dear disciple Ananda. {65}
- Mahaprajapati Gautami, the aunt who raised him, was the first woman to enter the Sangha.
- **Devadatta**, Buddha's cousin, was first a follower and good monk, then an enemy; he tried to remove Buddha and head the Sangha himself, but failed. {60}
- Buddhism became very popular in the Koshal state; most of Buddha's disciples were from Koshala.
- Buddha gave the most sermons at **Shravasti**, capital of Koshala. {27}
- Anathpindak, a rich merchant of Koshal, became his disciple and donated 'Jetavana' Vihara to the Sangha.
- Koshal king Prasenjit and his family adopted Buddhism.
- 'Pubharama' (Purva-Ram) vihara was built for the Sangha by Vishakha, a disciple of Buddha.
- Buddha visited Kaushambi in the reign of **King Udayana**; influenced by Pindola Bharadwaj, Udayana turned to Buddhism and gave Ghoshitram Vihar to the Bhikshu Sangh. {28}
- Buddha did not name any successor; he asked his followers to work towards liberation. {61}
## Last days and Mahaparinirvana
- Buddha spent his last rainy season at Beluva village, a suburb of **Vaishali**, at age eighty, with two disciples, one of them Ananda. {17}
- At Pava (capital of Mallas) he stayed in the mango grove of Chund, a blacksmith.
- Chund gave him 'Sukaramaddava' to eat; it caused sickness with bleeding and violent pains (food poisoning).
- At Kushinagar he gave his last sermon to **Subhadda**, the last person he converted. {15,16}
- Buddha died at **Kushinagar (Kushinara)**, in the Republic of the Mallas, in 483 BC at age 80; this is called **'Mahaparinirvana'**. {9,10,11,12}
- Kushinagar was the capital of the Malla republic (first capital city of the Malla kingdom) and one of the sixteen Mahajanapadas of the 6th century B.C. {108}
- **Mahaparinirvana temple** is in Kushinagar district, Uttar Pradesh. {13}
- Its Buddha statue was found in excavation in 1876-77 AD; carved from Chunar sandstone; 6.10 metres; it shows the dying Buddha lying on his right side.
- Four holy places of Buddha's life: Lumbini (birth), Bodhgaya (enlightenment), Sarnath (first sermon), Kushinagar (death).
## Teachings and philosophy
- Buddha's teachings rest mainly on purity and holiness of conduct. {59}
- Buddha gave the 'Four Noble Truths' and the 'Noble Eightfold Path', accepted as basic tenets by all Buddhist sects.
- Four Noble Truths (Arya Satya) in order: there is suffering → there is a cause of suffering → there is a cessation of suffering → there is a path to the cessation of suffering. {137}
- Also worded: life means suffering; the origin of suffering is attachment; cessation is attainable; there is a path to cessation.
- The eightfold path is the theme of the **'Dharma Chakra Pravartana Sutta'** (sermon of the turning of the wheel of law); it is the way to end life's miseries and reach Nirvana. {62}
- Buddha was born near Kapilavastu, got enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, discarded the Vedas and Vedic rituals (favouring noble deeds), and preached the noble truths. {55}
- Buddha believed in Karma, rebirth and attaining Nirvana, but not in the existence or power of God. {64}
- Buddha's previous lives are recorded in the Jatakas.
- Buddhism sees desire as the cause of all pain; **Nirvana** literally means 'blown out': the extinction of the flame of desire, a state free from desire, pain and pleasure. {18}
- As per Buddhist philosophy the cause of Samskara (impression) is **Avidya** (ignorance). {63}
- Avidya = absence of knowledge, not understanding reality; ignorance is the root of all suffering.
- Buddhism preaches **Anatmavada** (no-self): no permanent self, soul or essence; this follows from Buddha's principle of Pratityasamutpada. {87}
- **Pratityasamutpada** is the essence of Buddhist teaching: life arises from interdependent preconditions; one state comes from a previous state.
- Buddhism rejected the infallibility of the Vedas and the immortality of the soul, yet accepted rebirth and Karma. {139}
- **Anitya** (impermanence) is an essential doctrine and one of the three marks of existence, given by Buddha himself: all conditioned existence is "transient, evanescent, inconstant". {88}
- All temporal things, material or mental, are compounded objects in constant change, subject to decline and destruction; impermanence is a source of Dukkha.
- The doctrine of **momentariness** was propounded by the Buddha; through it he explained impermanence. {93}
- The seed of Buddhist philosophy lies in Buddha's own teachings; he stressed a moral life to end suffering, not philosophical problems. {75}
- Later Buddhist scholars built a deep philosophy on Buddha's teachings.
- **Triratna** (three jewels) of Buddhism: Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha. {74}
- **'Paramitas'** = perfections; qualities a bodhisattva perfects to purify karma and kleshas and develop bodhichitta. {94}
- Upasaka = lay follower of Buddhism (not a monk); Parivrajaka = male renunciant and wanderer; a Buddhist Bhikkhu is primarily a parivrajaka. {106}
- Shramana = wandering monk in ascetic traditions such as Jainism, Buddhism and Ajivikism.
- **Pavarana**: after the four months of rainy season (spent in the Mahavihar without preaching), monks confess their offences and plan future work. {76}
## Buddhism and Jainism; society; spread and decline
- Common to Buddhism and Jainism: indifference to the authority of the Vedas, denial of the efficacy of rituals, and non-injury to animals (Ahimsa). {134,135}
- Avoiding extremes of penance and enjoyment is a Buddhist trait, not a Jain one. {136}
- Both Buddhism and Jainism believe in Karma and rebirth: past actions affect present and future life. {138}
- Karma idea: one who does evil in this life may be reborn as a worm; afflictions of this life are explained as effects of Karma of a previous life or of earlier misdeeds in this life.
- Buddhism accepted the caste system to some extent, challenged the top rank of Brahmins, and placed some castes as low. {140}
- Reasons for spread: simplicity (eightfold path, non-violence), appeal to non-Brahmins and lower classes (Dalits), missionary spirit, and preaching in the common people's language. {141}
- Buddhism did not believe in caste distinctions.
- Buddhism declined in early medieval India because Buddha came to be seen as an incarnation of Vishnu and became part of Vaishnavism. {142}
- Gupta kings supported the Brahmanical faith but were not opposed to Buddhism.
- **Pushyamitra Shunga** (185-149 BCE), founder and first ruler of the Shunga empire, did not help Buddhism; Buddhist texts say he cruelly persecuted Buddhists. {107}
- Pushyamitra was a general of the Mauryan Empire; in 185 BCE he killed the last Mauryan emperor Brihadratha and made himself emperor.
- **Neo-Buddhism (Navayana, "new vehicle")** is B.R. Ambedkar's re-interpretation of Buddhism. {92}
- Navayana rejects renouncing monk and monasticism, Karma, rebirth, samsara, meditation, enlightenment and the four noble truths; it revises Buddha's teachings on class struggle and social equality.
- Karmapa Lama belongs to the **Kangyupa** sect of Tibetan Buddhism. {54}

- The Sangha held several councils to reach agreement on doctrine and practice; Buddha's teachings existed only in oral tradition.

- It met in the **Saptaparni Cave** of Rajgriha. {33}
- **Mahakassapa** presided over the first council. {29,30,31}
- Its patron was the Magadha king **Ajatashatru**. {46}
- Aim of the first council: preserve Buddha's teachings (Sutta) and rules for disciples (Vinaya); Ananda recited the Suttas and Upali the Vinaya; Abhidhamma Pitaka was also included.
- Second council: at **Vaishali**, 100 years after Buddha's death. {44}
- Second council was held in the reign of **Kalasoka**; Sabakami presided. {48}

- Fourth council was held at Kundalavana in **Kashmir**; so it was the one not held in Bihar. {37,49}
- Fourth council was held under the Kushan king **Kanishka**. {38,39,40}
- **Vasumitra** presided over the fourth council; Ashvaghosha was his deputy. {35}
- The fourth council clearly split Buddhism into **Hinayana and Mahayana**; Mahasanghikas dominated it. {36}
## Sects: Hinayana, Mahayana and others
- In the first century, those holding the traditional faith were called 'Sthavira', later 'Theravada'; led by Mahakatyayana.
- Those who accepted change were called 'Mahasanghika'; led by Mahakassapa.
- Sthaviravadins belong to Hinayana; the Lokottaravadin sect was an offshoot of the Mahasanghikas; deification of Buddha by Mahasanghikas fostered Mahayana. {112}
- Basic difference: Hinayana sees Buddha as a great man; Mahayana treats him as God and worships him through idols. {96}
- Buddha was raised to the position of God in **Kanishka's** period. {97}
- Buddha's were the first human statues worshipped in India; Mahayana followers began it, so Buddhism laid the foundation of idol worship in the country. {98,99}
| Point | Hinayana | Mahayana |
| Literal meaning | Lesser Vehicle | Greater Vehicle |
| View of Buddha | A great man | A god |
| Nature | Individualistic: each person gets salvation by own effort | Simple and accessible principles; common devotees matter along with monks; believes in soul and rebirth |
| Idol worship | Not believed in | Believed in |
| Practice | Very strict worship; follows monk life | Idol worship and rituals |
| Ideal | To attain 'Arhat' | 'Bodhisattva' |
| Major sects | Vaibhashika and Sautrantika | Shunyawad (Madhyamik) and Vigyanwad (Yogachar) |
- Mahayana features: belief in Buddha's divinity and many forms and incarnations; the path of the Bodhisattva; idol worship and rituals. {41}
- **Bodhisattva** = bodhi (enlightenment) + sattva (essence, spirit / living being): one who aspires to enlightenment and does altruistic acts.
- Bodhisattva is the goal and ideal of Mahayana (not Hinayana): a compassionate one who delays his own salvation to help all sentient beings. {114}
- **Avalokiteshvara**, Bodhisattva of infinite compassion, is also called **Padmapani** ("Lotus-Bearer"); the sculpture stands in tribhanga (tri-bent) pose. {110}
- **Maitreya** is the 'future Buddha' of Buddhist tradition. {113}
- Maitreya first appears in the Cakkavatti Sutta of the Pali Tripitaka, where Buddha speaks of a future when dharma is forgotten and Maitreya gains awakening with a Sangha of thousands.
- This is the only time the historical Buddha is recorded as mentioning Maitreya.
- Sautrantika and Sammitiya were sects of Buddhism, not Jainism.
- **Sarvastivada** = "doctrine that all exist": past, present and future phenomena all have some form of existence; things empirical are transient but dharma factors exist forever. {143}
- **Vajrayana** was the main form of Buddhism in the medieval (Sultanate) period; it flourished mostly in the 8th century A.D. {150}
- Vajrayana principles are in 'Arya Manjushrimulkalpa' and 'Guhyasamaja'.
- 'Aryamanjushrimulakalpa' is a Buddhist tantric text linked with Mahayana and Vajrayana; it gives instructions on rituals, meditations and practices.
## Scholars
- **Nagarjuna's** main contribution was the concept of **Sunyata** (doctrine of void). {122}
- Nagarjuna wrote "Madhyamikakarika" (Madhyamika school), which has his famous words on emptiness or co-arising. {123}
- Nagarjuna was an eminent figure in Kanishka's court; Hiuen Tsang called him one of the world's four great guiding powers.
- In "Madhyamikakarika" Nagarjuna discussed the theory of relativity; he is called the 'Indian Einstein'.
- Chinese mythology says Nagarjuna travelled to China and preached Buddhism. {121}
- Aryadeva was a Buddhist philosopher and disciple of Nagarjuna; Dignaga was a Buddhist scholar; Nathamuni was a Vaishnava scholar, seen as the first teacher of the Vaishnava sect. {124}
- **Sanghabhuti**, Indian Buddhist monk who went to China at the end of the 4th century AD, wrote a commentary on the **Sarvastivada Vinaya**. {151}
- Sarvastivada Vinaya gives rules of monastic life for the Sarvastivada school; it was the most common vinaya tradition in early China; Sanghabhuti's commentary explained the Vinaya rules for Chinese Buddhist practitioners.
## Buddhist literature
- **Tripitaka** is the most important holy book of Buddhism: the collection of Buddha's teachings in three parts, compiled after his death. {66,67,68,69}
- **Sutta Pitaka**: sayings, sermons and teachings of Buddha; the book of discourses on ethics and doctrine; the basic doctrinal part of the canon. {73}
- Digha Nikaya, a part of the Sutta Pitaka, has 34 long Suttas with doctrinal expositions, legends and moral rules.
- **Vinaya Pitaka**: rules for members of the Sangha and monks (monastic life). {70}
- **Abhidhamma Pitaka**: philosophical exposition of the Dhamma.
- Abhidhamma Pitaka has seven books: **Yamaka**, Patthana, Kathavatthu, Puggalapannatti, Dhatukatha, Vibhanga and Dhammasangani. {71}
- Mahavamsa gives regional histories of Buddhism. {72}
- **Jatakas** = texts with stories of Buddha's previous births; kept alive orally before being written in Pali.
- Divyavadana and Aryamanjushree Mulakalpa were written in Sanskrit; Deepvamsa and Mahavamsa (history of ancient India and Sri Lanka) in Pali. {120}

## Centres of learning
- Main Buddhist centres of learning (Gupta and post-Gupta period): Nalanda, **Vikramshila** and Vallabhi; Nagpur was not one. {125,126}
- They drew students from many Janapadas and from abroad: China, Japan, Tibet and other countries of South-East Asia.
- Taxila and Nalanda had the status of universities.
- Nalanda was the main centre for Mahayana education; Vallabhi for Hinayana.
- **Vallabhi** (Vallabhipura) was in Gujarat; capital of the ancient Maitraka dynasty. {127}
- **Vikramshila Mahavihara** was founded by Pala king Dharmapala, who also built temples and monasteries there.
- **Nalanda**: large Buddhist monastery in Magadha (Bihar), near Rajgriha (Rajgir); it came up and flourished in the **Gupta** period. {128}
- Nalanda was famous for Buddhist religious philosophy. {131}
- Nalanda, called the world's first international university, was founded in 450 AD in the reign of **Kumaragupta**. {129}
- Kumaragupta I first gave donations to Nalanda Vihara; later Buddhgupta, Tathagatgupta and Baladitya also donated.
- Alexander Cunningham identified the site as Nalanda University (year printed as 1915-16).
- Kautilya (Chanakya; 4th-3rd century BCE; minister of Chandragupta Maurya and Bindusara; author of Arthashastra) was linked with Takshashila, not Nalanda. {130}
- By the end of the 12th century Nalanda Mahavihara waned due to Muslim invasion; it had been losing importance as Pala rulers of Bengal gave protection to Vikramshila instead. {132}
- **Nava Nalanda Mahavihara** is a modern Pali and Buddhist research centre; foundation stone laid on 20 November 1951. {133}
- **Odantapuri** (Uddantapura), in Magadha (Bihar), was a major education centre; founded by Pala emperor Gopala I.
- **Dhanyakataka** (present Dharnikota, Palnadu district, **Andhra** Pradesh): major Buddhist centre under the Mahasanghikas; key Deccan town and capital of the later Satavahanas. {149}
- Excavation at Dharnikota has revealed viharas there and nearby.
- Ashokaram Vihar was built at **Pataliputra** by Ashoka, under the monk Indragupta, as per the Mahavansh. {77}
- Rajgir is a pilgrim centre for Buddhists, Hindus and Jains, famous for hot water springs.
- Lion Capital of Ashoka is at Sarnath, not Rajgir; Lothal has an ancient dockyard; Sarnath saw the first sermon; Nalanda was a great seat of Buddhist learning. {109}
## Stupa, Chaitya and Vihara
- Stupa, Chaitya and Vihara are all related to **Buddhism**. {145}
- **Chaitya** is a place of worship (a meeting or assembly hall used for prayer); **Vihara** is the dwelling place of monks, their shelter in the rainy season. {144,146}
- "Chaitya" literally means 'related to Chita (funeral pyre)'.
- Samadhis built over buried bodies were first called Chaitya or Stupa; relics of great men kept in them made Chaityas centres of worship.
- Houses built near monasteries or Chaitya Griha were called Vihara.
- The word 'Stupa' is first used in the **Rig Veda**, where it means "tree stem". {81}
- Rigveda speaks of a stupa raised by king Varuna above the forest in a place with no foundation; anything raised like a heap or pile may have been called a stupa.
- The idea of the stupa is older than Buddhism (word found in Rig Veda, Atharva Veda, Vajasaneyi Samhita, Taittiriya Samhita); stupas were usually repositories of relics and later became votive and commemorative. {83}
- 'Stupa' literally means 'to pile up': something raised by accumulation; building stupas in memory of the dead existed before Buddhism. {148}
- The practice grew from old burial customs: tombs over the remains of dead community leaders later became stupas.
- In Buddhism a stupa is a monument (dirt burial mound faced with stone) to keep the remains of Buddha or his chief disciples; the earliest held portions of Buddha's ashes.
- Four types of stupa: Sharirik (body), Paribhogika, Uddheshika (objective) and Vratanusthit (worshipping).
- **Sanchi Stupa** is not linked to any event of Buddha's life; Bodhgaya = enlightenment, Sarnath = Dharma Chakra Pravartana, Kushinagar = death. {82}
- Bharhut and Sanchi stupas were built in Ashoka's reign; some sources also place Dhamekh stupa in Ashoka's reign.
- **Amaravati stupa** was built in the Satavahana period. {85}
! The book says the order of building of Bharhut, Sanchi and Dhamekh stupas cannot be decided, so the stupa-chronology question should be excluded from evaluation.
- **Devnimori (Devni Mori)** is in Arvalli district, **Gujarat**; remains of a Buddhist monastery and stupa of the 3rd-4th century AD were found. {86}
- **Vishwa Shanti Stupa** (World Peace Pagoda) on the hills of **Rajgir**, Bihar, is called the highest stupa in the world (height printed as 400 metres). {78}
- **Kesariya Stupa** (East Champaran, Bihar): about 104 feet high and 400 feet in circumference; called the largest and tallest Buddhist stupa in the world. {79}
- By ASI's 1998 excavation and State Government literature, Kesariya is one foot taller than Borobudur (Indonesia).
- Early Chaityagrihas and Viharas of Guntuphalli (then West Godavari district, Andhra Pradesh; now NTR district) are rock-cut; Chaityas and Viharas of Eastern Deccan are usually rock-cut. {147}
- The largest and most evolved Chaitya cave of the Hinayana phase is at **Karle**, Pune district, Maharashtra. {117}
- **Kanheri caves** (Salsette Island, Mumbai; in Sanjay Gandhi National Park): cave 41 (earlier 23) has an Avalokiteshvara with four arms and eleven heads, the only such statue in India. {118}
- Kanheri, called Kanhashail, Krishnagiri, Kanhagiri in old records, was a main Buddhist centre; it has the most caves on a single hill; built in 1st century B.C., in use till 11th century AD.
- Worship of the eleven-headed Bodhisattva was current in China, Chinese Turkistan, Cambodia and Japan in 7th-8th CE.
## Images, mudras and paintings
- Gandhara art flourished under the Kushanas, especially Kanishka, a great patron of art and architecture; standing statues of Buddha belong to the **Kushana period**. {101}
- Both sitting and standing Buddha statues were carved in the Mathura school.
- Kushana art of Gandhara is known for the many sculptures found in Buddhist stupas and monasteries.
- Gandharan Buddha image drew on Hellenistic realism, tempered by Persian, Scythian and Parthian models; its main subject is Buddha's life.
- Gandharan images show Dharma Chakra, Meditation, Abhaya and Varada postures; the Sarnath first sermon is shown by the **Dharma Chakra** mudra. {100}
- First Buddha statue: Mathura as per V.S. Agrawal; Kaushambi as per Hiuen Tsang; other historians credit Gandhara; so it cannot be said for certain where it was first made. {102}
- **Bhumisparsha Mudra**: Buddha seated, right hand over right knee reaching the ground, palm inward, touching the lotus throne; left hand in lap, palm up.
- It shows Buddha calling the earth to witness his enlightenment (purity) despite Mara; Mara tried to frighten him with armies of demons and was defeated. {103}
- The Bhumisparsha Mudra Buddha image of Sarnath belongs to the **Gupta period**. {104,105}
- This mudra stands for steadfastness; Dhyani Buddha Akshobhya is linked with it for keeping his vow never to feel anger or disgust.
- It also shows the union of skilful means (upaya; right hand touching earth) and wisdom (prajna; left hand in lap).
- The Giant Buddha statue at Bodh Gaya is 80 feet high, in dhyana mudra, seated on a lotus in the open; built with help of the Daijokyo sect of **Japan**. {80}
- It took seven years to complete; made of sandstone blocks and red granite.
- The painting of **Bodhisattva Padmapani** is in Cave I of **Ajanta**. {115,116}
- Padmapani in Sanskrit = "one who holds the lotus"; he is shown among devotees (princesses and commoners), with long dark hair under a jewelled crown, a blue lotus in his right hand.
