## Robert Clive
- **Robert Clive** is widely considered the founder of British India (British Empire in India). {6}
- Clive arrived in Madras in 1744 as a clerk of the East India Company.
- Clive's leadership consolidated the Company's commercial and territorial position in India.
- 1757: Company troops defeated the Nawab of Bengal at Plassey → creation of British Raj in India.
- Battle of Buxar: 22/23 October 1764; fought under the leadership of **Hector Munro**.
- Clive's re-arrival in India: 3 May 1765; Treaty of Allahabad: August 1765.
- Clive enforced the **Dual Government** system in Bengal in 1765; administration divided into Diwani and Nizamat. {12,130}
- Clive made both civil and military reforms of the Company's services.
- Clive's civil reforms: banned gifts to officials, controlled (banned) private trade, made internal tax compulsory.
- Clive's gift ban was conditional: gifts above Rs. 1000 needed the Governor's prior permission.
- Clive's military reform: ended **double batta** (dual allowance) for European officers.
- From 1 January 1766 double batta was given only to soldiers serving outside Bengal and Bihar (except Allahabad).
- Clive was not the first Governor-General of Bengal.
## Warren Hastings
- Warren Hastings became Governor of Bengal in 1772 and Governor-General of Bengal in 1774.
! One exam question wrongly called Warren Hastings "Governor of India"; he was Governor of Bengal (1772) and Governor-General of Bengal (1774).
- Order of events: Battle of Buxar (1764) → Clive's re-arrival (May 1765) → Treaty of Allahabad (August 1765) → Warren Hastings Governor of Bengal (1772).
- **Regulating Act 1773**: British Parliament set up government in Bengal = Governor-General + four councillors ("Council of four"), total five.
- Regulating Act 1773 was passed after acquiring Bengal, to regulate Company administration; the Company's Governor became Governor-General of Bengal.
- The Governor-General was head of government and chairman of the Executive Council; had a casting vote if votes were equal.
- The council was established to look after the affairs of Bengal.
- **Warren Hastings** = first Governor-General of Bengal (1774, under Regulating Act 1773).
- Warren Hastings ended the dual government of Bengal in 1772 by order of the Court of Directors. {11}
- Court of Directors (1772) ordered the Calcutta council and its chiefs to act as Diwan and administer Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
- Warren Hastings dismissed both deputy Diwans: Muhammad Raza Khan and Raja Shitab Roy.
- Warren Hastings shifted the State treasury from Murshidabad to Calcutta.
- Warren Hastings believed the whole land belonged to the ruler.
- In revenue reform Warren Hastings used the trial and error method.
- Warren Hastings firmly laid the foundation of civil administration; Cornwallis raised the superstructure on it.
- **Banaras Rebellion** (1781) was led by Raja Chet Singh. {7}
- In the Banaras Rebellion Warren Hastings ran away with his soldiers and took refuge in Chunar Fort.
- **Asiatic Society of Bengal** was founded in Calcutta on 15 January 1784 by Sir William Jones, when Warren Hastings was Governor-General. {8}
- **Ring Fence** policy (also called 'Security Cell' / policy of siege / buffer state) is related to Warren Hastings and Wellesley. {9,10}
- Warren Hastings fought Mysore and the Marathas to gain status equal to other Indian states.
- Ring Fence aim: create buffer states to protect Company territory from Afghans and Marathas.
- Under Ring Fence the Company agreed to protect Oudh if the Nawab of Oudh bore the expenses.
- Wellesley's main aim was to make Indian states rely on his army power.
- Warren Hastings faced **impeachment** proceedings (1788–1795) for injustices, atrocities and arbitrary acts. {13,14}
- British Parliament dropped all charges in view of his service; he was acquitted in April 1795.
- It was said that Warren Hastings had done all this for the benefit of the empire.
- Pitt's India Act is matched with Warren Hastings.

## Cornwallis
- **Lord Cornwallis** was Governor-General of Bengal 1786–1793 and again 30 July 1805 – 5 October 1805.
- Cornwallis died at Ghazipur (Uttar Pradesh) on 5 October 1805; his grave/tomb is at Ghazipur. {20}
- Cornwallis is known for Istamarari (Permanent Settlement), the judicial code and as promoter of Indian administrative services.
- Cornwallis introduced the **Permanent Settlement** in Bengal in 1793. {82,84,113}
- Cornwallis's rule is remembered for two events: Permanent Settlement in Bengal and the Third Mysore War.
- Major events of Cornwallis's tenure: ban on slave trade, start of civil services, police services, Permanent Settlement.
- Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–1792) is associated with Cornwallis; he himself took charge of the army. {40}
- Treaty of Srirangapatna (1792): Tipu Sultan gave up almost half of his state.
- Reforms in police, judiciary, taxation and Faujdari-related Acts took place under Cornwallis.
- British built a hierarchical justice system of Diwani and Faujdari courts; started by Warren Hastings, made more rigid by Cornwallis.
- Cornwallis first concentrated all powers in the Collector.
- 1787: District Collector made Diwani Judge of Diwani courts and given some Faujdari powers.
- Cornwallis gave limited judicial powers to the Company's revenue collectors, who already served as civil magistrates.
- Cornwallis established the judicial organisation in India; his criminal and judicial regulations still underpin the Indian judicial system. {16}
- 1790: Company took over administration of justice from the Nawab.
- Cornwallis introduced circuit courts with a superior court at Calcutta having power of review over circuit court decisions.
- 1790–92: District Faujdari courts with Indian judges abolished; four Circuit Courts set up (three for Bengal, one for Bihar).
- Circuit Courts were headed by European officials; justice delivered with help of Indian Kazis and Muftis.
- Cornwallis started rule by specification of law: regulations codifying administrative, revenue and judicial reforms of the Company in Bengal.
- **Cornwallis Code** (1793) = collection of his judicial reforms, based on 'Separation of Powers'.
- Cornwallis Code separated revenue and judicial administration; Collector lost judicial and faujdari powers, kept only taxation powers.
- A new hierarchy of officers was created for District Diwani Courts; District Judges were also given Faujdari and police work.
- Cornwallis reorganised and professionalised the civil service (1793): created the Covenanted Civil Service, later the **Indian Civil Service**. {17,18}
- Cornwallis forbade private trade and gifts, raised salaries, separated functions, gave legal form to civil service rules; called reformer of civil service.
- Cornwallis restructured administration, abolished the revenue boards and laid down new laws for them.
- 1793: Cornwallis removed police duties from landlords (zamindars); set up a police station in every 20–30 square miles.
- The police officer of the station was the **Daroga**, appointed and supervised by the Magistrate.
- Farmers saw the Daroga as local representative of the "aura and authority (pride) of Company Bahadur". {19}
## George Barlow and Vellore
- **Vellore Mutiny** (1806) took place under Governor-General Sir George Barlow. {15}
- Vellore Mutiny was a protest against interference in social and religious practices of sepoys.
- Vellore sepoys unfurled the flag of the ruler of Mysore.
## Wellesley and Subsidiary Alliance
- **Lord Wellesley** was Governor-General 1798–1805.
- Wellesley came to India in 1798 (one explanation prints 1797), the darkest period of British history.
- Britain was then in a life-and-death struggle with France; the European front against France had been disbanded.
- Napoleon had conquered Egypt and Syria and was seriously thinking of attacking India.
- Wellesley used Subsidiary Alliance to establish British sovereignty and avert the fear of Napoleon; he kept the British flag flying. {32}
- **Subsidiary Alliance** system was introduced (given final shape, codified in 1798) by Lord Wellesley. {25,29,31}
- Wellesley first followed non-intervention in princely states, later adopted Subsidiary Alliance.
- Wellesley did not invent the method; it existed earlier, evolved gradually and got final shape from him.
- **Dupleix** was the first European to lend his army to an Indian ruler for a fixed payment.
- First such British instance: 1765 treaty with Awadh (Oudh), precursor of subsidiary alliance; Company to guard Awadh's borders for fixed payment. {24}
! Nawab of Oudh is the first ruler to join a subsidiary alliance (1765); Hyderabad is the answer if asked the first state in Wellesley's subsidiary alliance.
- **Nizam of Hyderabad** was the first to enter Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance (1798 and 1800). {27}
- Order of Wellesley's alliances: Hyderabad (1798, 1800) → Mysore (1799) → Tanjore (October 1799) → Awadh (November 1801) → Peshwa (December 1802) → Bhonsle of Nagpur/Berar (December 1803) → Sindhia (February 1804). {28}
- Bharatpur (1803–05) is also listed with Sindhia.
- Other states listed: Holkar (1818), Jodhpur (1818), Udaipur/Mewar (1818), Bundi (February 1818), Bikaner (March 1818), Jaipur (April 1818), Machheri, Bharatpur.
- Peshwa **Bajirao II** was the first Maratha chief to accept Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance. {22}
- **Treaty of Bassein** (now Vasai): 31 December 1802, between English East India Company and Peshwa Bajirao II, after the Battle of Poona. {23}
- Bassein terms: about 6,000 British troops to reinstate and protect the Peshwa at Poona; Peshwa to give land with revenue of 26 lakh a year.
- Subsidiary Alliance aims: keep a large army at others' expense, guard India from Napoleonic danger, establish British paramountcy over Indian states.
- Securing a fixed income for the Company was not an aim of Subsidiary Alliance. {21}
- Aim of subsidiary alliances with Rajput states: establish British sovereignty; brought Indian states within British political power. {30}
- Wellesley used the alliance to make Indian provinces puppets of the British.
- Term 1: ruler accepts permanent British force in his territory and pays a subsidy for its maintenance.
- Term 2: ruler accepts a British Resident in his state.
- Term 3: ruler will not enter any further alliance with any other power.
- Term 4: ruler will not employ Europeans other than the British.
- Term 5: in conflict with another state, ruler accepts the resolution decided by the British.
- Term 6: ruler acknowledges East India Company as paramount power in India.
- Term 7: in return Company protects the state from external dangers and disorders.
- Term 8: if payments fail, part of the ruler's territory is taken as penalty.
- Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799) took place under Lord Wellesley. {89}
- Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805) was fought under Lord Wellesley, not Lord Hastings. {85}
- Major events of Wellesley's tenure: Subsidiary Alliance, Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, Second Anglo-Maratha War, Fort William College at Calcutta.
- Battle of Buxar 1764; trade in India opened for British citizens and companies 1813; Company's monopoly in India 1833.
## Lord Hastings
- **Lord Hastings** was Governor-General 1813–23.
- **Anglo-Nepal War** (1814–16) took place under Lord Hastings (not Sir John Shore); ended by Treaty of Sugauli. {33,88}
- **Treaty of Sugauli** signed 2 December 1815, ratified 4 March 1816. {34}
- First Anglo-Nepal war (1814) ended without result; second took place in 1815.
- 1815: Almora in Kumaon was won; Major-General Ochterlony forced Gorkha sardar Amar Singh to hand over the Fort of Malwa.
- British army then stood only 30 miles from Kathmandu.
- By Sugauli Nepal ceded Kumaon, eastern Garhwal and some parts of the Terai (also printed: Kumaon, Garhwal and Shimla districts).
- Ranikhet and Nainital lie in Kumaon (present Uttarakhand), so they came under the Company by Sugauli. {35}
- Kathmandu stayed Nepal's capital; the treaty only provided for a British Resident there.
- British got control of the hill tract of Shimla; land for Shimla station/town was actually transferred in 1830 from Keonthal/Patiala.
- **Third Anglo-Maratha War** (1817–18) took place under Lord Hastings, not Cornwallis. {36,37}
- After the war Lord Hastings abolished the title of Peshwa (1818) and merged Peshwa's regions in Bombay Presidency. {86}
- Company also took political authority over Bundelkhand, Malwa and other Peshwa-dominated regions.
- Lord Hastings wanted British paramountcy; this needed suppression of Marathas and Pindaris.
- He saw Rajputana states as natural allies against Marathas and Pindaris.
- **Charles Metcalf** negotiated the subordinate alliances of 1817–18 with Rajputana states. {87}
- **Sir Thomas Munro** was Governor of Madras 1820–1827. {38}
- Munro = father of the Ryotwari System: land tax collected directly from ryots (owners of land).
## William Bentinck
- **Lord William Bentinck** became Governor-General of Bengal in July 1828; ruled seven years (1828–35); returned to England in 1835.
- **Charter Act 1833** tried to fully centralise administration; Governor-General of Bengal became Governor-General of India.
- Bentinck = first Governor-General of India (Charter Act 1833). {1,3}
- Charter Act 1833 was passed in Bentinck's time.
- Under Bentinck the old policies of war and annexation were abandoned or reduced; he is remembered for administrative and social reforms, not for consolidating the empire. {5}
- Bentinck's social reforms: abolition of Sati, abolition of slavery (Charter Act 1833), suppression of Thugs; also acted against female infanticide. {43}
- Bentinck is matched with social reforms; he also worked on education and freedom of the press.
- Removal of disabilities due to change of religion came in 1850, not under Bentinck.
- On the Court of Directors' wish Bentinck followed neutrality towards Indian states.
- Jaipur misrule led to an attack on the British Resident, yet Bentinck did not interfere.
- He also did not interfere in Jodhpur, Bundi, Kota and Bhopal.
- Exceptions for extreme misrule: Bentinck took over Mysore (1831), Kachhar (1832), Kurg (1834) and Jayantia (1835). {39}
- Bentinck abolished **Sati** in 1829 by Bengal Sati Regulation = Regulation XVII (17) of 1829 of the Bengal Code; declared it a crime. {44,45,46}
- In 1830 the Sati regulation was extended to Madras and Bombay Presidencies.
- Sati means 'holy and virtuous woman'; Hindu marriage is a Sanskar meaning 'relation of spirits'.
- 18th-century Brahmins believed a Sati sent seven generations of her husband to heaven.
- Sati was common among high Brahmins, Kshatriyas and Rajputs.
- Akbar tried to abolish Sati; Marathas abolished it entirely in their state.
- Portuguese tried to stop Sati in Goa; French in Chandranagar.
- Raja Ram Mohan Roy started the campaign against Sati.
- Bentinck appointed **Captain Sleeman** to suppress the Thugs. {41,42}
- Sleeman arrested 1562 thugs between 1826 and 1835; many hanged, the rest exiled for life.
- Organised thuggee ended by 1839 (also printed: 1837).
- Thugs included Hindus and Muslims; worshipped Kali, Durga or Bhavani; offered the cut head at the goddess's feet.
- **Macaulay** laid the foundation of modern (English) education during Bentinck's tenure.
- Macaulay's aim: 'a class of persons Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes, opinions, morals and intellect'.
- Macaulay's proposal approved on 7 March 1835; English to be the language of high-level administration.
- **Charles Metcalfe** removed restrictions on the press; called 'liberator of the Indian press (newspapers)'. {110}
- **Cleveland** set up the 'Hill Assembly Plan' for development of Adivasis (Scheduled Tribes). {47}
- Cleveland put the whole hill range of the Pahariyas under one Hill Assembly of hereditary hill chiefs called sardars.
- Hill Assembly met twice a year; could inflict or rescind capital punishment.
- Sardars had to report all crimes and law-and-order problems of their villages to the authorities.
## Slavery
- Export of slaves from Bengal was stopped by the declaration of 1789. {48}
- Laws on slaves were made in 1811 and 1823.
- Charter Act 1833 asked the Governor to make a law ending slavery as early as possible.
- Slavery abolished by **Act V of 1843** under Governor-General Lord Ellenborough (tenure 1842–44). {81}
- Act of 1843 removed legal enforceability of slave property in civil courts; slavery illegal in most parts (also printed: all parts) of British India.
- 1860: Indian Penal Code made slavery a crime.
- IPC was repealed and Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita came into force in 2024.
## Auckland, Ellenborough and Afghan policy
- First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842): Company vs Afghanistan, under Governor-General Lord Auckland, not Lytton. {90}
- **Sindh** was fully merged into the British Empire in March 1843 under Lord Ellenborough (1842–44), by **Charles Napier**. {63,64,65,112}
- Also printed: the merger of Sindh was the main reason for the First Anglo-Afghan War.
- **Masterly Inactivity** policy in Anglo-Afghan relations: began under Ellenborough, continued till Lord Northbrook.
- Masterly Inactivity is linked with **John Lawrence**; the policy evolved and was fully followed in his time. {83,91}
- Term 'Masterly Inactivity' came from an article by J.W.S. Wiley in the Edinburgh Review.
## Dalhousie
- **Lord Dalhousie** was Governor-General 1848–56; came in 1848; also called 'The Earl of Dalhousie'.
- Dalhousie's real name: James Andrew Broun Ramsay. {62}
- Dalhousie formulated and implemented the **Doctrine of Lapse**. {57,111}
- Doctrine of Lapse: annex a state if its ruler died childless or was manifestly incompetent.
- Order of lapse annexations: Satara → Jaitpur, Sambalpur → Baghat → Udaipur → Jhansi → Nagpur; Karnataka and Jaipur are not in the list. {50,51,55,59,132}
| State annexed by Doctrine of Lapse | Year |
| Satara (first state annexed) | 1848 {60,61} |
| Jaitpur | 1849 |
| Sambalpur (in Orissa) | 1849 |
| Baghat | 1850 {52} |
| Udaipur | 1852 |
| Jhansi | 1853 (also printed 1854) |
| Nagpur | 1854 |
- Punjab was merged in 1849.
- **Awadh** was annexed in February 1856 by Dalhousie on the charge of maladministration against Nawab Wajid Ali Shah. {49,54,56,58}
- 1854: **James Outram** became British Resident of Awadh in place of W.H. Sleeman; he was Resident at annexation. {67}
- Outram reported Awadh's administration corrupt (polluted) and people's condition miserable; this report was the basis of annexation.
- Dalhousie sent the report to London to get permission to merge Awadh.
- Henry Lawrence was British Resident at Lucknow in the 1857 revolt; killed by the revolutionaries.
- Of Sind, Gwalior, Awadh and Satara, only Gwalior was not annexed by the British. {53}
- Dalhousie annexed part of **Sikkim** (Darjeeling and other far areas) on the pretext that its king misbehaved with two English doctors. {66}
- Sikkim was a small state between Nepal and Bhutan.
- Part of Sikkim including Darjeeling was ceded in 1835; more Sikkim territory taken in 1850; Sikkim never fully merged with British India.
- Dalhousie made the first comprehensive railway policy in 1853 and strongly promoted railways.
- Earlier railway proposals: early plan of 1832, Lord Hardinge's plan of 1844.
- First railway line: Bombay to Thane, 1853, in Dalhousie's time. {68,69,70}
- **Great Indian Peninsula Railway** Company started the first rail service (Boribandar, Bombay to Thane, 1853). {71}
- Railway aim 1: carry raw materials from the interior to ports and open Indian markets to British goods.
- Railway aim 2: move the army quickly to remote areas to crush revolts.
- Railway expansion sped up between 1880 and 1900; Curzon's role was administrative: Railway Board, 1905.
- Dalhousie set up a separate **Public Works Department** (first in 1854); before him public works were under the army board. {73}
- A large amount was spent on public works through the PWD.
## Canning and the Crown
- **Hindu Widow Remarriage Act** (Act XV of 1856) was passed under Lord Canning by the efforts of Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar. {74}
- The Act legalised widow remarriage and children born of it.
- Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar was an important figure of the Bengal Renaissance.
- Dhondo Keshav Karve and Vishnu Shastri were propagators of widow remarriage.
- Dhondo Keshav Karve founded a widow ashram in Poona in 1896.
- **Government of India Act 1858** passed by British Parliament on 2 August 1858; power moved from Company to the Crown.
- The 1858 Act legally abolished the Company and created the Secretary of State; it led to the Queen's Proclamation.
- **Queen's Proclamation** was read by Lord Canning on 1 November 1858 at a Durbar in Allahabad (Prayagraj). {75,77}
- Queen's Proclamation was the most important result of the 1857 revolt.
- **Lord Canning** became the first Viceroy of India. {76}
- Queen Victoria became ruler of India in 1858.
- Queen's Proclamation of 1858 restored the right of native rulers to adopt an heir; policy of conquest and annexation was abolished. {78}
- The Proclamation changed policy towards native states so they could be used for rescue in a crisis.
- Titles 'Rai Bahadur' and 'Khan Bahadur' were given to loyal native rulers and zamindars for suppressing the 1857 revolt; evidence of conferral in 1859 and 1860, so not under Ripon, Lytton, Mayo or Dufferin. {134}
- 1861: Lord Canning appointed Alexander Cunningham as Archaeological Surveyor.
## Mayo
- **Lord Mayo** was Viceroy 1869–72.
- First census in British India: 1872, under Lord Mayo; regular (proper) census began in 1881 under Lord Ripon. {93,94}
- Lord Mayo was killed by a convict, Sher Ali Afridi, in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands; first Viceroy killed in office. {95,96}
- Lord Mayo started **financial decentralisation** in 1870 (Mayo's Resolution of 1870). {92,97,98}
- Indian Councils Act 1861 began legislative devolution; Mayo's Resolution was its probable outcome.
- Mayo's Resolution: first step to separate Central and Provincial finances; tried to rectify existing imparity; focused on actual needs of provinces.
- Control of some departments (education, medical services, roads) passed to provincial governments, for efficiency and financial constraints.
- This began local financial management; provinces could use local taxation to balance their budgets.
- Resolution said local interest, supervision and care are needed for funds of education, sanitation, medical relief and local public works.
- It hoped to develop self-government, strengthen municipal institutions and associate Natives and Europeans more in administration.
- Department of Archaeological Survey was set up in 1871 on recommendation of the Duke of Argyll, George John Douglas Campbell, Secretary of State.
- The Earl of Mayo was Governor-General in 1871; Cunningham became Director General of the separate Archaeological Survey.
! A Chhattisgarh question on who set up the Archaeological Survey Department was cancelled, as neither Mayo nor Argyll was an option.
## Lytton
- **Lord Lytton** became Viceroy in 1876 (served 1876–1880), appointed by Conservative Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli.
- Lytton changed Afghan policy: followed the aggressive **'Forward Policy'** in place of 'Masterly Inactivity'. {99,100}
- Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–1880) took place under Lytton.
- Royal Titles Act was passed by British Parliament in 1876.
- Grand **Delhi Durbar** held by Lytton in January 1877; Queen Victoria proclaimed Empress (Sovereign) of India on 1 January 1877. {79,80}
- Queen Victoria received the title Kaiser-i-Hind (Qaiser-i-Hind).
- **Vernacular Press Act** (Native Press Act) 1878 was passed under Lord Lytton, not Curzon; also called the 'Gagging Act'. {127,128}
- Some historians call it the silencing act for its strict censorship.
- The Act let government censor reports and editorials in the vernacular press and stop criticism of British policies.
- Its purpose: control printing and circulation of seditious material causing disaffection against British Government.
- Under the Act Som Prakash, Bharat Mihir, Dhaka Prakash, Sahachar and many others were charged.
- Sir Erskine Perry called it a dangerous step against India's right to expression.
- S.N. Banerjee called it 'lightning from the sky'.
- **Indian Arms Act 1878**: criminal offence for Indians to carry arms without a licence.
- Arms Act did not apply to British, Anglo-Indians, Europeans and certain government personnel.
- Famine Commission set up in 1878 under Sir Richard Strachey (Strachey Commission) in Lytton's time; it gave the Famine Codes.
- The Famine Codes were criticised for handling of the 1876–1878 famine, which caused heavy loss of life.
## Ripon
- 1880: Liberal Party came to power in England under Gladstone, a supporter of freedom; Lord Ripon was appointed Viceroy.
- **Lord Ripon** is seen as the most pro-Indian, most liberal Viceroy.
- Ripon's resolution of 18 May 1882 strengthened local self-government; he is 'Father of Local Self-Government' in India. {103,104,108,109}
- The 1882 resolution is seen as the beginning of modern self-government in India.
- Ripon wanted to develop municipalities; he said political education of the country begins there.
- Local boards set up in rural areas; district sub-division, Tehsil or Taluka boards in every district; municipalities in cities.
- These bodies were given several tasks and sources of income.
- Ripon asked provinces to examine their finances and fix which expenses could pass to local administration.
- **First Factory Act 1881** was made under Lord Ripon, for factories employing 100 or more. {106}
- Act of 1881: no employment of children below 7; children of 7–12 to work at most 9 hours a day; four holidays a month.
- Act of 1881 also required dangerous machinery to be fenced; it focused on safety of child labour.
- Factory Act 1881 did not fix wages or allow trade unions.
- Second Factory Act 1891 gave a weekly holiday.
- **N.M. Lokhande** was a pioneer of the labour movement in India. {107}
- Lokhande improved working conditions of textile mill-hands in the 19th century; took bold initiatives on caste and communal issues.
- **Ilbert Bill** (1883), under Ripon not Lytton: allow Indian judges and magistrates to try British offenders in criminal cases at district level. {101,105}
- Sir C.P. Ilbert, law member of the Viceroy's executive council, presented the Bill in the Legislative Council on 2 February 1883.
- The Bill aimed to end judicial disqualification based on race and equalise European and Indian judges.
- The Bill met intense opposition in Britain and from British settlers in India, who saw it as an attack on their privileges.
## Dufferin
- **Lord Dufferin** was Viceroy 1884–88.
- Third Anglo-Burma War: 1885; Burma added to British India on 1 January 1886 = last major extension of British territory, under Dufferin. {72}
- A.O. Hume founded the Indian National Congress during Dufferin's tenure.
## Curzon
- **Lord Curzon** (1899–1905) had the longest tenure as Viceroy. {102}
| Viceroy | Tenure |
| Lord Mayo | 1869–1872 = 3 years |
| Lord Dufferin | 1884–1888 = 4 years |
| Lord Curzon | 1899–1905 = 7 years |
| Lord Hardinge | 1910–1916 = 6 years |
| Lord Chelmsford | 1916–1921 |
| Lord Reading | 1921–1926 |
| Lord Irwin | 1926–1931 |
- Order of Viceroys: Curzon → Hardinge → Chelmsford → Irwin. {120}
- Archaeological work began in 1784 with the Asiatic Society; Cunningham was Archaeological Surveyor in 1861.
- Under Curzon the **Archaeological Survey of India** was integrated and centralised (1901) and the Director General's post restored. {114,115}
- **John Marshall** was appointed Director General in 1901 and took charge in 1902.
- **Ancient Monuments Preservation Act 1904** was passed under Curzon. {117}
- The 1904 Act: preserve ancient monuments, control traffic in antiquities and excavation, protect and acquire monuments and objects of archaeological, historical or artistic interest.
- About 50,000 pounds were allotted to protect and maintain historical monuments.
- **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** compared Curzon's rule with that of Mughal emperor Aurangzeb. {118}
- **Imperial Cadet Corps** was formed under Curzon; approved by the Secretary of State in November 1901. {119}
- Major D.H. Cameroon was its Commandant; Maharaja Pratap Singh of Idar its honorary Commandant.
- Calcutta Municipal Act 1899 (Mackenzie Act) under Curzon reformed Calcutta Corporation and cut the number of elected Indian members.
- Curzon: "To me, the message is carved in granite, it is hewn out of the rock of doom - that our work is righteous and that it shall endure." {122}
- 1905: Curzon rearranged provincial boundaries; divided Bengal and made the new province 'Eastern Bengal and Assam'. {121}
- Western part kept Bengal, Bihar, Odisha and Chhota Nagpur; he also changed boundaries of Madras and Central Province.
- Partition of Bengal took place in 1905 under Curzon.
- Curzon and Minto used **'Divide and Rule'**, exploiting Hindu-Muslim differences; partition of Bengal 1905 was its symbol. {123}
- Two aims of partition: divide Hindus and Muslims; demoralise newly awakened Bengal.
- Curzon wrote (1900): "Congress is tottering to its fall, and one of my great ambitions while in India is to assist it to a peaceful demise." {124}
- Curzon's time is described as full of missions, commissions and omissions.
- Curzon refused to recognise India as a nation; called nationalist activity "letting off of gas".
## Minto, Hardinge and Reading
- November 1905: Lord Minto replaced Curzon as Viceroy; John Morley became Secretary of State for India.
- **Morley-Minto Reforms** (Government of India Act 1909) gave separate electorates and constituencies to Muslims; Minto promoted them. {125}
- Under **Lord Hardinge II** (Charles Hardinge) the capital was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi. {129}
- Hardinge invited George V and Queen Mary to the third Delhi Durbar (1911).
- At the 1911 Durbar the partition of Bengal was annulled and the transfer of capital announced.
- **Lord Reading** (Rufus Isaacs), Viceroy 1921–1926, was the only Jewish Viceroy of India. {126}
- Reading first sat in the House of Commons; Solicitor General in 1910, then Attorney General.
- Reading got Cabinet rank in 1912; Lord Chief Justice of England in 1913; Viceroy in 1921.
- Chauri-Chaura incident and formation of the Swaraj Party took place in Reading's period.
## Match of offices and persons
- Offices by Act: Governor-General of Fort William in Bengal (Regulating Act 1773), Governor-General of India (Charter Act 1833), Governor-General and Viceroy (1858), Governor-General and Crown Representative (Government of India Act 1935).
- Match given: Regulating Act 1773 → Cornwallis; Act of 1833 → Dalhousie; Act of 1858 → Minto; Act of 1935 → Wavell. {131}
! Warren Hastings was the first Governor-General under the 1773 Act; Cornwallis is matched only because Hastings was not among the choices.
- Full names: Archibald Percival Wavell (Viscount and Earl Wavell); Charles Cornwallis (2nd Earl, first Marquess of Cornwallis).
- Full names: Gilbert John Elliot-Murray-Kynynmond (Earl of Minto); Louis Mountbatten (Earl Mountbatten of Burma); Dalhousie = Earl and Marquess of Dalhousie.
