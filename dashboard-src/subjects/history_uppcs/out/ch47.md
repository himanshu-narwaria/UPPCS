## Indigo Revolt
- The revolt of 1857 gave confidence to fight the British and led to other civil uprisings.
- **Indigo Revolt** (1859–60) took place in Bengal immediately after the revolt of 1857. {1}
- Indigo Revolt began in Govindpur village, Nadia district, West Bengal.
- Leaders: **Digambar Biswas and Bishnu Biswas**, former employees of an indigo planter; peasants refused to sow indigo.
- Cause: British planters forced peasants to take advances and sign fraudulent contracts, to grow indigo on least profitable (unacceptable) terms. {3}
- September 1859: all cultivators revolted; all indigo factories shut down due to the strike.
- By 1860 it spread to Nadia, Pabna, Khulna, Dhaka, Malda, Dinajpur.
- Bengal's intelligentsia supported it through public meetings, rallies, journals and newspaper articles.
- **Harishchandra Mukherjee**, editor of 'Hindu Patriot', played a major role.
- **Dinabandhu Mitra** wrote the play 'Neel Darpan' (1858–1859) on the atrocities of indigo planters on farmers. {2,4,5,6}
- Indigo cultivation declined by the early 20th century: artificial dyes made it unprofitable in the world market. {8}
## Sanyasi Revolt and Bankim Chandra
- **Sanyasi Rebellion** (Bengal): 1763–1800.
- Bankim Chandra Chatterjee's novel **'Anandamath'** is based on the Sanyasi Revolt; he made it famous. {11,12,13,14,15}
- 'Vande Mataram', the national song, is taken from Anandamath; composed by Bankim Chandra Chatterjee. {10}
- 'Vande Mataram' was first sung at the Calcutta session of Congress, 1896, by Rabindranath Tagore.
- Causes of Sanyasi revolt: peasants, landlords and artisans ruined by British rule and its new economic policy.
- A terrible famine came in 1770; sanyasis were humiliated by the ban on pilgrimage.
- Warren Hastings suppressed the Sanyasi uprising after a long campaign.
- Bankim Chandra and Joddu Nath Bose were the first graduates of Calcutta University; 'Sitaram' was Bankim's last novel. {9}
## Wahabi, Kuka, Pagal Panthi and Faraizi movements
- **Wahabi Movement** ran from the 4th to the 7th decade of the 19th century; gave organised, stiff resistance to the British.
- **Syed Ahmad Barelvi** of Rae Bareli was its proponent (leader).
- He was inspired by Abdul Wahab of Arabia; in India the movement drew mainly on Shah Waliullah of Delhi.
- It was a revivalist movement: return to true Islam as the way out of socio-political oppression.
- Wahabi ideas spread to North-West Frontier Province, Bengal, Bihar, Kabul and Central Provinces.
- Syed Ahmad captured Peshawar for a time in 1830 and issued coins in his name there.
- He was killed in the Battle of Balakot (1831).
- After his death **Patna** became the centre of the Wahabi Movement. {18}
- Later Wahabi leaders: Maulvi Qasim, Vilayat Ali, Inayat Ali, Ahmadullah.
- After Patna, branches opened in Hyderabad, Madras, Bengal, U.P. and Bombay.
- Kuka and Wahabi movements began as religious revolts and became political movements to remove British rule.
- **Kuka (Namdhari) Movement** = socio-religious reform movement among Sikhs of Punjab, mid-19th century. {20}
- It was started by Bhagat Jawahar Mal (Sian/Sai Sahib) in the 1840s and Baba Balak Singh.
- They stressed devotion to one God, spiritual purity and revival of Sikh values; aim was to purge Sikhism of superstition.
- **Baba (Guru) Ram Singh** (1816–1885) organised the Namdhari sect and made it a powerful movement. {19}
- Called 'Kuka' because followers shouted ecstatic cries called 'Kuk' in devotion.
- Ram Singh preached monotheism, prayer, meditation (Naam Simran) and strict moral discipline.
- He opposed caste, female infanticide, early marriage and exchange of daughters in marriage.
- He popularised simple, cheap Anand marriages.
- The movement took political overtones against British rule; the British grew suspicious.
- 1872 Kuka uprising against cow slaughter and British authority was severely suppressed; peak clashes 1871–72.
- Ram Singh was exiled in 1872 (Rangoon; state prisoner at Mergui, Burma) and died there in 1885.
- Kuka revolt is dated 1840–72; its main centres were in Punjab.
- **Pagal Panth** was a semi-religious sect founded by Karim/Karam Shah of North Bengal. {22}
- The Pagalpanthi rebellion was a rebellion of the Garo tribe. {21}
- Tribal peasants organised under Karam Shah's son **Tipu Shah** to fight zamindars' oppression.
- Tipu Shah (printed with the year 1852) became leader after his father Karim Shah's death in 1813.
- Under him the sect became a peasant rebellion against zamindars and indirectly against the Company.
- Main cause: heavy oppression and extra taxes on raiyats.
- After division of Sherpur zamindari in 1820, litigation and Company financial pressure raised tax burdens further.
- Tipu Shah said land belongs equally to all as God's creation; an oppressive ruler has no moral right to rule.
- Tipu Shah and his mother Chandi Bibi (Pir-Mata) ran an independent administration in Sherpur for over two years.
- Gar-jaripa, a mud fort north of Sherpur, was his capital.
- He captured Sherpur in 1825, became king, and appointed a judge, a magistrate and a collector.
- Tipu Shah had religious and political aims: to uphold the religion and rights of the peasants of Bengal.
- Tipu Shah was arrested several times between December 1824 and January 1825, tried and imprisoned.
- Janku Pathor and Dubraj Pathor continued the movement for some time.
- **Faraizis** = followers of the Muslim sect founded by Haji Shariatullah of Faridpur, Eastern Bengal. {24}
- Faraizis advocated radical religious, social and political changes.
- Shariatullah's son **Dudu (Dadu) Miyan** led the Faraizi rebellion; organised peasants against landlords and indigo planters. {23}
- Faraizi revolt ran from 1818 to 1862; after Dudu Miyan's death it merged into the Wahabi Movement.
## Peasant movements
- **Pabna** peasant uprising (Bengal): 1873–76, against zamindari oppression, illegal abwabs/forced levies and rent enhancement. {70}
- Order: Sanyasi (1763–1800) → Indigo (1859–60) → Kuka (1871–72) → Pabna (1873–76). {7}
- Pabna rebellion is also dated 1873–85; intense in 1873–76, after that only a legal dispute. {73}
- Bengal Tenancy Act 1885 clarified farmers' land rights, the main demand of the Pabna movement.
- **Deccan (Kisan) Riots**: 1875.
- Immediate cause of the Deccan Riots of 1875: shadow of famine and high interest rates charged by Mahajans. {68}
- Order: Santhal Rebellion (1855–56) → Sepoy Mutiny (1857) → Indigo Revolt (1859–60) → Deccan Riot (1875). {67}
- Movements after 1857: Indigo Revolt, Deccan Agriculturists Riots, Birsa Munda Uprising; Santhal Rebellion was before. {69}
- Barhiyataal resistance in Munger demanded restoration of Bakasht land. {17}
- **Eka Movement** (1921–22) was started by farmers of Hardoi, Behraich, Sitapur (Barabanki) and other districts of Uttar Pradesh (Oudh). {74}
- Eka issue: collection of 50% more tax than the accepted tax; leader Madari Pasi.
- Eka Movement is also dated 1922 in one match list.
- **Moplah Revolt** took place in 1921 in Malabar (Tirurangadi), Kerala. {71,76,77}
- Moplah tenants (sharecroppers) were Muslims; landlords (zamindars) were Hindus.
- Moplah causes: landlord exploitation, resistance to British Raj, Khilafat movement.
- Moplah grievances: no security of tenure, renewal fees, high rent and other landlord oppression.
- Moplah (Malabar) Rebellion was an extended version of the Khilafat movement in Kerala.
- **Tebhaga Movement** (1946–47), Bengal, started under Bangiya Pradeshik Kisan Sabha. {88}
- Tebhaga demand: cut rent rate to 1/3 as recommended by the Floud Commission.
- Tebhaga is printed as a movement 'against the sharecroppers and Jotedars'; most prominent leaders Kampram Singh and Bhuvan Singh.
- **N.M. Lokhande** (Narayanji Meghaji Lokhande, 1848–1897) was the first to organise the labour movement in India. {90}
- Lokhande worked for cotton mill workers in the 19th century and on social and communal issues.
## Revolts of South and West India
- 1805: Wellesley forced the Maharaja of Travancore (Kerala) into Subsidiary Alliance.
- The Maharaja was unhappy and would not pay the subsidy; the British Resident was rude.
- Diwan **Velu Thampi** revolted in Travancore (Kerala), backed by the Nair battalion. {25}
- Ganjam rebellion: 1800–1805 (its leader is given as Dhananjay); Gumsur rebellion: 1808–1837.
- Gumsur revolt was led first by Shrikar Bhanja, later by his son Dhananjay Bhanja II. {26}
- **Vasudev Balwant Phadke** (1845–83) founded Ramosi Krishak Jatha (Ramoshi farmers group) in Maharashtra. {27}
- Phadke, among the earliest revolutionaries, organised the Ramoshi tribes into a fighting force.
- Phadke got a life sentence; died in 1883 from a hunger strike in Kala Pani.
- **Ramoshi** hill tribes lived in the Western Ghats; resented British rule and administration. {28}
- 1822: Ramoshis revolted under Chittar (Chittur) Singh and plundered around Satara.
- Ramoshis revolted again in 1825–26; the area stayed disturbed till 1831–32.
- **Gadakaris** = hereditary fort-servants/garrison men of Maratha forts, mainly in Kolhapur region.
- Gadakaris revolted at Kolhapur in 1844–45. {29}
- Causes: disbanded from fort service; their lands put under the Mamlatdar; harsh land revenue and acquisition policies.
- British army struggled hard to suppress the Gadakari rebellion.
- **Waghera Revolt** in Okha Mandal, Gujarat, was against the British: 1818–1819.
- Gaekwad of Baroda, backed by the British, forced the Waghera chief to take up arms; peace treaty in 1820.
- Another Waghera revolt episode took place in Okhamandal in 1858–59.
- **Bhil** uprising (1818–31) centred in Khandesh (present Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh). {50,81}
- Bhils lived in the hill ranges of Khandesh, upland basin of the Deccan tableland.
- British occupation of Khandesh in 1818 angered the Bhils, who feared outsiders.
- Officials blamed Peshwa Bajirao II and his minister Trimbakji Dengle for inciting the Bhils.
- Real causes: agricultural hardship and fear of the new English government.
- 1819: general Bhil insurrection; small groups ravaged the plains.
- 1825: Bhils revolted again under Sevaram; British suppressed it ruthlessly.
- British used force and conciliation, yet failed to win over the Bhils.
- Some Bhil agitation also took place in Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh in the 19th–20th century.
- Koli tribes rose in the Taranga hills of Mehsana district, Gujarat.
- **Chuar Revolt** (1767–1802) broke out in Bankura and Midnapore, Bengal, not Madhya Pradesh. {80}
- **Rampa** hill area lies north of the Godavari in Andhra Pradesh.
- Rampa tribal revolt was against moneylenders and the Forest Acts; dated 1879 (1879–80).
- Madras Forest Act 1882 curbed tribal movement in forests and stopped 'Podu' (shifting cultivation).
- Rampa Rebellion 1879–80; Pabna 1873–76; Bengal Indigo 1859–60; **Jaintia Rebellion** 1860–63. {75}
- Surji Bhagat and **Govind Guru (Govind Giri)** reformed tribals in the second half of the 19th century.
- Their area: Mewar, Dungarpur, Banswada, Pratapgarh, Sirohi, Pali (Bagar).
- Govind Giri launched the **Lasodia Movement** among Bhils and founded 'Samp Sabha' in 1883 to organise Bhils. {51}
- Govind Guru is also called Lasodhiya.

## Sepoy mutinies before 1857
- Order before 1857: Bengal Sepoy Mutiny (1764) → Vellore Mutiny (1806) → Kutch Rebellion (1816–32) → Kol Revolt (1831–32) → Santhal Revolt (1855–56). {34}
- Bengal Sepoy Mutiny 1764: sepoys (a battalion) of Hector Munro's army joined Mir Qasim at Buxar.
- **Vellore Mutiny** (July 1806) took place under Governor-General Sir George Barlow (1805–1807). {35}
- Vellore sepoys rose against interference in social and religious customs; raised the flag of the Raja of Mysore.
- Barrackpur Mutiny: November 1824; Berhampur Revolt: February 1857. {78}
## Tribal revolts of East and North-East
- Key common factor of 19th-century tribal revolts: complete disruption of the old agrarian order of tribal communities. {63}
- Other reasons: new land revenue and tax on tribal products; foreign missionaries; moneylenders, traders and revenue farmers as middlemen.
- **Khonds** lived in the hills of Orissa and nearby districts, almost independent; also described as stretching from Bengal to Tamil Nadu, covering the central provinces.
- Khonds rebelled from 1846 to 1855 under **Chakra Bisoi**, a young king.
- Khonds rose against the ban on human sacrifice (Mariah), new taxes and entry of zamindars. {30}
- Ghumsar, Kalahandi and other tribals joined; Khonds opposed the Mariah Agency.
- **Kol Revolt** (1831–32) in Chhotanagpur was led by Buddho Bhagat (also Ganga Narayan). {31}
- Kols live in Chhotanagpur: Ranchi, Singhbhum, Hazaribagh, Palamu, Manbhum. {32}
- 1831: new land laws brought outsiders (Hindu, Sikh, Muslim farmers, moneylenders) who exploited the tribes.
- Kol rebels killed about a thousand outsiders; order returned only after large military operations.
- Kols of Singhbhum had independent power under chiefs; resisted the Rajas of Chota Nagpur and Mayurbhanj.
- Kol rebels burnt houses and looted property of settlers; the revolt continued off and on and was finally crushed.
- **Ho (Hoz) Revolt**: 1820–21, in then Santhal subdivision of Bihar (now Singhbhum, Jharkhand). {64}
- Munda and Ho tribes of Chhotanagpur (then Bihar) challenged the British army in 1831; unrest lasted till 1837. {37}
! There was no revolt named 'Chhotanagpur Tribal Revolt'; if the Ho revolt (1820–21) is meant, the year is 1820.
- Chhotanagpur revolts: Chero (1800), Kol (1831–32), Santhal (1855–56), Munda (1899–1900).
- **Bhumij Revolt** (1832–33) was led by Ganga Narain in Jungle Mahal region. {87}
- Bhumij = 'one born from the soil'; it began as an inheritance dispute in Barabhum zamindari (West Bengal).
- Bhumij cause: persecution by the king and exploitative British policies.
- **Ahom Revolt** (1828) was led by Gomdhar Kunwar; so 'Ahom – 1815' is wrong. {45,79}
- **Khasi Rebellion** (1829–33) was led by Teerat Singh.
- **Khairwar** tribal movement (1874) was led by Bhagirath Manjhi. {65}
- **Surendra Sai**, claimant to the throne of Sambalpur, led revolts against the British; surrendered in 1862. {66}
- **Kuki revolt** (1917–19) took place in Manipur and Tripura. {84}
! 'Kuki Rebellion 1860–90' and 'Kuka Revolt 1832–34' are both wrong matches in one question; it should be cancelled or both accepted.
- Kuki revolt began in 1917 against the order to join the Labour Corps for World War I.
- **Tana Bhagat Movement** began in 1914 in Chhotanagpur among the Oraon tribe. {83}
- Tana Bhagat leaders: Oraon saints Jatra Bhagat, Balram Bhagat and Devmenia Bhagat; so it is not linked to 'Nayak'. {82}
- Tana Bhagats opposed taxes imposed by the British.
- **Haipou Jadonang**, Naga leader of Manipur, was the first tribal leader inspired by Mahatma Gandhi. {89}
- Jadonang with Rani Gaidinliu started social and religious reforms and freedom struggle.
- **Thakkar Bapa** (Amritlal Vithaldas Thakkar) first used the word 'Adivasi' for tribal people. {62}
- Thakkar Bapa was a member of the Servants of India Society; worked for untouchables and tribals.
- He toured with Gandhi (Harijan tour, 1933–34); General Secretary of Harijan Sevak Sangh set up by Gandhi.
## Santhal Rebellion
- **Santhal Rebellion (Hool)** took place in 1855–56. {40,44}
- Leaders: four brothers **Sidhu (Sido), Kanhu, Chand and Bhairav**; Sidhu was the chief leader. {38,39,41,42,43}
- Area: Damin-e-Koh, between Bhagalpur and the Rajmahal hills.
- Moneylenders and the British exploited Santhals together; interest of 50 to 500% was charged.
- Santhals rose against the British revenue system and the zamindari system.
- Their main enemy was the 'Dikus' (corrupt government officers), not the 'Whites'/'Sahibs'.
- 30 June 1855: over ten thousand Santhals met at Bhognadih (near Barhait, Sahibganj) and resolved to fight.
- Santhals said the Dikus had betrayed them, forced them into slavery and abused their women; they pledged to fight till independence.
- The win over Major Burrough fuelled the rebellion, which spread like wildfire.
- The Santhal revolt is described as held in Bihar, Jharkhand and Odisha.
- Sidhu said: "God said to me that this country is not of bosses. God will fight from our side."
- Santhals defeated the British force of **Major Burrough** at Pirpainati (near Bhagalpur). {46}
- Gokko, an old headman, was an important leader of Godda; Mahajan Deen Dayal Rai is also a key name; Santhals never held sovereignty over the Ganga valley near Muzaffarpur. {47}
- Brown, Commissioner of Bhagalpur, and Major General Lloyd ruthlessly crushed the revolt.
- Sidhu was arrested and hanged; Kanhu was killed in action; revolt ended in 1856.
- After the revolt a separate district 'Santhal Pargana' was created; transfer of Santhal land to non-Santhals was made illegal. {48}
- This came through the Santhal Pargana Tenancy Act.
- Santhal Pargana was called 'Narikhand' in ancient times. {49}
- Buddhist literature calls it 'Kajangala'; in Mahabharata times it was part of Anga Mahajanapada.
## Birsa Munda
- **Birsa Munda** led the Munda Rebellion or **Ulgulan** ('Great Tumult') of 1899–1900 (also printed 1895–1900). {52,53,55,56}
- Mundas had collective farming; zamindars, contractors and moneylenders attacked it.
- Munda leaders fought about 30 years to save their farming; the earlier agitation was Sardari Larai (also printed: Birsa's movement itself was popularly known as Sardari ladai).
- **Khuntkatti** (Mundari) = customary common land tenure of Mundas as original clearers of the forest; it gave them a preferential rent rate.
- Khuntkatti was eroded by jagirdars and thikedars coming as merchants and moneylenders.
- Revolt began against replacing collective farming with zamindari or individual ownership.
- 1895: Birsa claimed a vision of God; declared himself prophet (Messenger of God) with healing powers.
- Birsa turned the revolt into a religious and political movement.
- Thousands flocked to hear Birsa's 'new word' with its prophecy of an imminent deluge.
- Birsa's main aims: social reform in tribal areas and keeping away from British rule.
- Ulgulan sought the right of indigenous people over resources taken by landlords, moneylenders and colonial rulers.
- The Munda revolt was the most famous revolt of its time; it took place south of Ranchi.
- Birsa is called 'Dharti Aaba' (Father of Earth) or 'Jagat Pita'; born in 1875. {54}
- He said they would fight non-tribals and colour the land red with their blood like a red flag.
- He called for worship of one god, 'Sing Bonga'.
- Birsa asked Mundas to fight superstition, give up animal sacrifice and intoxicants, wear the sacred thread, worship in the Sarna (sacred grove).
- It was a revivalist movement to purge Munda society of foreign elements; used Hindu and Christian idioms.
- Two reasons for the movement: agrarian discontent and the coming of Christianity; it aimed to rebuild tribal society breaking up under colonial rule.
- Ulgulan aimed at an independent Munda Raj, but Birsa did not favour a separate state. {59}
- Birsa's working area was Ranchi (Ranchi/Khunti to Singhbhum, Chakradharpur; one line says Ranchi to Bhagalpur). {60,61}
- Christmas Eve 1899: Mundas shot arrows and tried to burn churches across six police stations in Ranchi and Singhbhum.
- January 1900: police stations targeted; rumour of attack on Ranchi on 8 January; rebels defeated on 9 January.
- Birsa was captured asleep in Jamkopai forest, Chakradharpur, on 3 February 1900. {58}
- Birsa died in prison on 9 June 1900.
- Nearly 350 Mundas were tried; three hanged and 44 transported for life.
- Birsa Munda's revolt took place in Bihar (now Jharkhand).
