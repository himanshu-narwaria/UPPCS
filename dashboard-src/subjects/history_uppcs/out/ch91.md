@ 91 | Constitutional Development of India
## Regulating Act 1773
- The British Parliament passed the **Regulating Act in 1773** to curb rampant corruption and misgovernance. {1,2,3}
- Its aim: put the East India Company's activities in India (territories mainly in Bengal) under the supervision of the British Government.
- Madras and Bombay Presidencies came under the Calcutta Presidency; the Governor-General of Fort William got supervisory powers over them.
- The Act first provided for the post of **Governor-General of Bengal** (the Governor of Bengal became Governor-General). {4}
- **Warren Hastings** was appointed the first Governor-General of Bengal.
- The Governor-General had a Council of four, who could vote but had no veto.
- The Act required the Company's directors to present to the British Government all correspondence and documents on the Company's administration. {24}
- The Regulating Act set up India's first **Supreme Court**, at Fort William, Calcutta, with a Chief Justice and three other (puisne) judges. {5,6}
- The Supreme Court came into being in 1774; **Sir Elijah Impey** was its first Chief Justice. {7}
- Its other judges: Robert Chambers, Le Maistre and John Hyde.
- It was the supreme judiciary over all British subjects, including in the provinces of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
## Pitt's India Act 1784 and Act of 1786
- **Pitt's India Act, 1784** set up the **Board of Control** in Britain to fully regulate the affairs of the East India Company. {25}
- The **Act of 1786** gave the Governor-General (Cornwallis) power to overrule his Council in special cases. {8,9}
- The special cases: matters of peace, defence or well-being of the Indian empire; he could override the Council's majority.
- The 1786 bill was brought to persuade Cornwallis to accept the Governor-Generalship of Bengal; he wanted the powers of both Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief.
## Cornwallis Code 1793
- Cornwallis's judicial reforms took final shape in 1793 in the **Cornwallis Code**, based on the principle of Separation of Powers.
- Influenced by 18th-century French philosophers, he separated revenue administration from the administration of justice.
- The Collector was head of the district revenue department and also had wide judicial and magisterial powers.
- By the 1793 Regulation the Collector lost judicial and magisterial powers because Cornwallis was alarmed at such absolute power in one person. {10}
- Cornwallis's reasoning: a Collector sitting as judge of the Diwani Adalat could not fairly redress wrongs he did as collector or assessor of revenue.
- So neither landlords nor cultivators could see the Collector as an impartial judge in revenue cases.
- A new officer, the **District Judge**, was created to preside over the District Civil Court, with magisterial and police functions.
## Charter Act 1813
- The **Charter Act of 1813** ended the Company's monopoly of India trade, except trade in tea and trade with China. {11,12,13,14}
- For the next 20 years the Company kept control of the revenue of British territorial acquisitions in India.
- The Act asserted the sovereignty of the British Crown over the Company's territories in India.
- It allowed **Christian missionaries** to come to India. {23}
- It made the Company responsible for Indians' education, with an annual grant of **1 lakh rupees** for promoting education. {15}
## Charter Act 1833
- The **Charter Act of 1833** ended all the Company's commercial privileges, including the tea trade and China trade monopoly. {27}
- The Company ceased to be a mercantile body and kept only political/administrative work on behalf of the Crown.
- The charter was renewed on condition that the Company give up trade entirely and let Europeans settle freely in India.
- The **Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India** by this Act. {17}
- All financial, administrative and law-making powers were centralised in the Governor-General-in-Council.
- A **Law Member** was added as the 4th member of the Governor-General's executive council.
- The Law Member was to be British (not an Indian) and not from the Company; he could act only for legislative purposes, without sitting or voting in executive meetings. {16}
- **Lord Macaulay** was the first Law Member.
- In **1834** almost all of present Uttar Pradesh was separated from Bengal Presidency to form the North-Western Provinces under **Agra Presidency**. {28}
- The Company had acquired this area gradually from the last quarter of the 18th century to the mid-19th century.
! The question on abolition of the monopoly on trading of "tea and sugar" was treated as wrong by MPPSC, with bonus marks; had it said "sugar and China" in place of "sugar and tea", Charter Act 1833 would be the answer.
## Charter Act 1853
- The **Charter Act of 1853** opened civil service recruitment to competitive examination, without discrimination (as in the 1833 Act's clause). {19}
- The Court of Directors lost its right of patronage.
- A Committee with Macaulay was formed in 1854 to give effect to recruitment by competition.
- Salaries of Board of Control members, its Secretary and officers were to be fixed by the Crown but paid from Company funds.
- Directors were reduced from 24 to 18, of whom 6 were to be nominated by the Crown.
- The 1853 Act for the first time created a functioning **Legislative Council** in India, with 12 members. {20}
- It was a decisive step in separating the Executive and the Legislature.
## Government of India Act 1858
- The **Government of India Act, 1858** transferred the Government of India from the East India Company to the Crown, which now ruled India directly. {29,30}
- Powers of the Board of Control and Court of Directors passed to the **Secretary of State for India**.
- The Secretary of State was a member of the British Parliament, responsible to it, and assisted by the Council of India.
- The Council of India had 15 members: 8 appointed by the Crown and 7 elected by the Court of Directors.
## Indian Councils Act 1861
- The **Indian Councils Act, 1861** empowered the Governor-General to issue **ordinances** in an emergency; valid for at most 6 months. {31}
- The Act strengthened the Viceroy's authority by replacing corporate functioning with the **portfolio (departmental) system**. {32}
- **Lord Canning** started the portfolio system: each department was given to one council member; this laid the base of ministerial government in India.
- The Viceroy's council was expanded for law-making with additional members: minimum 6, maximum 12.
- They were nominated by the Viceroy for two years; at least half had to be non-officials.
- By the 1861 Act the Viceroy's executive council was given law-making powers.
- Madras and Bombay Presidencies got back their law-making power and could amend existing law, but their laws needed the Governor-General's assent.
- High Courts at **Bombay, Madras and Calcutta** were set up under the **Indian High Courts Act, 1861** (established in 1862). {36}
## Indian Councils Act 1892
- The **Indian Councils Act, 1892** let council members discuss the budget, without the right to vote on it or move amendments. {33}
- Members could ask questions on matters of public interest after six days' notice; no supplementary questions.
- The principle of representation and **indirect election** was first brought in by the 1892 Act. {34}
## Press Acts
- Acts to control the press were passed in **1835, 1867, 1878 and 1908**. {35}
| Year | Act | Detail |
| 1835 | Press Act | For censorship of media |
| 1867 | Press and Registration of Books Act | Not given |
| 1878 | Vernacular Press Act | By Lord Lytton; to control printing and circulation of seditious material |
| 1908 | Press Act of Lord Minto | Government could seize any anti-government publication |
## Indian Councils Act 1909
- The **Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto Reforms)** introduced communal representation: separate electorates for Muslims. {38,39}
- **Lord Minto** is known as the Father of Communal Electorate in India.
- The 1909 Act was the most short-lived of Britain's constitutional experiments in India. {37}
- Ramsay MacDonald called it an incomplete and short-term deal between democracy and bureaucracy.
## Montagu Declaration and the 1919 Act
- The reforms declaration of **20 August 1917** is called the **Montagu Declaration**, after Secretary of State Edwin Montagu. {40}
- The **Montagu-Chelmsford Report** (1918), by Secretary of State Montagu and Viceroy Lord Chelmsford, became the basis of the **Government of India Act, 1919**. {42}
- The Montagu-Chelmsford proposals concerned **constitutional reforms**, to bring in self-governing institutions gradually. {46}
- **David Lloyd George** was Prime Minister of England when the 1919 Act (Montagu-Chelmsford Act, also printed as Indian Councils Act, 1919) was passed; his tenure was 1916 to 1922. {43}
- The 1919 Act is not called the Morley-Minto Reforms Act; it is the Montagu-Chelmsford (Mont-ford) Reforms. {49}
- The Government of India Act 1919 came into force in 1921.
- The 1919 Act classified central and provincial subjects, clearly defining the jurisdiction of the Central and Provincial Governments. {47}
- **Dyarchy** in the provinces was introduced by the Government of India Act, **1919**. {44,48}
- Dyarchy = provincial subjects divided into two categories, Reserved and Transferred; a dual government brought in for responsible government in provinces. {45,51}
- Reserved subjects (Finance, Law and order, Army, Police) stayed with the Governor and his executive council.
- Transferred subjects (Public health, education, agriculture, Local Self-Government) went to ministers responsible to the Provincial Legislature.
- **Sir Lionel Curtis** is known as the father of Dyarchy. {41}
- Under the 1919 Act much authority went to the provinces; governance was generally federal in structure, not unitary, though the Governor-General had major executive powers. {50}
- 50 subjects were given to Provinces: Education, Local Self-Government, Medical Relief, Public Health and Sanitation, Agriculture, Cooperative Societies, Public Works, Excise, Justice, Police, Irrigation and Waterways, Labour, Welfare and Industrial Disputes and others.
- The Central Legislature had 47 subjects, such as Defence, Foreign Affairs, Money and Communication.
- The post of **Indian High Commissioner** was created by the Government of India Act, 1919. {52}
- Part V of the 1919 Act provided for a statutory Commission after 10 years to review the system; the Simon Commission of 1927 came from this.
- Central Legislative Assembly elections under the 1919 Act were held in 1923, 1926, 1930, 1934 and **1945** (the last). {67,68}
- The provincial assembly elections of 1937 were held under the Government of India Act 1935.
## Civil Services
- The **Aitchison Commission** report (1887) opposed holding the Indian Civil Service exam in India and England together.
- The **Montagu-Chelmsford Report** recommended more Indians in administration and a simultaneous ICS exam in India and England. {18}
- It also proposed that at least one-third of posts be held by Indians.
- The ICS exam was held simultaneously in India and England from **1922**. {21}
- The Government of India Act 1919 was the basis for the separate ICS exam in India from 1922; on 1 January 1941 Indians (617) outnumbered Europeans (585) in the ICS. {22}
- The **Lee Commission** (1924) recommended an independent, impartial Public Service Commission; it was set up in **1926**.
- The 1935 Act set up separate Public Service Commissions for central and provincial services; the central one became the **Federal Public Service Commission** (set up 1 April 1937).
- From 1937 the Federal Public Service Commission and the Civil Service Commission (UK) held their exams separately.
- In 1950, after the Constitution, the Federal Public Service Commission was renamed **Union Public Service Commission**.
## Government of India Act 1935
- The Simon Commission Report was the baseline of the Government of India Act, 1935. {55}
- Chain: Nehru Report of the All-Party Conference; Simon Commission Report; three Round Table Conferences (1930-1932); White Paper of 1933; Joint Select Committee of British Parliament; then the Act.
- The Act was passed in August 1935 and received Royal Assent on 2 August 1935.
- It was the longest and most elaborate, most complex British enactment for India: **14 parts, 321 sections and 10 schedules**.
- It provided for an **All-India Federation** of British India and princely states and a Federal Legislature. {53}
- It introduced **Provincial Autonomy**: provinces got more independence in certain administrative areas, though the Governor still held significant powers. {54,75}
- It **abolished dyarchy in the provinces** and introduced dyarchy at the Centre; the reserved/transferred distinction in provinces ended. {57,61}
- Governors could veto legislative action and legislate on their own; communal representation was not abolished. {56}
- It established a **Federal Court**, with provincial autonomy and an All-India Federation at the Centre. {59}
- Other provisions: bicameral central legislature, system of provincial government, Provincial Legislative Assemblies, division of powers between Centre and provinces, direct elections.
- Provincial assembly membership was altered to include more elected Indian representatives.
- The Governor-General's Executive Council was restructured with more Indian members, but he kept ultimate authority; the Act had no provision for its own appraisal. {58}
- Reserved seats under joint electorate were expanded to include Scheduled Castes, Muslims, Sikhs, Europeans, Anglo-Indians and Indian Christians.
- **Residuary powers** in the Federation were given to the **Governor-General**. {71}
- The **India Council** was abolished by the Government of India Act 1935. {72}
- With dyarchy started at the central level, subjects were divided into Reserved and Transferred; provinces got more autonomy.
- The 1935 Act introduced the **"Principle of Constitutional Autocracy"**. {65}
- The 1935 Act is a main source of the Indian Constitution; the present Constitution is very similar to it. {60}
- Its **"Instrument of Instructions"** became the **Directive Principles of State Policy** in the Constitution in 1950; critics called that part holy phrases. {70}
## Federal scheme and the princes
- The real British aim in including Princely States in the Federation was to use the princes to counter-balance the anti-imperialist doctrines of nationalist leaders. {62}
- Princely States had about 25% of India's population.
- Upper House: 260 members, 156 for provinces and 104 for Princely States.
- Lower House: 375 members, 250 for provinces and 125 for Princely States.
- State members were not to be elected.
- The princely states refused to join, alarmed by the rapid spread of the National Movement; the federation was never fully implemented.
- The British failed to overcome the opposition of Congress, Muslim League and the princes to the Federal Scheme.
- The Federal Scheme was withheld except for the Federal Court; the provincial scheme began on **1 April 1937**.
## Reactions to the 1935 Act
- **Jawaharlal Nehru** called the 1935 Act "a machine (car) with strong brakes but no engine". {63,64}
- Congress rejected the 1935 Act at the **Lucknow Session, 1936**, presided over by Jawaharlal Nehru. {66}
- Nehru called the Act (the Governor-General's safeguards and special powers) a **"Charter of Slavery"**. {69}
- Nehru was Prime Minister of India 1947-1964, architect of Panchsheel (Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence) and creator of the Non-Alignment policy.
- **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** said, in the debates on Constitution-making, that he need not beg pardon for the charge that the draft Constitution reproduced a major part of the 1935 Act. {73}
- **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** said in the Constituent Assembly: "Can you show me one free country where there are separate electorates? The British element is gone, but they have left mischief behind." {74}
## Protection of Civil Rights Act
- The **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** is not a landmark of constitutional development under British rule. {76}
- It was enacted and notified on 8 May 1955 as the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955; amended and renamed in 1976.
- It extends to the whole of India and punishes the practice of untouchability.
