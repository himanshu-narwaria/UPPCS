@ 66 | Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (1919)
## Rowlatt Act
- 1917: Government set up a (Sedition) committee under Justice **Sidney Rowlatt** to report on criminal conspiracies linked to revolutionary movements and suggest legal changes.
- Rowlatt Act was based on the recommendations of the 'Sedition Committee'; in the Rowlatt Satyagraha Gandhiji tried to use the Home Rule League. {5}
- **Rowlatt Act** was passed in March 1919 by the Imperial Legislative Council. {3}
- Its purpose: to curb national and revolutionary (sedition) activities. {2}
- It let the Government arrest and imprison suspects without trial or warrant, search any place without warrant, and try some cases without jury (summary procedures). {1,4}
- The Act was called "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal" (no plea, no lawyer, no appeal); Gandhiji, the Press and the Congress called it the 'Black Act' (Black Law).
- Official name: **Anarchical and Revolutionary Crime Act (1919)**, popularly known as the Rowlatt Act. {12}
- Rowlatt Act was passed during the viceroyalty of **Lord Chelmsford** (1916–1921). {6,7}
- Congress and the Indian public opposed the Act because it aimed to limit individual liberty. {8}
- The Act curbed fundamental rights such as freedom of expression, put severe restrictions on the press and strengthened police powers.
- **Swami Shraddhanand** suggested starting the protest against the Act with non-payment of lagaan (no-tax campaign). {11}
## Rowlatt Satyagraha
- **Rowlatt Satyagraha** (1919) = Gandhiji's first bold step (first venture) in all-India politics. {10}
- It was the first all-India struggle against the British government, though largely restricted to cities. {9}
- Against the Rowlatt Act Gandhiji used stages: Rowlatt Satyagraha with hartals/civil disobedience (1919), then non-cooperation and Khilafat movement (began January 1921).
- Champaran Peasant Movement (1917), Ahmedabad Mill Workers Movement (1918) and Kheda Peasant Movement (1918) were limited to specific issues of one section and a small area.
- Rowlatt Satyagraha opposed curbs on press freedom and detention without trial; all sections, religions, castes, age groups and both genders joined.
- National newspapers and political journals made Gandhi a popular figure.
- Gandhi organised a country-wide strike on 6 April 1919 against the Act.
- Demonstrations against the Simon Commission did not coincide with it: the Commission reached India on 3 February 1928, met with strikes and the slogan 'Simon! Go Back'.
## Jallianwala Bagh Massacre
- Enactment of the Rowlatt Act immediately preceded the massacre; its wave of popular indignation led to Jallianwala Bagh. {13,40}
- The massacre happened in the context of the Rowlatt Satyagraha. {15}
- Dr. **Saifuddin Kitchlew** and Dr. **Satyapal**, popular Punjab leaders, were arrested by the Deputy Commissioner of Amritsar on 9 April 1919.
- Sequence: Dr. Satyapal's arrest (9 April 1919) → Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13 April 1919) → Amritsar Congress Session (December 1919). {32}
- At Amritsar the arrest of Gandhiji and of Kitchlew and Satyapal aroused a wave of protests.
- **Jallianwala Bagh (Amritsar) massacre** took place on 13 April 1919, Baisakhi day. {14,16,17}
- The unarmed crowd had gathered to protest against the arrest of Kitchlew and Satyapal and the Government's repressive measures. {19,20}
- Others had come for the annual Baisakhi fair; many villagers from outside the city did not know martial law had been imposed.
- **Brigadier General Reginald Edward Harry Dyer** entered with troops, blocked the exits and fired without warning on the peaceful crowd. {18}
- The park was an enclosed ground with a small exit gate, which was closed; firing lasted about ten minutes and stopped only when ammunition ran out.
- Deaths: about 379 killed and hundreds wounded (estimate cited by UK Parliament); also given as about a thousand killed and many thousands wounded in ten minutes, and as thousands killed.
- Lord Chelmsford was Viceroy at the time of the massacre. {38}
- Other events of Chelmsford's tenure: Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, dyarchy in provinces), Rowlatt Act 1919, start of Non-Cooperation Movement.
- **Montagu** called the Jallianwala Bagh massacre 'Preventive Murder' (Preventive Killing). {39}
- Non-Cooperation Movement started in 1920; Communal Award came in 1932; Simon Commission came in 1928.
## Reactions
- **Rabindranath Tagore** returned his 'Knighthood' (title 'Sir') to the Government in protest against the Jallianwala Bagh massacre, 31 May 1919 (also given as 30 May 1919). {21,22,23,24,25,26,31}
- Tagore gave up the Knighthood because of the massacre, not to join the Non-Cooperation Movement. {27}
- Tagore wrote to the Governor-General: "The time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in their incongruous context of humiliation..."
- **(Chettur) Shankaran Nair** resigned from the Viceroy's Executive Council in protest against the massacre. {28,29,30}
- Gandhiji was given the task of drafting the Congress Inquiry Committee report on the massacre. {37}

## Hunter Committee
- **Hunter Committee** (Disorders Inquiry Committee) was appointed to inquire into the Jallianwala Bagh massacre. {33}
- Head: Lord William Hunter, a Senator of the College of Justice of Scotland; 7 members: 4 British and 3 Indians.
- Indian members: C.H. Sitalwad, Pt. Jagat Narayan and Sultan Ahmad Khan.
- Before the Hunter report, Congress gave its view: Dyer's act was inhuman and martial law in Punjab had no justification.
- Hunter report (1920) did not find the Government guilty: said disorder can become revolt, so martial law was needed and firing justified; called it Dyer's error of judgement.
- The report condemned most of Dyer's decisions but agreed with martial law in Punjab.
- It criticised Gandhi's method of Satyagraha and held him partly responsible for the "deteriorated" law and order.
- Dyer was told to resign as Brigade Commander and sent to England, relieved of his command.
- British newspapers showed Dyer as protector of the Empire; the Lords praised him as 'Lion of British Empire'; he was given the 'Sword of Honour'.
## Udham Singh
- **Udham Singh**, a Punjab revolutionary, shot dead **Sir Michael O'Dwyer** at Caxton Hall, London, on 13 March 1940 to avenge the massacre. {34,35,36}
- O'Dwyer was Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab at the time of the massacre; he had approved Dyer's action and was believed to be its main planner.
- Udham Singh had witnessed the Amritsar events and was himself wounded.
