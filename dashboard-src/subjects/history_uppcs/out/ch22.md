@ 22 | Delhi Sultanate: Administration
## Dynasties and nature of the state
- Slave dynasty 1206–1290 AD; Khalji 1290–1320 AD; Tughluq 1320–1414 AD; Sayyid 1414–1451 AD; Lodi 1451–1526 AD.
- Tughluq rule was the longest of the Sultanate period.
- Most Sultans and Amirs of the Sultanate period were **Turks**. {2}
- The Sultan was head of the central government and chief commander of the army.
- Holders of influential posts were called **Amirs**; they had great effect when the Sultan was unfit, weak or under-age.
- Barani did not consider the Delhi Sultans' state truly Islamic because the majority of the population did not follow Islam. {1}
- Medieval government was a composite structure: a fusion of **Perso-Arabic, Turko-Mongol and Indian** elements. {15}
- **Zawabit** = state laws in the Sultanate's administrative vocabulary. {23}
- **Hadis (Hadith)** = Islamic law: collected reports of the sayings, customs and doings of Muhammad and his companions, and what he enjoined, tolerated or forbade. {24}
- **Khareetadar** = dispatcher of royal decrees. {25}

- **Balban** (1266–1286 A.D.), a main architect of the Sultanate's government and institutions, separated the Diwan-i-Wizarat (Finance) and created the **Diwan-i-Arz**. {7}
- Balban reorganised the Diwan-i-Arz and deployed the army in different parts of the kingdom to put down rebellion.
## Wizarat
- Wizarat = institution recognized by Islamic administration; the Abbasi Caliphs took the idea from Persia.
- Non-Arabic institutions were being repealed; 'Wizarat' was the name used for the council of ministers under the emperor.
- Abbas Fazal-Bin-Ahmad, skilled in administration, was the first Wazir, under Mahmud Ghazni (Ghaznavid administration).
- **Wazir** = prime minister of the state and chief of the revenue/finance department (Diwan-i-Wizarat).
- The Wazir looked after Lagaan, the tax system, donations and military expenditure; he supervised state income, expenditure and other departments.
- The **Tughluq** period was the golden period (heyday) of Indian Muslim Wizarat; the Wazir's post peaked under Firuz Tughluq. {14}
## Army
- Army organization and posts were based on the decimal system.
- Head of a troop of horsemen = **'Sarkhel'**.
- 10 Sarkhels → one Sipahsalar; 10 Sipahsalars → one Amir; 10 Amirs → one Malik; 10 Maliks → one Khan.
## Taxes
- Firuz Tughluq taxed according to Islamic (Quran) law: four taxes — **Kharaj, Jizya, Khums and Zakat**.
- **Khums** = one-fifth share of war booty taken from non-believers, paid to the Caliph or Sultan.
- Kharaj, Ushr and Muktai were taxes on land produce; Khums was not. {16}
- Normally 4/5 of the loot went to the soldiers and 1/5 to the treasury; Alauddin Khalji and Muhammad Tughluq kept 4/5 in the treasury and gave 1/5 to soldiers.
- **'Haqq-i-Sharb'** = irrigation tax, levied by Firuz Tughluq at 10% of the crop yield on the advice of religious scholars. {22}
- Firuz Tughluq abolished 21 grievous taxes.
- **Fawazil** = excess amount paid to the exchequer by the Iqtadars. {26}
## Iqta and land
- **Iltutmish** first introduced the **Iqta system** in the Delhi Sultanate. {17}
- In the Caliph's Islamic empire, land was granted to army officials for limited periods in lieu of regular wages.
- The Iqta system began in the 9th century A.D. to relieve the treasury when low tax revenue and little war booty made salaries hard to pay.
- The Iqta holder (Iqtadar/Muqti) collected revenue and taxes in his area.
- Iqta revenue was **not deposited directly in the Sultan's account**; the Muqti used it for administrative and military expenses and kept troops for the Sultan. {4}
- **Siyasatnama** by Abu Ali Hasan Ibn Ali Tusi (Nizam-ul-Mulk) is the source of information on the early Iqta system.
- The Iqta system was not an indigenous Indian institution.
- Sultanate territories were divided into **Khalisa** and Jagirs (Iqta); Khalisa = land under the state's direct control, Jagir = a revenue assignment given to a Mansabdar/Jagirdar, not under direct state control. {5}

## Provincial and village administration
- The province was divided into Parganas or Kasbas; the Pargana official **Amil** collected revenue. {3}
- At the Pargana level the Mushrif kept accounts and the Khazandar was in charge of the treasury.
- Mir Bakshi headed the military department, nobility, information and intelligence agencies in Mughal administration, not under the Khaljis.
- Ascending order of size: **Pargana → Sarkar → Suba**. {6}
- The empire was divided into Subas (head: Subedar); each Suba into Sarkars (head: Fauzdar); each Sarkar into Parganas or Mahals (head: Shiqdar).
- Villages under a Pargana were called 'Mavada' or 'Deeha'; 'Nagla' were small colonies under a Mavada.
- In Shahjahan's reign a unit called 'Chakla', holding a few Parganas, was formed between Pargana and Sarkar.
- The village was the smallest unit of governance in the Sultanate.
- Main village functionaries: Khut, Muqaddam and Patwari, working closely with the muqti for revenue collection.
- **'Muqaddam or Chaudhari'** = village headman, and as such the highest revenue authority in the village. {19,20}
- **Chaudhary** = highest (supreme) rural official of land revenue; main revenue collector for villages. {21}
- Traditional social structure: Sultan at the top, then his governors, then the village headman (Chaudhary).
- At times of revolt the Sultanate reduced the influence of the Rais and took the Chaudhary's help to collect revenue.
## Coins
- Two main currencies of the Sultanate period: **Jital and Tanka**. {27}
- **Iltutmish** was the first Turk ruler to introduce Arabic coins; he started the silver **Tanka** and the copper Jital. {28,29}
- Tanka and Shashgani were silver coins; Jital was copper. {30}
- Ratio of Tanka to Jital = 1:48.
- Iltutmish was the first to issue regular currency and to declare Delhi the capital.
- The name of the last Khalifa of Baghdad first appeared on coins of **Alauddin Masud Shah** (1242–46 AD). {31}
- Last Khalifa of Baghdad = Al-Mustasim (1242–58 AD).
- The name of Khalifa al-Mustansir (served 1226–1242 A.D.) was on the coins of Iltutmish.
