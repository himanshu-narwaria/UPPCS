## Foreign writers
- Greek author **Herodotus** = 'Father of History'; his book 'Historica/The Histories' describes Indo-Persian relations in the 5th century BCE. {1}
- 'The Natural History' was written by **Pliny the Elder**, a Roman author — not by Ptolemy. {87}
- Correct pairs: Life of Hiuen Tsang – Hui-li; Historial Philippical – Pompeius Trogus; The Histories – Herodotus.
## Grammar works
- Ashtadhyayi – Panini; Mahabhashya – Patanjali; Nirukta – Yaska; Varttika – Katyayan. {2,13,16}
- Panini probably belonged to the 7th century B.C.
- Patanjali (not Panini) was associated with **Pushyamitra Shunga**.
## Gupta period literature
- Sanskrit literature reached its peak (apogee) in the Gupta period; the era is known for both prose and poetry.
- Gupta-era classics: **Amarakosha** by Amarsimha, **Kamasutra** by Vatsyayana, **Meghaduta** by Kalidasa, **Mudrarakshasa** by Vishakhadatta. {5}
- **Mudrarakshasa** = Sanskrit historical drama by **Vishakhadatta**. {3,4}
- Mudrarakshasa was written in the Gupta period but tells how Chandragupta Maurya, helped by Kautilya, overthrew the Nandas.
- Mudrarakshasa also gives a picture of the socio-economic condition under the Mauryas.
- Vishakhadatta = drama (playwright of Gupta dynasty); Varahamihira = astronomy/astrology; Charaka = medicine; Brahmagupta = mathematics. {21,22}
- Brahmagupta belonged to the Gupta dynasty; renowned astronomer and mathematician.
- Chandragupta II loved art and culture; nine gems (**Navratna**) adorned his court.
- **Kalidasa** was the most prominent Ratna of Chandragupta II; Amarsimha, a lexicographer, was also among the Navratna (not linked to Harshavardhana). {48}
- Amarkosha was written by Amarsimha, not Varahamihira; Varahamihira wrote **Panchasiddhantika, Brihat Samhita and Brihat Jataka**. {25}
- **Panchasiddhantika** of Varahamihira is based on Greek, Roman, Egyptian and Indian astronomy. {26}
- **Brihat Samhita** of Varahamihira = encyclopedic work of the Gupta period. {45}
## Kalidasa
- Kalidasa = Sanskrit poet and dramatist; probably the greatest Indian writer of any epoch.
- Seven genuine works of Kalidasa: Abhijnanashakuntalam, Vikramorvashiyam, Malavikagnimitram, Raghuvamsham, Kumarasambhavam, Meghadutam, Ritusamharam. {53}
- **Kumarasambhavam** (epic poem) was composed by Kalidasa. {8}
- Meghaduta = a lyric poem; Raghuvansham = an epic poem (both by Kalidasa).
- **Janaki-haranam** was written by **Kumaradasa**, not Kalidasa. {9}
- **Shringara Shatakam** is part of 'Shataka-traya', generally attributed to **Bhartrihari**, not Kalidasa. {10}
- **Uttararamacharitam** = Sanskrit play in seven acts on the life of Lord Rama, written by **Bhavabhuti**, not Kalidasa. {11}
- **Mrichchhakatikam** was composed by **Sudraka**, not Kalidasa. {54}
- **Malavikagnimitram** = first play of Kalidasa; 5 acts; love story of King **Agnimitra** of Vidisha (Shunga dynasty, son of the dynasty's founder) and Malavika. {55,56}
- It is often called 'Kalidasa Malavikagnimitram' in honour of Kalidasa; the Shungas are presumed to have ruled Magadha.

## Other Sanskrit poets and plays
- **Daskumarcharitam** was written by **Dandin (Dandi)**; it describes the adventures of 10 princes. {6,7}
- **Kiratarjuniyam** = epic poem by **Bharavi**, considered the most powerful poem in Sanskrit. {18,19}
- Kiratarjuniyam describes the combat between Arjuna and Lord Shiva (in the guise of a kirata = mountain-dwelling hunter) at Indrakeeladri Hills.
- Works with themes from the Mahabharata: Naishadhiyacharita, Kiratarjuniyam, Sisupalavadha (not Dasakumaracharita). {20}
- Naishadhiyacharita = story of Nala and Damayanti from the Mahabharata, written by **Sriharsha**.
- **Magha** wrote Sisupalavadha: Sisupala insulted Lord Krishna, who beheaded him in the ensuing duel.
- **Svapnavasavadattam** = Sanskrit play written by **Bhasa**. {57}
- The Sanskrit play **Madhyama-vyayoga** is attributed to Bhasa. {58}
- Kavyalankara was written by Bhamaha.
- **Natyashastra** = the 'Bible' of North Indian classical music; a Sanskrit treatise on the performing arts. {12}
- Natyashastra was written by **Bharat Muni**; it covers drama, dance and music, all aspects of classical Sanskrit theatre. {84}
- **Kadambari** was composed by **Banabhatta**, not Subandhu. {28}
- Correct pairs: Kalidasa – Raghuvansa; Bhasa – Svapna Vasavadattam; Harsha – Ratnavali; Harshacharita – Banabhatta.
- **Karpuramanjari** was written by **Rajshekhara**, poet laureate of the Gurjar-Pratiharas — not by Harsha. {52}
- **Bhavabhuti, Hastimalla and Kshemeshvara** were famous playwrights. {46}
- Bhavabhuti = major dramatist of the later Sanskrit dramatic period; court poet of King Yashovarman of Kannauj (8th century CE).
- Bhavabhuti's works: Mahaviracharitam (early life of Rama), Malatimadhava (romance of Malati and Madhava), Uttararamacharitam.
- Hastimalla = Kannada poet and playwright during the reign of the Hoysalas.
- Kshemeshvara (990–c. 1070 CE) = 11th-century Sanskrit poet from Kashmir; an important work attributed to him: Dasavatar Charita.
- **Mattavilasa Prahasana** was written by Pallava ruler and scholar **Mahendravarman I**; it describes social and religious life. {78}
- The drama **'Lalit Vigraharaj'** was authored by **Somadeva**. {79}
- **Geet Govind** = epic poem in Sanskrit by **Jayadeva**, 12th century; organized into twelve chapters. {59,62}
- Jayadeva was the royal poet in the court of King **Lakshman Sen** of Bengal. {60}

## Harsha and Ashvaghosha
- **Harshavardhana** is supposed to be the author of three plays: **Ratnavali, Priyadarshika and Nagananda** (Harshacharita is not his). {42,43,44}
- Harsha was a great conqueror, religious-minded and generous king, and a great patron of education and learning.
- Historian Rama Shankar Tripathi: "Harsha was a patron of letters. He appears to have wielded the pen with no less dexterity and effect than the sword".
- The plays are said to be composed by a king named Harshadeva; he is identified with Harsha of Kannauj, as no other sovereign of this name fits.
- **Ashvaghosha**, a noted poet, was instrumental in the propagation of Buddhism; he wrote the famous court poem **Saundarananda** Kavya. {41}
- Saundarananda was discovered and edited by Pandit Haraprasada Shastri.
- **Buddhacharita** = poetic narrative of Buddha's life by Sanskrit poet **Ashvaghosha**, not Vasubandhu. {47}
- Correct pairs: Mrichchhakatikam – Sudraka; Mudrarakshasa – Vishakhadatta; Saundarananda – Asvaghosha.
## Kashmir chronicle: Rajatarangini
- **Kalhana**, a Kashmiri Brahmin, wrote **Rajatarangini** ('River of Kings'), which gives the history of Kashmir. {14,15}
- Rajatarangini = metrical historical chronicle of Kashmir, particularly its kings; written in Sanskrit in the 12th century.
- **Jai Singh (Jaisimha, 1128–1149)** of Kashmir was Kalhana's contemporary ruler; Kalhana completed Rajatarangini in his reign. {38}
- Rajatarangini has **8 Tarangas** and 7826 verses; the first three Tarangas narrate the conventional ancient history of Kashmir. {39}
- **Jonaraja** continued the narration up to the reign of Sultan Zainul Abedin and also called it Rajatarangini (Rajavali); after his death his pupil **Srivara** continued it. {40}
- **Gaudavaho** = Prakrit epic poem by **Vakpati**, poet in the court of Yasovarman, king of Kanauj; it describes the digvijaya of Yasovarman. {17}
## Buddhist and Jain texts
- **Milindapanha** = Buddhist text in **Pali**; a dialogue between Indian monk **Nagasena** and Indo-Greek king **Milinda (Menander)**. {29,30,31,32,33}
- Milindapanha, written by Buddhist monk Nagasena, explains the eight different phases of agricultural works. {34}
- Jain texts: **Parishishtaparvan** (by Hemchandra) and **Trishashtilakshana Mahapurana** (by Chavund Rai/Chamund Rai). {36}
- Nettipakarana and Avadanashataka are Buddhist texts.

## Law texts
- **Manusmriti** (Manav Dharmashastra) is mainly related to the social system; one of the 18 major Smritis. {81}
- Manusmriti = earliest metrical work on Brahminical Dharma in Hinduism.
- Its actual human author is presumed to have used the eponym 'Manu', linking the text with the first human being and first king of Indian tradition.
- **Manu** is considered the originator of law in India (first/great law-giver). {82,83}
- **Mitakshara** = a vivarti (commentary) on the Yajnavalkya Smriti by Vijnanesvara; **Dayabhaga** = Hindu law treatise by Jimutavahana; both deal with laws of inheritance.
- Mitakshara: sons can claim property during the father's lifetime; Dayabhaga: only after the father's death. {72}
- Mitakshara deals with property held by both male and female family members; Dayabhaga only with property held by male members.
- Wrong statement: Mitakshara was civil law for upper castes and Dayabhaga for lower castes.
## Medicine, mathematics, astronomy and music
- **Charaka Samhita** = comprehensive text on ancient Indian medicine (Ayurveda), by Acharya Charaka, the father of Indian medicine. {23}
- Charaka Samhita = oldest surviving Ayurveda text; divided into **120 chapters in eight sections**. {24}
- Charaka = famous court physician of Kanishka; believed to have lived in the 1st–2nd century CE (exact dates uncertain).
- Ashtanga Samgraha – medical; Das rupaka – play; Lilavati – maths; Mahabhasya – grammar. {63}
- **Bhaskaracharya (Bhaskara II)**, 12th century, made significant contributions to mathematics and astronomy; he has six well-known works.
- His book **Siddhantasiromani** has four parts: Lilavati, Bijaganita (Algebra), Ganitadhyay, Goladhyay; so he is specially known for Algebra. {73}
- **Lilavati**, a treatise on mathematics, was written by mathematician Bhaskaracharya (Bhaskar-II). {74,75}
- **Aryabhatta** = renowned mathematician and astronomer of ancient India; major work **Aryabhatiya**. {76}
- Aryabhatiya deals with astronomy, spherical trigonometry, arithmetic, algebra and plane trigonometry.
- Aryabhatta was the pioneer to infer that the earth is spherical and rotates on its axis around the sun; also considered the father of Trigonometry.
- Aryabhatta discovered the **decimal place** value. {77}
- **'Zero'** was discovered by an anonymous Indian in the second century B.C.; Arabs learned it from India and spread it to Europe. {85,86}
- Arabs are found to have first used zero in 873 A.D.
- **Veena** = most ancient instrument among veena, sitar, sarod and tabla; the other three were introduced in the medieval era. {88}
