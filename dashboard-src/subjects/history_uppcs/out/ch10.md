## Invaders and Indo-Greeks
- Order of invaders in ancient India: **Greeks** (326 BC, Alexander) → **Sakas** (Scythians, 1st century BC) → **Kushanas** (1st century AD). {10,53}
- **Darius I** (522–486 BC) = first Persian ruler to invade/capture some parts of India. {54}
- Herodotus: Darius conquered the lands around the Indus river, up to the desert of Rajputana, and parts of Punjab.
- **Strato II**, Indo-Greek ruler (25 BC–10 AD), issued lead coins. {1}
- **Agathocles**, Greco-Bactrian king (about 190–180 BC), issued silver coins showing both Sankarshana (Balram) and Vasudeva. {2}
- Agathocles held Paropamisadae, a satrapy between Bactria and India; known mostly from his many coins.
- Agathocles = first Greco-Bactrian ruler to put bilingual legends on coins, mainly Greek and Brahmi.
- **Milinda (Menander)** = Indo-Greek ruler of north-western India; not a contemporary of Bimbisara, Buddha and Prasenjit. {3}
- Bimbisara (544–492 BC, Haryanka) = real founder of the Magadha empire; Prasenjit ruled Koshala; both were contemporaries of Buddha.
- Sixteen coins of King Menander were found at **Bairat**. {42}

## Rudradaman and the Saka Kshatrapas
- **Junagadh inscription of Rudradaman** (130–150 AD; dated about 150 AD) was found at Girnar hills, Gujarat; it tells Rudradaman I's achievements. {5}
- It is in Brahmi script; an early inscription in perfect Sanskrit; the earliest specimen of the **'Kavya'** style. {4}
- It credits Rudradaman I with supporting cultural arts and Sanskrit literature.
- **Rudradaman I** repaired the embankments of **Sudarshan Lake** (artificial reservoir) without using forced labour. {6,7}
- Sudarshan Lake was built under Chandragupta Maurya by his western province governor **Pushyagupta**, to check floods.
- **Karddamaka Kshatrapa** coins: silver (frequent) and potin; Chastana issued silver and copper; **copper** coins were very rare. {8}
- Gold : silver coin ratio in the Saka-Kshatrapa period = **1 : 35**. {45}
## Kushana coins
- Indo-Greek kings introduced (and made popular) gold coins in northern and north-western India.
- **Kushanas** circulated gold and copper coins on the widest scale; they issued gold coins regularly and the most copper coins. {11,12}
- **Vima Kadphises** = first Kushana king to issue gold coins; he introduced gold coins for regular use, on a large scale. {14,15,16}
- Kujula Kadphises circulated copper coins.
- Vima Kadphises (Kadphises II), father of Kanishka I, took the title **'Sarvalokeshvara'** (Lord of the Whole World). {17}
- **Buddha** is shown on the coins of Kushana ruler Kanishka. {13}
- **Yaudheya** copper coins (several varieties) show standing six-headed god **Kartikeya** with a peacock on the right; found in the Kushana ruler's time. {18}
- Evidence of the Yaudheyas' coins comes from the Puranas and the Ashtadhyayi.

## Kanishka: dates and eras
- Kanishka's **Sarnath Buddha image inscription** is dated **81 AD**, three years after his coronation. {19}
- Kanishka's coronation date is disputed; two international conventions in London (1913 and 1960); the second agreed on **78 AD**. {20}
- **Saka Samvat** began in **78 AD**; so December 2009 = Saka 1931 (2009 − 78). {21,25}
- Jain texts: Sakas beat Vikramaditya's successor in 135 Vikrama Samvat and began Saka Samvat to mark it: 135 − 57 = 78 AD.
- Most historians hold that Kushana ruler Kanishka started the Saka Samvat.
- **Vikrama Samvat** began in **57 BC**; its two other names are Krit and Malav. {24}
- Jain texts put 470 years between Mahavira's salvation (527 BC) and Vikrama Samvat: 527 − 470 = 57 BC.
- Gregorian calendar = internationally used civil calendar; Vikrama Samvat = Gregorian year + 57; Saka Samvat = Gregorian year − 78.
- Difference between Vikrama Samvat and Saka Samvat = **135 years** (e.g. 2019 → Vikrama 2076, Saka 1941). {22}
- Saka Samvat is adopted as India's national calendar; **Chaitra** is its first month.
- Chaitra 1 = **22 March** normally, **21 March** in a leap year; national calendar dates match the Gregorian calendar. {23}
## Kanishka: court, empire, society
- **Ashvaghosha** = poet laureate and contemporary of Kanishka; works: Saundaranand, Buddhacharita, Sariputraprakaran. {26,27}
- **Vasumitra**, renowned Buddhist scholar and Kanishka's contemporary, headed the Fourth Buddhist Council.
- Kalidas belonged to the Gupta period; Kamban was not Kanishka's contemporary.
- Scholars of Kanishka's court: Parsva, Vasumitra, Ashvaghosha, Nagarjuna, Charaka, Mathara; Vishakhadatta was not among them. {29}
- **Charaka** = court physician of Kanishka.
- **Patanjali** belonged to the Shunga period (not Kanishka's court); author of the Mahabhashya. {28}
- **Rabatak inscription** of Kanishka names four cities: Saket, Kaushambi, Pataliputra, Sri Champa; Shravasti is not named. {30}
- Rabatak inscription: found in 1993 at Rabatak near Surkh Kotal, Afghanistan; Greek script, Bactrian language.
- **Taxila University** alumni: Kautilya, Chandragupta Maurya, Ayurvedacharya Charaka, King Prasenjit of Koshala, Ayurvedacharya Jivaka (of Bimbisara), Vasubandhu. {31}
- Taxila University was a meeting point where students of different countries exchanged knowledge.
- The **Kushana** empire spread outside India: northern Turpan (China) and Kashmir to the Vindhya hills (south); northern Afghanistan (west) to eastern U.P. and Bihar; the Maurya empire also went beyond India. {40}
- Maurya empire = first pan-India empire; at its height it covered parts of modern Iran and almost the whole subcontinent except the southern peninsula.
- Chinese texts (73–94 AD): Kanishka (Yue-tche) asked to marry a Han princess and was refused; he marched in 86 AD with 70,000 men but Chinese general **Pan Chao**'s smaller force defeated him. {39}
- Kushanas traded through the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea, but no evidence describes a Kushana naval force. {69}
- Proof of ports and trade centres: the travelogue 'Periplus of the Erythraean Sea' and the Arikamedu excavations.
- **'Periplus of the Erythraean Sea'** (Periplus Maris Erythraei): anonymous work of about mid-1st century CE by a Greek-speaking merchant — not by Ctesias, Pliny, Ptolemy or Strabo. {78}
- It was translated by Wilfred H. Scoff as 'The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea: Travel and Trade in the Indian Ocean by a Merchant of the First Century'.
- **Child marriage** began in the Kushana period (1st century); age 8–10 years was thought best for a girl's marriage. {41}
- Ending of 'Upanayana' for females plus child marriage brought women to the lowest stage of society.
## Gandhara art
- **Gandhara School of Art** developed in the Kushana period, with encouragement and protection from Kanishka. {43,44}
- Two major sculpture schools of the Kushana period with distinct styles: Gandhara and Mathura.
- Gandhara style = synthesis of Indian and Greek art; also called Greco-Buddhist, **Indo-Greek** or Greco-Roman art; its subject was only Buddha. {49,50,51}
- Gandhara Buddhist art grew from the merger of Greek, Syrian, Persian and Indian influences; flourished in the Saka and Kushan era.
- **Gandhara sculptures** always used **green schist** as the medium; they were influenced by Greek sculpture. {52}
- **Bamiyan** (Afghanistan) was famous for Buddha statues cut into its hills; the Taliban destroyed them. {48}

## Shunga and Kanva dynasties
- Order of rule: **Maurya** (about 321–185 BCE) → **Shunga** (about 185–73 BCE) → **Kanva** (about 73–28 BCE) → **Kushan** (1st–3rd century CE). {32}
- **Pushyamitra Shunga**, commander of the last Maurya Brihadratha, killed him in 185 BC and founded the Shunga dynasty.
- The **Ayodhya inscription** records two Ashwamedha Yajnas performed by Pushyamitra Shunga. {33}
- Ancestors of the Shungas belonged to **Ujjain**. {34}
- Last Shunga ruler Devabhuti was killed by his minister (Amatya) Vasudeva Kanva; the **Kanvas** then replaced the Shungas. {35}
- Kanvas were Brahmins, like the Shungas.
## Satavahanas
- **Satavahanas** = most powerful dynasty in the South after the Mauryas. {37}
- Puranas: **Simuka** (Sindhuk / Shiprak / Sisuk) founded the Satavahana dynasty after killing Kanva king Susharman. {38}
- Satavahana is older than the Chalukyas, Pallavas and Rashtrakutas; founded by Simuka near the 2nd century BC (printed elsewhere as 1st century BC). {55}
- Chronological order: Satavahanas (Simuka) → Vakatakas (Vindhyashakti, 3rd century AD) → Chalukyas of Gujarat (Mulraj I, 10th century AD). {62}
- **Matsya Purana** has the longest list of Andhra-Satavahana kings: 30 rulers. {56}
- Real capital of the Satavahanas = **Paithan (Pratishthan)**. {58}
- Initial capital of the Satavahanas is believed to be **Amravati**. {57}
- Krishna, brother of Simuka, succeeded him.
- **Satakarni I**, son and successor of Krishna, was first to hold the title 'Satakarni'; the **Nanaghat** inscriptions tell of his rule.
- **Gautamiputra Satakarni**, patron of Brahmanism = protector of the **Varna system**: Nashik inscription says he allowed no inter-caste marriage among the four Varnas and stopped crossbreeding. {36}
- Nashik inscription of Gautami Balashri calls her son Gautamiputra Satakarni **'Ekabrahmana'** (unrivalled Brahmana), hinting at Brahmin origin. {59}
- Satavahana period: Sanskrit, Prakrit and other folk languages developed, because the kings promoted them for literary writing. {63}
- Satavahana kings nurtured Prakrit more than Sanskrit; the public aspect of the arts got more encouragement in their time. {64}
- Political inscriptions show that Sanskrit was rarely used; Satavahana architecture and sculpture are associated with Buddhism.
- **Amaravati Stupa** (Andhra Pradesh) has a platform with **Ayaka (Aryak) pillars**. {66}
- After Satavahana power weakened, the **Vakatakas** rose in Dakshinapath; founder Vindhyashakti; Pravarasena was Vindhyashakti's son (not the reverse). {65}
- Puranas call the **Ikshvaku** rulers **'Shriparvatiya'**. {68}
- Ikshvakus were lords under the Satavahanas and became independent after their decline; founder Shrisantmula.

## Kharavela of Kalinga
- **Kharavela**, king of Kalinga (present Odisha), is known chiefly from the **Hathigumpha inscription**, which he had inscribed. {70,75}
- Hathigumpha inscription is undated and partly damaged; it records 13 years of Kharavela's reign; a few minor inscriptions are also in the Udayagiri caves.
- Kharavela belonged to the **Chedi** dynasty, also called the **Mahameghavahana** dynasty; he was its best-known king. {72,74}
- Kharavela was a follower and patron of **Jainism**. {71,73}
- **Udayagiri and Khandagiri** hills (Bhubaneswar, Odisha) have both natural and artificial rock-cut caves: Udayagiri 18, Khandagiri 15. {76}
- The Hathigumpha inscription is in the Udayagiri caves (near Bhubaneswar), not Khandagiri.

## Trade and other facts
- Ancient India had many river ports and large entrepots (warehouses) of goods and traffic. {79}
- Indus Valley: port Lothal and warehouses at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro show large trading hubs.
- In historical times the Ganga, Indus and Kaveri were important trade and transport routes.
- Ports **Muziris** and **Sopara** were famous for trade with Rome and Southeast Asia.
- Eastern Roman ruler **Justinian** is known for Roman **law**: he fully revised it; the Corpus Juris Civilis is still the basis of civil law in many states. {77}
