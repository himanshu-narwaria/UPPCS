@ 11 | Gupta and Post-Gupta Period
## Gupta dynasty: start and early rulers
- **Gupta dynasty** ruled 275-550 AD; founded by King **Srigupta** around 275 AD; among exam options "319-500 AD" is the most accurate. {1}
- **Chandragupta I** (319-335 AD) = first powerful Gupta ruler; took the title **'Maharajadhiraja'** (unlike his predecessors) to show his importance.
- Gupta dynasty was founded by Srigupta, but the reign of Chandragupta I is taken as the beginning of the Gupta empire.
- Chandragupta I was the first Gupta ruler to issue coins; no evidence of coins of the early rulers Srigupta and Ghatotkacha; Samudragupta's many coin types came after him. {61}

| Chandragupta (I) | Kumaradevi {40} |

| Chandragupta II | Kubernaga |
| Kumaragupta I | Anantdevi |
- After Samudragupta his son **Ramagupta** became ruler but could not oppose the Saka invasion.
- Chandragupta II killed his (elder) brother Ramagupta, married his wife **Dhruvadevi** and won against the Sakas. {18}
## Samudragupta
- **Samudragupta** ruled 335-375 AD; father = Chandragupta I; mother = Kumaradevi, a Lichchhavi princess.
- Samudragupta is called **'Lichchhavi-Dauhitra'** (son of the daughter / grandson of the Lichchhavis) because of his mother Kumaradevi. {13}
- Historian **V. A. Smith** in 'The Early History of India' called Samudragupta the **'Napoleon of India'** for his great military conquests. {3,4}
- The Nalanda and Gaya copper plate inscriptions of Samudragupta give him the title **'Param Bhagavata'** (first Gupta ruler with this title). {6,7}
! Param Bhagavata question: UPPSC gave Chandragupta II as the answer, while UGC accepted Samudragupta.
- Tej Ram Sharma's book 'A Political History of the Imperial Guptas' describes the Ashvamedha Yajna done by Samudragupta.
- After the Ashvamedha, Samudragupta took the title **'Prithivyama Pratiratha'** = a man with no rival on earth (first brave man on Earth: 'Prithivyah Pratham Veer'). {14}
! UPPSC excluded the 'Prithivyah Pratham Veer' question (U.P.P.C.S. Pre 2016) from evaluation.
- **Meghavarman**, Buddhist king of Sri Lanka (Ceylon), sent a missionary to Samudragupta; Samudragupta allowed him to build a monastery/Buddhist temple at Bodh Gaya (Chinese source). {84}
- Samudragupta is linked with the **Eran inscription**.
- Samudragupta issued six types of gold coins: Garuda, Archer, Parashu (battle-axe), Ashvamedha, Vyaghra-Parakramah (tiger), Veena type.
## Prayag Prashasti (Allahabad pillar)
- The **Allahabad (Prayag) pillar inscription**, engraved on an Ashoka pillar at Allahabad (Prayagraj), is associated with Samudragupta and tells of his reign and military campaigns. {8,9,10}
- It is also called **'Prayag Prashasti'**; most important historical document of the classical Gupta age; written in excellent Sanskrit.
- **Harisena**, court poet, minister and secretary of Samudragupta, wrote the Prayag Prashasti; he was not linked with Harsha. {15,180}
- The Prashasti is a panegyric (eulogy/tribute) praising Samudragupta; lists his political and military achievements, including expeditions to the south.
- The pillar was built by Ashoka, originally stood at **Kaushambi**; later shifted to Allahabad by **Akbar**.
- Names of **Jahangir** and **Birbal** are also mentioned on the pillar.
- The pillar has 4 inscriptions: (1) Ashokan inscription in Brahmi; (2) Queen's edict on the charity of Ashoka's wife **Karuvaki**; (3) Samudragupta's inscription by Harisena in Sanskrit language, Brahmi script; (4) **Jahangir's inscription in Persian**. {12}
- Prayag Prashasti lists Koshala among the kingdoms and kings of **'Dakshinapath'**; king of Koshala (Kosal) = **Mahendra**. {11}

## Chandragupta II Vikramaditya
- **Chandragupta II 'Vikramaditya'** ruled 375-415 AD.
- Another name of Chandragupta II = **Devagupta** (evidence: Sanchi and Vakataka inscriptions); also called **Devaraja** (Sanchi inscription) and **Devashri**. {5}
- Chandragupta II is called **'Saka Conqueror'**: he ended Saka power by defeating the last Saka king of western India, **Rudrasimha III**, in the first decade of the 5th century; then took the title **'Sakari'** (destroyer of the Sakas). {26}
- Victory over the mighty Sakas was his foremost success; annexing the Saka kingdom (Gujarat and part of Malwa) strengthened the empire and brought it in direct touch with western sea ports.
- After the Saka victory Chandragupta II issued (dated) **silver coins** to mark his win over the Saka Kshatrapas; first Gupta ruler to issue silver coins. {27,29}
- His silver coins are the strongest evidence of the Saka victory; they usually weigh about 30 to 33 grains. {28}
- In the Gupta period Chandragupta II made **Ujjain** his second capital, so Ujjain's importance grew.
- Chandragupta II is linked with the **Udayagiri cave inscription**; no record of Prabhavati Gupta is found there. {35}

## Mehrauli iron pillar
- **Mehrauli Iron Pillar inscription** (from Mehrauli, Delhi) now stands in the centre of the courtyard of the **Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque** near Qutub Minar; it describes king **'Chandra'**, taken to be Chandragupta II. {17}
- By the Mehrauli inscription king Chandra was devoted to Vishnu and set up a **Vishnu-dhvaja** on a hill called **Vishnupada**.
- The pillar is believed to have stood at **Vishnugiri near Udaigiri Hills** in Chandragupta II's time, around 410 AD.
- Chandragupta II, a devotee of Vishnu, built the iron pillar in honour of Vishnu; **Garuda**, Vishnu's emblem-bearer, was engraved on its top.
## Fa-Hien
- **Fa-Hien (Faxian)**, Chinese Buddhist monk and pilgrim, came to India in the reign of Chandragupta II (not Harsha); he did not attend Kanishka's fourth Buddhist Council. {30,31,170}
- Fa-Hien travelled by the Silk Route through Central Asia to India and Sri Lanka, about 399-412 CE; stayed in India about ten years, early 5th century.
- Fa-Hien was in India only for 10 years (402-412 AD); his whole trip from China was 399-414 AD.
- Fa-Hien did not meet the emperor or the court; his account focuses on Buddhist life and monasteries.
- Fa-Hien mentions a hospital at **Pataliputra** (the capital), endowed by benevolent citizens; poor and helpless patients got doctors, food and medicine there. {54}
- According to Fa-Hien, people used **cowrie shells** in routine trade; (another passage) people used Dinara for daily business.
## Vakatakas and Prabhavati Gupta
- **Vakataka dynasty** ruled central and southern India from the 3rd century CE to the end of 5th / beginning of 6th century CE.
- Founder of the Vakatakas = **Vindhyashakti**; his successor (son) **Pravarasena I** is called the real founder; in his time the kingdom reached from Bundelkhand (north) to the Deccan incl. modern Hyderabad (south); Puranas call him **Pravira**. {34}
- **Pravarasena I** (275-335 AD) was the first Vakataka ruler to call himself **Samrat**; he performed **four Ashvamedha** Yajnas and many Vedic sacrifices. {2}
- **Pravarasena II** of the same dynasty loved literature; he wrote **'Setubandha'**.
- **Prabhavati Gupta**, daughter of Chandragupta II, was married to **Rudrasena II** of the Vakataka dynasty (powerful in the Deccan); after his death she ruled the Vakataka kingdom as regent. {32,33}
## Kumaragupta I and the Mandsaur (Dashpur) inscription
- **Kumaragupta I 'Mahendraditya'** (415-455 AD) was the son of Chandragupta II and Dhruvadevi.
- **Mandsaur Prashasti** gives the achievements of Kumaragupta I; composed in Malwa by his contemporary **Vatsabhatta (Vatsabhatti)**. {37}
- The **Dashpur (Mandsaur) inscription** of Kumaragupta I's time is called the first advertisement of the world: it advertises the silk trade (silk fabrics). {36}
- The Dashpur inscription mentions the **Tantuwayas** (silk weavers' guild), from the Lata region (Gujarat), who built a Sun (Surya) temple at Dashpur; the same guild renovated it. {38}
- It also names Kumaragupta's governor **Bandhuvarman**, who repaired the Sun temple.
- The inscription calls Dashpur **Pashchimapur**; it was composed by Vatsabhatti, not by Bhavabhuti. {39}
## Skandagupta and the Hunas
- First Huna invasion of India came in the reign of **Skandagupta** (455-467 AD); he badly defeated the Hunas. {20,21,22,23}
- This invasion was like a raid with no lasting impact, but it hastened the decline of the Gupta Empire (the dynasty most disturbed by Hunas). {19}
- After Skandagupta's death the Hunas annexed many north-west regions in the 5th-6th century transition.
- **Bhitari Pillar inscription** (Bhitari, Saidpur tehsil, Ghazipur district, U.P.) describes the war between Skandagupta and the Hunas. {24}
- **Toramana** (a Huna) led the second Huna attack on India. {80}
- **Mihirakula**, of Central Asian Huna origin, was one of the most important Hephthalite emperors; the Gwalior inscription shows his territory included Gwalior.
- Mihirakula was defeated by the Aulikara king **Yashodharman of Malwa** in 528 AD (other sources: 495 AD). {81}
- **Vasula Kavi** composed Yashodharman's **Sondani (Sondhni) inscription**; an important historical and literary record of his reign; it tells of his victories and governance. {175}
- Causes of Gupta decline included Huna invasion, feudal set-up and later Guptas accepting Buddhism; **Arab invasion was not a cause** (Arabs came in the medieval era, much later). {25}
## Gupta administration
- Gupta rule was monarchical; Gupta rulers believed in their divine (supernatural) origin (more than the Mauryan rulers) and extended the tradition of land grants; administration was NOT highly centralised. {85}
- The Gupta king was king of many smaller kingdoms; feudal and provincial rulers had full independence in their areas (a federal-type system).
- Administrative divisions in order: **Bhukti → Vishaya → Vithi → Grama**; the empire was divided into provinces called Bhukti. {41}
| Unit | Officer |
| Empire | King |
| Bhukti (province) | Uparika |
| Vishaya | Vishayapati |
| Vithi | Aayulan and Vithimahattar |
| Gram | Gramik / Ashtakuladhikari |
- **Vishti** = forced labour imposed by the state, provincial governor or local chief; counted as a source of state income, a kind of tax paid by the people. {86}
- Vishti is listed among taxes in Gupta copper inscriptions; the Junagadh inscription shows it was imposed in Gujarat and Malwa.
- Vishti was not wage labour (wage labour existed but was not called Vishti); it could fall on anyone, not only the eldest son.
## Land, revenue and taxes
- Gupta rulers donated the maximum number of villages to temples and Brahmins; income from village land went to the land's owner. {67}
- The Gupta Empire gave tax-free farm land to religious functionaries, i.e. **Brahmins**. {75}
- Gupta land revenue rate = **one-sixth (1/6)** of produce, paid by those who tilled the ruler's official land. {70}
- In every religious text (Hindu law) the land revenue rate is 1/6 of the produce. {71,72}
- Land revenue is called **'Udranga'** and **'Bhag'** in Gupta inscriptions.
- Irrigation tax in ancient India = **'Udakabhaga'** (Udakabhog). {76}
- **Hiranya** = cash tax in the Mauryan period.
- **Uparikara (Uparnika)** = tax on farmers with no ownership rights in the soil / temporary tenants (farmers living on the land), or an additional cess.
- **'Aprahat'** = uncultivated forest land; a term of the Gupta revenue system. {73}
- **Kulyavapa** and **Dronavapa** = measures of land in the Gupta period. {74}
- **Araghatta** = water-lifting device: a large wheel with earthen pots tied to the ends of its spokes (Ar = spokes/wheel, Ghata = pot); used in ancient and early medieval India; known as the Persian wheel. {77}
## Trade, ports, guilds and industry
- When the Huna invasion ended the Roman Empire in the 3rd century AD, Indian merchants relied more and more on **South-East Asian trade**. {42}
- **Tamralipti** port in Bengal handled North Indian trade in the Gupta period; trade went to South-East Asia, China, Lanka, Java, Sumatra and northern India. {43}
- **Bhrigukachchha (Bharuch/Broach)** = main west-coast port; North India's sea trade with western countries went from here.
- Most Gupta trade was with South-East Asia, so Tamralipti was the most important port; Bharuch, Kalyan and Cambay were also used for North Indian trade. {44}
- India's early trade and cultural links with South-East Asia across the Bay of Bengal were helped by the **monsoon winds**, which made sea voyages easy. {45}
- Main centres of international trade in the Gupta period: Tamralipti, Bhrigukachchha, Arikamedu, Kaveripattanam, Muziris, Pratishthana, Sopara, Barberikam.
- **Ghantasala, Kadura and Chaul** were ports handling foreign trade in the Gupta period (external and internal trade flourished). {79}
- **Guilds (Shreni)** were set up by merchants to regulate their trade; they fixed wages, rules of work, standards and prices and had judicial power over members. {46}
- Each guild had its own head, with no interference by the kingdom, but worked under royal recognition (not registered under the king's control); guilds also mattered in governing local bodies.
- Head of a trade guild = **Sarthavaha** or **Srenipramukh**; the term Sarthavaha is linked with the **Vaishya** varna. {48}
- Rules of conduct for trade set by the guild head were called **Samay** and **Srenidharma**.
- Gujarat, Bengal, Deccan and Tamil country were famous for **textile (garment) manufacture**; textiles were the chief industry of the Gupta period. {47}
- **Warangal** was famous for iron implements (3rd century AD); the **'Agaria'** people lived there and iron-making was their traditional work. {78}
- **'Kausheya'** = word for silk in the Gupta and later Gupta periods. {156}
## Gupta coins
- Gupta gold coins were called **Dinara**. {58,59}
- Gupta silver coins were called **Rupaka**. {60}
- Gupta coins have been found in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Odisha; the most coins came from **Bayana (Bharatpur, Rajasthan)**.
- **Gadhaiya (Gandhaiya)** = a type of silver coin, issued on the lines of Sassanian coins. {130}
## Society and culture in the Gupta age
- Gupta period is called the **Golden Age** of ancient India for its excellent growth in art and culture. {68}
- Towns declined gradually in the Gupta period; most prosperous towns of the valley declined. {66}
- Pataliputra was in ruins, a village with a small settlement, when Hiuen-Tsang came; Mathura, Sonpur, Hastinapur and Sravasti also show decline.
- First inscriptional evidence of **Sati** = **Eran inscription** of 510 AD: wife of war commander **Goparaja** became sati. {63}
- In Sanskrit plays of the Gupta period, women and Sudras speak **Prakrit**; upper varnas speak Sanskrit. {62}
- Except the Vedic age, women's condition was never good in ancient India; inferior status is seen in Gupta, Maurya and Chola ages; in no age were women equal to men. {69}
- **'Mrichchhakatika'** ('The Little Clay Cart') by **Shudraka** tells the love story of rich merchant Charudatta and Vasantasena, a courtesan's daughter. {88}
- Mrichchhakatika shows urban social life, politics and social change in the Gupta period; it also tells of some Shudra officers in Ujjayini.
- **Chess** began in India in the Gupta period as **'Chaturanga'**; it went from India to Iran and then Europe. {87}
## Science, mathematics and medicine
- **Dhanvantari**, one of the Navratnas of Chandragupta II, was a master of Ayurveda.
- **Charaka** and **Sushruta** were great Ayurveda scholars; Charaka was Kanishka's physician and wrote **'Charaka Samhita'**.
- **Sushruta** = father of surgery; resident of Kashi; period around 600 BC.
- **Shaunaka** = a sage of the Rigveda; **Shudraka** = dramatist of the Gupta period; **Saumilla** = Gupta-period playwright remembered by Kalidasa.
- None of Sushruta, Saumilla, Shudraka, Shaunaka worked on medicine in the Gupta period; Sushruta (600 BC) lived about 875 years before the Gupta start (275 AD). {49}
! BPSC's first answer key gave Sushruta for "medicine in the Gupta period"; the book says Sushruta cannot be a Gupta contemporary.
- **Bhaskaracharya (Bhaskara II)**, great astronomer and mathematician of the 12th century (not a Gupta contemporary, not linked with medicine), wrote **'Siddhanta Shiromani'** and **'Lilavati'**. {51,52}
- **Bhaskara I** was a post-Gupta contemporary; he wrote **Mahabhaskariya** and **Laghubhaskariya**.
- **Aryabhatta**, mathematician and astronomer, wrote **'Aryabhatiyam'**; first to say day and night come from the earth's rotation on its axis. {57}
- Many special surgical instruments were in use by the 1st century AD; transplant of internal organs was not possible then; sine known in 5th century; cyclic quadrilateral known in 7th century. {53}
- Sine, cosine and inverse sine were known in the 5th century AD; they are in **'Surya Siddhanta'** and **'Aryabhatiya'**.
- **Brahmagupta** described the cyclic quadrilateral (in 700 AD).
## Six systems of philosophy
- Hindu philosophy = a group of darshans (philosophies, worldviews, teachings) that arose in ancient India.
- Six mainstream systems (**Saddarsana**): Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa, Vedanta; Lokayata and Kapalika are not among them. {119}

- **Bauddha** (Buddhist) school is a **Nastika** school because it does not take the Vedas as a valid source of knowledge. {120}
- Indian philosophy grew through constant dialogue and debate between non-Vedic schools and other schools; Buddhism had a massive impact on Indian philosophy and culture.
## Sankhya
- **Sankhya** is one of the oldest schools, one of six orthodox schools; **Maharshi Kapila** is its Acharya and earliest exponent. {89,90,91,93}
- Sankhya teaches that man's life is shaped by the forces of nature, not by any divine agency.
- **'Sankhya Sutra'** and **Tattvasamasa** (late 14th-15th century text of uncertain author) are traditionally attributed to Kapila.
- Sankhya accepts rebirth (transmigration of the soul); it holds that self-knowledge, not any outside influence or agent, leads to liberation. {92}
- In Sankhya, nescience (ignorance) is the cause of sorrow; self-enlightenment through knowledge is the way out.
- **Satkaryavada** (cause-effect doctrine) belongs to Sankhya: the effect pre-exists in the cause and is manifested from it. {94}
- This inseparable cause-effect link is called **Avinabhava**: no cause, no effect; example: without clay no pot can be made.
## Yoga
- **Maharshi Patanjali** = founder of **Yoga Darshan**; he explained Yoga as a compact philosophical theory, so it is called **'Patanjali Darshan'**. {95,96}
- Yoga's history is very old; it is described in the Upanishads, Mahabharata, Bhagavad Gita, Jain and Buddhist texts.
- **Ashtanga Yoga** (eight-limbed yoga) of Patanjali's Yoga Sutras: Yama (social discipline), Niyama (personal discipline), Asana (postures), Pranayama (breath control), Pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), Dharana (concentration), Dhyana (meditation), Samadhi (spiritual absorption); Anusmriti is not part of it. {98}
- Ashtanga Yoga is meant for physical, mental and spiritual development.
- **Antaranga Sadhana** = the last three limbs, Dharana, Dhyana and Samadhi; internal aids to self-realization. {97}
- Patanjali, author of **Mahabhashya**, was a contemporary of **Pushyamitra Shunga** (184-148 BC). {99}
## Nyaya
- **Nyaya Darshan** was propagated by **Gautama**, also called **'Akshapada'**; its original text **'Nyayasutra'** is Gautama's masterpiece. {106,107,108,109,112}
- 'Nyaya' means decision or logic; Nyaya recognizes 16 substances (logics).
- In Nyaya, knowledge (**Gyana**) is a synonym of both **Buddhi** (cognition) and **Upalabdhi** (apprehension). {111}
- Gyana has two senses: restricted = unmanifested knowledge; broad = both manifested and unmanifested knowledge.
- In Indian philosophy the means of gaining knowledge is called **'Pramana'** (valid means of knowledge).
- **Navya-Nyaya** school was founded by the work of Mithila scholar **Udayana (Udayanacharya)** and after him **Gangesh**. {100}
## Mimamsa
- **Jaimini** started (Purva) Mimamsa. {113}
- The **theory of Karma** belongs to Mimamsa; it is also called Karma Mimamsa, Purva Mimamsa or Dharma Mimamsa. {114}
- Mimamsa acharya **Kumaril Bhatta** is seen as the link between Purva Mimamsa and Vedanta.
- **Mimamsa** holds that the Vedas contain the eternal truth. {115}
- Purva Mimamsa deals with **'Karmakanda'**; Uttar Mimamsa with **'Gyankand'**.
- The doctrine of **Apurva** belongs to Mimamsa: an unseen force created by deeds, the mediator between Karma and its result (something not there before the deed). {116}
## Vaisheshika
- **Maharshi Kanad** founded the **Vaisheshika** school and gave the atom theory; he is called the founder of Indian Atomism. {117,118}
- Kanad was a scientist and philosopher; he gave the atomistic approach to physics and philosophy in Sanskrit.
- Atom theory: material things are made of atoms; Vaisheshika marks the start of physics in India.
- Kanad was first to prove the atom is the smallest unit of matter; it cannot be seen by the naked eye or divided further.
- Vaisheshika stresses the study of substance (**dravya**); Earth, Water, Fire, Air and Sky combine to make new objects.
## Vedanta and Shankaracharya
- **Vedanta** is one of the six darshans, often treated as the peak of Vedic thought; the word means 'the end of the Vedas' / climax of Vedic ideology.
- Vedanta has three bases, the **'Prasthanatrayi'**: Upanishads, Brahmasutra and Bhagavad Gita.
- **Adi Shankaracharya** was the main exponent (founder) of **Advaita Vedanta**; he systematized earlier philosophers' work. {122}
- Shankara's philosophy = **non-dualism**: Brahman is the one and only reality; everything else is mere illusion. {123}
- In Advaita Vedanta, **Mukti** (liberation) comes by knowledge (**Gyana**) of the identity of Atman (soul) and Brahman. {125}
- Shankara took many Buddhist ideas into his philosophy, so he is called **'Crypto-Buddhist'**. {124}
- **Visistadvaita** = a main branch of Vedanta; means "non-dualism with differentiation"; Vaishnava saint **Ramanujacharya** (c. 1017-1137) spread it. {126}
- Great Vedanta scholars: Shankaracharya (8th century), Ramanuja (11th century), Madhava (13th century); **Abhinav Gupta** is not related to Vedanta. {127}
- Shankaracharya (also Shankar, Shree Shankaracharya) was born in **Kaladi**, a small village in **Kerala**, in the 8th century. {177}
- Hinduism had declined with the rise of Jainism and Buddhism; it rose again with Shankaracharya.
- Shankaracharya preached Vedanta, set up four religious centres and travelled all over India on foot to study and compile the Vedas; he countered the spread of Buddhism and Jainism; he did not name Prayag 'Teerthraj'. {176}
- Four monasteries of Shankaracharya: **Sringeri** (Karnataka, south), **Dwarka** (Gujarat, west), **Puri** (Odisha, east), **Jyotirmath / Joshimath** (Uttarakhand, north). {178}
- Shankaracharya's four centres are counted as the most important centres of Hinduism; his philosophy is called 'Advaita Vedanta'.
- Vedanta philosophy has had an enormous impact on Hindu thought and culture; its great scholars gave varying interpretations of it.
- **Chardham** = Badrinath, Dwarka, Puri, Rameswaram; Mansarovar is not included. {179}
- **Chota (lesser) Chardham** in Uttarakhand = Gangotri, Yamunotri, Kedarnath, Badrinath.
## Charvaka (Lokayata)
- **Charvaka** philosophy, originally called **Lokayata**, is the ancient school of Indian materialism; it values material things and pleasures. {105,121}
- Charvaka saying: "Live well as long as you live; live well even by borrowing, for once cremated there is no return." {101}
- Charvaka accepts direct **perception**, empiricism and conditional inference as sources of knowledge; it holds philosophical scepticism and rejects the Vedas, Vedic ritualism and supernaturalism. {103}
- Charvaka: "the conscious body is the soul"; body is the basis of consciousness, soul and body are the same; this view = **'Dehatmavada'**. {102}
- Charvaka statement: "A body without consciousness is a corpse, and consciousness without a body is not a soul."
- **Ajita Kesakambali** is credited as the forerunner of the Charvakas.
- For Charvaka the highest value is **Kama** (pleasure): wisdom is to enjoy pleasure and avoid pain; it rejected austerities. {104}
- **Sarvasiddhanta Samgraha** on Charvaka: heaven's joy is good food, company of young women, fine clothes, perfumes, garlands, sandal paste; Moksha is death (end of life-breath).

## Kingdoms after the Guptas
- According to the Puranas, **Chandravansh (Somvansha)** was a prime dynasty of the Kshatriya varna; its rulers' prime place was Prayag.
- After a natural calamity, the Dwapar Yug Chandravanshi king **Samvaram** set up his capital at **Pratishthanpur** (present Jhunsi, Prayagraj). {134}
- From the Gupta decline to Harsha's rise, powers in North India were: the Guptas of Magadha, the Pushyabhutis of Thanesar, the Maukharis of Kanauj and the Maitrakas of Valabhi. {137}
- **Later Guptas** followed the Guptas as rulers of Magadha (present Bihar) and parts of North India, into the early 7th century.
- **Pushyabhutis of Thanesar** (present Haryana) ruled parts of North India in the 6th and 7th centuries AD.
- **Maitrakas of Valabhi** ruled western and northern parts of present Gujarat, late 5th to late 8th century AD.
- **Paramaras** ruled western and central India incl. Malwa, 9th to 14th century AD, before the Delhi Sultanate took over.
- **Yadavas of Devagiri** ruled western India from around 1187 to 1317 AD.
- **Maukharis** were feudatories of the Guptas, originally from Gaya; they made **Kannauj** their capital. {135}
- Maukharis ruled parts of Uttar Pradesh and Magadha incl. Kannauj, till around 606 AD, when the Later Guptas reconquered it.
- Main Maukhari rulers: Hari Varma, Aditya Varma, Ishvara Varma, Ishan Varma, Sharva-Varma (Sarva Verma), Graha-Varma.
- A copper seal inscription of Maukhari king **Sarvavarman** (c. 6th century AD) was found at **Asirgarh fort**, north of Burhanpur, M.P. (not a Gaya copper plate); it gives the Maukhari genealogy from Harivarman. {136}
- Correct pairs: Ishwarverman – Jaunpur stone inscription; Ishanverman – Harha stone inscription; Jivit Gupta II – Deva Barnark inscription.
## Harshavardhana
- By Banabhatta's Harshacharita, the **Vardhana dynasty** was founded by **Pushyabhuti** at Thaneshwar (Sthaneshwar); Harshavardhana was its greatest ruler. {138}
- **Harshacharita** was written by **Banabhatta**, Harsha's court poet; it is the main source for the Vardhana dynasty. {140}
- Harshacharita describes the author's contemporary ruler and the biographies of his ancestors.
- Some information on Harsha also comes from **Kalhana's Rajatarangini**; Kalidasa's and Harisena's books say nothing of Harsha. {139}
- **Banabhatta** was born at village **Prithikuta** on the Son river in Aurangabad district (present Bihar); others say Prithikuta village in Kannauj, U.P. {150}
- Banabhatta's father = Chitrabhanu; mother = Rajdevi.
- Harsha shifted his capital from Thaneshwar to **Kannauj** to help his sister **Rajyashri** rule Kannauj; Chinese sources say Harsha and Rajyashri ruled Kannauj together. {141,142}
- Kannauj was a suitable place to rule Harsha's wide empire.
- Harsha held two great religious assemblies: at **Kannauj** and at **Prayag**. {143,144}
- Kannauj council: for many religions and sects, to prove Mahayana's excellence; rulers of 20 countries came with Brahmins, shramanas and courtiers; Harsha presided.
- **'Mahamoksha council'** was held at the Sangam of Prayag every fifth year of Harsha's rule; Hiuen Tsang attended the 6th one, attended by 18 rulers.
- **Bhaskar Varman** ruled Assam in Harsha's time; he was the last ruler of the Varman dynasty. {145}
- Harsha's southward march was stopped on the **Narmada** by Chalukya king **Pulakesin II**, who defeated him; recorded in Pulakesin II's Aihole inscription and by Hiuen Tsang. {146,147}
- Pulakesin II also wanted to expand north, so war was certain; it was fought on the bank of the Narmada.
- Pulakesin ruled much of modern Karnataka and Maharashtra; capital **Badami** in modern Bijapur district, Karnataka.
- Harsha–Pulakesin battle is usually placed between 630 and 634 AD: after the battle of Vallabhi (not before 630); Pulakesin's Lohnara inscription (630 AD) omits Harsha, Aihole inscription (634 AD) mentions the battle.
- In April 2016 Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute researchers fixed Pulakesin's victory over Harsha at **618 AD**, from a newly found copper plate of Pulakesin II. {148}
! Pulakesin–Harsha battle year: the book finds 630-634 AD more correct (nearest option 634 AD); Jharkhand PSC accepted 618 AD.
- Harsha leaned to Buddhism before meeting Hiuen-Tsang (not hostile to it); after meeting him Harsha gave royal patronage to **Mahayana** and became Buddhist. {169}
- After Harsha's death Kannauj became the centre of attraction for powers; it was called **'Mahodaya'**, **'Mahodayashri'**.
- **Tripartite struggle** for Kannauj among Pala, Gurjara-Pratihara and Rashtrakuta (8th-9th century) = most important event of North India then; finally the Pratiharas won.
## Hiuen Tsang
- **Hiuen Tsang (Yuan Chwang)** visited India in Harsha's reign; his coming was the most important event of Harsha's period. {151,152}
- He wished to see places linked with Buddha and study Buddhist texts; left for India in 629 AD from **Changan**, capital of the Tang ruler; he was a Buddhist.
- Hiuen Tsang wrote **'Sei-Yu-Kei' (Si-Yu-Ki)** on his India tour; he is remembered for it as it has much information on ancient India. {158,160}
- Hiuen-Tsang was a contemporary of **Taizong** of the Tang dynasty, who had banned foreign travel to the West; still he came by the silk route in 629 AD (not sent as an emissary). {154}
- He returned to China after 13 years; the emperor welcomed him, made him spiritual adviser; at the emperor's request he wrote "A Record of the Western Regions".
- Hiuen Tsang went to **Nalanda University** in 637 AD and studied there; Acharya **Sheelbhadra** was its Vice-Chancellor. {157}
- At Nalanda he learned Yogashastra for almost one and a half years; he gave a speech on his second visit.
- Hiuen Tsang: roads and river-routes were not fully free from robbery (he was robbed many times); ordeals by fire, water and poison tested guilt; tradesmen paid duties at ferries and barrier stations. {153}
- He says punishment for crime was harsh; tax was levied on trade routes, river banks, products and goods, giving the state ample money.
- Hiuen Tsang stayed in India and visited almost every province.
- By Hiuen Tsang, **Mathura** was famous for cotton textiles and **Varanasi** for silk textiles. {155}
- By Hiuen Tsang, trade was the main reason for Thaneshwar's prosperity; Ujjayini and Kannauj were also prosperous.
- Hiuen Tsang also visited **Bhinmal**. {159}
- The **Sun temple of Multan** is mentioned by Hiuen Tsang, Abuzaiyid, Al-Masudi and Alberuni; pilgrimage to **Sambapur** was linked with Sun worship. {173}
## Other travellers and Nalanda
- Chinese traveller **Sungyun** travelled in India 518-521 AD (early 6th century); in three years he collected texts of Buddha's teachings. {82,83}
- **I-tsing (Itsing)** (671-695 AD) set out with 37 Buddhist companions in 671 or 672 AD to see Buddhist relics; companions left him, he went on alone from Canton; so not in 405, 635 or 637 AD. {161}
- I-tsing reached India by the southern sea route at Tamralipti (another passage: western sea route); returned to China in 693-94 AD via Sumatra.

- Chinese writers called India **'Yin-tu'** and **'Thian-tu'**, a variation of Hindu or Sindhu. {163}
- **Nalanda University** drew scholars and students from Tibet, China, Greece and Persia.
- Nalanda was ransacked and destroyed by the army of **Bakhtiyar Khalji** (Muslims) at the end of the 12th century (around 1193 AD); the final blow (coup de grace) to Buddhism in India. {164}
- **Nalanda** is the most ancient monastery (among Nalanda, Udantpuri, Vikramshila, Bhaja); its history goes back to Buddha and Mahavira, 6th century BC. {165}
- Nalanda, the ancient university town, is in **Bihar**. {166}
- Excavations show no sign that the Nalanda site was occupied before the Gupta period (5th century AD).
## Post-Gupta economy and feudalism
- In the post-Gupta period the main business centre shifted from Ujjain to **Kannauj**. {167}
- Kannauj lay on the banks of the Ganga and Jamuna, so it had business value and drew three powers: Palas, Pratiharas, Rashtrakutas.
- Growth of **feudalism** was the main feature of post-Gupta agrarian structure, because a land-owning middle class and a dependent peasant class arose. {168}
- Large land grants to officials, temples and nobles made landed intermediaries (landlords); cultivators became dependent peasants paying rent and services.
- Essential elements of the feudal system: administrative structure based on control and possession of land; lord-vassal relationship between feudal lord and overlord. {174}
- Feudal system in India was laid in the Saka-Kushan period and peaked in the Rajput period; part of administration under Kushanas, Rajputs, Sultanate and Mughals.
- Strong central authority divided the empire into parts run through feudals; feudals were weaker and followed the king's orders; they had rights to collect tax and to govern.
- A master-slave relation between king and feudal is not found in India; Indian feudalism differed from Europe's.
- **'Hundi'** in post-Harsha sources = a **bill of exchange**; RBI: Hundis are financial instruments evolved on the Indian subcontinent for trade and credit. {162}
## Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas
- **Pratihara dynasty** was one of the most famous Agnikula clans; known as **Gurjara-Pratihara** from inscriptions.
- First mention of the Gurjara caste = Aihole text of Pulakeshin II; Gurjars also appear in Banabhatta's Harshacharita.
- Founder of the Gurjara-Pratiharas = **Nagabhata I** (730-756 AD); Gwalior inscription says he destroyed the large army of a Mlechcha king, probably the Arab ruler of Sindh.
- **Rashtrakuta dynasty** was founded in 753 AD by **Dantidurga**; capital **Manyakheta**; he is said to have done a **Hiranyagarbha (Mahadan) Yajna** at Ujjaini.
- Rashtrakuta **Amoghavarsha I** was born in 800 AD in the military camp of Sribhavan on the Narmada, when his father **Govind III** was returning from successful North India campaigns.
