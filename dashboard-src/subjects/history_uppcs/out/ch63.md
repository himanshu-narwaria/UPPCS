@ 63 | Gandhi and His Early Movements
## Early life
- Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 at Porbandar, Gujarat.
- His education began in a pathshala in Porbandar; at seven he moved to Rajkot on his father's transfer and studied there till high school. {39}
- He was married to Kasturba at the age of 13.
- He studied in Samaldas Arts College, Bhavnagar, in 1885. {2}
- He trained as a barrister (studied law) at the Inner Temple, London.
- His father **Karamchand Gandhi**, better known as 'Kaba Gandhi', was Diwan of Porbandar, Wankaner and Rajkot. {1}
## South Africa
- On 24 May 1893, aged 23 (also given as 24), Gandhi went to Durban, South Africa, as legal counsel in a case of Gujarati trader Dada Abdullah.
- He lived in South Africa for about 21 years. {14}
- In June 1893 Dada Abdullah sent him to Pretoria in the Transvaal; the journey first took him to **Pietermaritzburg**, where he was thrown out of the train. {16}
- He had a first-class ticket and sat in the first-class compartment; a European called railway officials, who ordered him to the van compartment, as 'coolies' and non-whites were not allowed in first class.
- Gandhi protested and showed his ticket; on refusing to leave he was pushed out and his luggage tossed on to the platform.
- In 1894 Gandhi founded the **Natal Indian Congress** in South Africa; he was imprisoned several times during the long movement there.
- He started the newspaper **Indian Opinion** in South Africa (in English, Gujarati, Hindi and Tamil); first issue June 1903. {3,4}
- In 1903 he founded a law firm in Johannesburg and lived there till 1910 (before moving to Tolstoy Farm); he applied the principle of **Trusteeship** (South Africa, 1903). {72}
- He set up the Phoenix Ashram (Phoenix Farm) in 1904 near Phoenix station, **Durban**, on land he bought. {5}
- **Phoenix Ashram** was Gandhi's first (oldest) ashram; it was reopened on 27 February 2000. {20}
- In 1910 he and his colleagues set up a new colony, **Tolstoy Farm**, and began living there.
- Gandhi adopted 'Seva Dharma' in South Africa: he served helpless poor patients as an attendant in Rustomji's charity hospitals. {21}
- He gave voluntary service there as a compounder and also served the wounded soldiers of the Boer War.
- In South Africa Gandhi discovered himself: from a shy, timid youth he became a successful organiser and leader.
- The struggles in South Africa prepared Gandhi to lead the national movement; but that struggle was fully non-violent and based on Satyagraha, not militancy. {22}
- During his South Africa years Gandhi came to India in 1901 and opened his law office at Bombay in 1902.
- Gandhi first attended a Congress session at **Calcutta in 1901**. {17}
- That session, chaired by Dinshaw Wacha, passed Gandhi's resolution on Africa.
## Return to India and Gokhale
- Gandhi returned to India from South Africa in January 1915; people welcomed him warmly. {15}
- His struggles and victories in South Africa had made him popular in India too.
- **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** was Gandhi's political guru (mentor); under his influence Gandhi joined India's active politics. {23}
- Gokhale visited South Africa in 1912 at Gandhi's invitation.
- Gokhale gave Gandhi personal guidance, including knowledge of India and the issues facing common Indians; in his autobiography Gandhi calls Gokhale his mentor and guide.
- By 1920 Gandhi had emerged as the leader of the Indian independence movement.
- Gandhi promised Gokhale to spend his first year in India studying the country "with his ears open but his mouth shut". {61,62}
- Gandhi's first major public speech in India was at the opening of Banaras Hindu University (BHU), Varanasi, in February 1916. {67}
## Ashrams at Ahmedabad
- In 1915 Gandhi set up the **Satyagraha Ashram** at Ahmedabad, first at the Kocharab Bungalow of Jivanlal Desai, a barrister and friend.
- Gandhi wanted farming, animal husbandry and other activities needing much more land.
- On 17 June 1917 the Ashram moved to a 36-acre site on the banks of the Sabarmati at Ahmedabad; it came to be known as **Sabarmati Ashram**. {18,19}
## Gandhi's thought: state, socialism and politics
- Gandhi stressed practical idealism rather than idealism.
- Like Marx he believed in the removal of the State and did not mind being called a **Philosophical Anarchist**. {6}
- His thought has also been linked to Utopian socialism and compared with strands of Marxist and western liberal thought.
- In his ideal non-violent state every citizen would feel self-governing, so the state would not be needed to enforce the law.
- Yet in present circumstances he did not favour abolishing the state: humans cannot yet manage collective affairs alone, so state and law are needed, with minimum jurisdiction.
- **Truth and Non-Violence** were the twin principles of Gandhi's Ram Rajya and the base of the modern society of his dreams. {7}
- Some believe his policy of truth came from Hindu religion and non-violence from Buddhist, Jain and Christian ideology.
- For Gandhi 'Truth' stands for 'reality'; he described Truth as existence, consciousness and bliss.
- Gandhi: Ahimsa is the means, Truth is the end; non-violence is the way to attain truth. {8}
- Gandhi: Ahimsa and Truth are so intertwined that they cannot be separated, like two sides of a coin or of a smooth unstamped metallic disc.
- Gandhi: means must always be within our reach, so Ahimsa is our supreme duty; if we take care of the means we reach the end sooner or later.
- Gandhi: whatever the difficulties or reverses, we may not give up the quest for Truth, which alone is, being God Himself.
- Gandhi said "Wrong means never take us to right ends"; means and ends are two sides of a coin, and immoral means vitiate the end. {63}
- Gandhi was convinced that if we take care of our means, the end will take care of itself.
- Gandhi strongly supported the principle "Which is morally wrong can never be politically right". {64}
- Gandhi was a true socialist who cared for both private and social interests and wanted to make social justice work in practice.
- Gandhi to Louis Fischer: "I am a true socialist and the aim of my socialism is progress of all" (Sarvodaya).
- Gandhi's socialism has glimpses of Marxism; he gave extraordinary importance to labour.
- He wanted work for everyone according to capacity and pay according to need.
- Gandhi: "I am engaged in solving the same problem that faces scientific socialists. It is true, however, that my approach is always and only through unadulterated non-violence".
- Gandhi is called an Individualist among Socialists and a Marxist among Socialists. {9}
- Gandhi and Marx both aimed at a stateless and classless society; Gandhi's means were non-violent, Marx's violent.
- For Gandhi politics meant activity for social uplift and public welfare. {24}
- His most important political suggestion was **decentralisation of power**: power to Gram Panchayats to manage and administer their villages.
- Gandhi's model of politics joined morality (ethics), religion and humanity; it had no place for authority (State power), as he wanted a stateless society. {25}
- According to Gandhi the cruelest form of violence is the persistence of poverty. {12}
- Gandhi called self-control the best means of family planning. {13}
## Satyagraha
- Gandhi first called the struggle in South Africa 'passive resistance' and later replaced the term with **Satyagraha**; he is best known for using passive resistance to win Indian independence. {32}
- For Gandhi the two differed: passive resistance is a political weapon, Satyagraha is moral power; he compared them to the north and south poles.
- Gandhi's nephew Magan Lal Gandhi first used the term 'Sadagraha' in a competition and was rewarded; Gandhi improved it to 'Satyagraha', so Gandhi coined the word. {28}
- Satyagraha = Satya (truth) + Agraha (insistence): 'insistence on truth'.
- Gandhi was the first to start Satyagraha during India's freedom struggle. {29}
- Fasting was the first weapon and **strike the last step** in the Gandhian strategy of Satyagraha. {10}
- He termed fasting 'Fire Arrow'; Satyagraha is also said to be 'Agni-Baan' for him.
- The aim of Satyagraha was not to defeat the enemy but to change his heart, through the suffering the Satyagrahi takes on himself. {11}
- Satyagraha means non-violence as a force greater than violence; the weapon is Ahimsa, the Satyagrahi must be firm in belief and bear no ill feeling to enemies.
## Western influences
- Gandhi read **'Unto This Last'** by John Ruskin in South Africa; his autobiography says the book changed his life and most influenced him.
- Its message for Gandhi: "The good of the individual is contained in the good of all"; on this he built his idea of the welfare of all. {40}
- Thoreau, Ruskin and Tolstoy strongly influenced Gandhian ideology. {41}
- From Henry David Thoreau (essay on Civil Disobedience) he learnt civil disobedience and the benefit of refusing to pay taxes.
- Ruskin taught him to respect physical labour.
- Tolstoy's "The Kingdom of God is within you" became the base of his non-violence and non-cooperation.
## Champaran Satyagraha (1917)
- **Champaran Satyagraha** (Bihar, 1917) was Gandhi's first Satyagraha, first civil disobedience and first successful Satyagraha in India; it was a farmers' movement. {30,38,80,96,99,100,101}
- Mahatma Gandhi led the Champaran Satyagraha and was the national leader of the Champaran indigo movement. {97,105}
- From the early 19th century European planters bound the farmers by agreement to grow indigo on 3/20th of their land: the **Tinkathia (Teen Kathiya) system**. {31,77,82,83,84}
- Tinkathia, the most prevalent system in Champaran = three kathas per bigha; factory owners got the tenant to grow indigo on a set part of his holding at a fixed price.
- Planters imposed Tawan (a lump-sum compensation or illegal levy) and other cesses to exploit farmers and make leaving the contract impossible.
- Towards the end of the 19th century German synthetic dyes pushed indigo out of the market; planters then demanded higher rents and illegal dues as the price of release.
- Resistance had surfaced in 1908.
- In 1917 Champaran farmers were protesting against having to grow indigo with barely any payment.
- The Champaran movement was to solve the problems of indigo workers. {81}
- The Champaran movement was basically against cash crops. {88}
- **Pt. Raj Kumar Shukla**, a local indigo cultivator, persuaded and invited Gandhi to Champaran to look into the exploitation of farmers. {78,79,87,98,102,103}
- Raj Kumar Shukla made the request on meeting Gandhi at Lucknow.
- Gandhi reached Patna on his way to Champaran with Rajkumar Shukla and began his mission of freeing bonded labour with the Champaran Satyagraha.
- Gandhi arrived in Champaran on 10 April 1917 with eminent lawyers Brajkishore, Rajendra Prasad, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, Acharya Kripalani and others.
- Acharya J.B. Kripalani was one of Gandhi's colleagues in the Champaran investigation; Rajendra Prasad did not persuade Gandhi to come. {85}
- Gandhi's Champaran colleagues: J.B. Kripalani, Dr. Rajendra Prasad, Mahadev Desai, C.F. Andrews, Dr. Anugrah Narayan Sinha, Braj Kishor Prasad, H.S. Polak. {86}
- Jai Prakash Narayan was not connected with the Champaran movement. {90}
- Ram Manohar Lohia was not related to the Champaran Satyagraha. {91}
- J.B. Kripalani was a teacher by profession; Rajendra Prasad taught for some time but was basically a lawyer. {52}
- The government instituted the **Champaran Agrarian Committee** to look into the peasants' grievances. {95}
- The committee was appointed in agreement between the Indian Government and Gandhiji; Gandhi was a member. {94}
- Champaran Agrarian Enquiry Committee: president F.G. Slay; members D.J. Reid, Mahatma Gandhi, L.C. Adami, Raja Harihar Prasad, Narayan Singh, G. Rainny and M.E.L. Tainer; Anugrah Narayan was not a member. {93}
- The planters agreed to return 25% of the illegally collected money; so the first Satyagraha succeeded.
- A very significant aspect of Champaran: drastic decrease in the cultivation of plantation and commercial crops. {92}
- Champaran Satyagraha was limited to Champaran; Gandhi's first all-India movement was the Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919). {104}
- Champaran proved that Satyagraha in South Africa was not a fluke but a powerful way of conflict resolution.
- **N.G. Ranga** opposed Gandhi's Champaran Satyagraha. {106}
- **Rabindranath Tagore** gave Gandhi the title 'Mahatma' during the Champaran Satyagraha. {46,47}
- **Batak Mian**, cook of a British indigo plantation manager in Bihar (known as Erwin), saved Gandhi from poisoning in 1917. {26,27}
- Erwin invited Gandhi to dinner during the Champaran Satyagraha and coerced Batak Mian to poison him through milk; Batak Mian broke down and told Gandhi.
- Dr. Rajendra Prasad confirmed the story in a 1950s speech at Motihari, cited in Delhi University Professor Dr. Girish Mishra's article "Gandhi's Champaran Struggle".
## Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918)
- After Champaran, Gandhi took up the dispute between cotton mill owners and workers of Ahmedabad over the **plague bonus** (February-March 1918).
- The Ahmedabad Satyagraha was for industrial (cotton mill) workers. {69,70,71}
- When Champaran is not a choice, Gandhi's first Satyagraha is taken as the one against payment of low wages to workers (Ahmedabad). {65}
- Gandhi advised the workers to strike and demand a higher plague bonus.
- Gandhi went on a "fast unto death" to strengthen the workers; this was his first use of the hunger strike as a weapon. {36,66}
- The mill owners gave in after 21 days of strike and agreed to submit the issue to a tribunal.
- The tribunal later awarded the 35% increase (bonus / dearness allowance) the workers had demanded.
- Anasuya Behn, sister of Ambalal Sarabhai, was a main lieutenant of Gandhi in this struggle; her brother, Gandhi's friend, was one of the main advisers.
## Kheda Satyagraha (1918)
- Kheda, like Champaran, was a farmers' movement.
- In Kheda (Gujarat) crops failed in 1918; the government gave no revenue concession and did not stop collecting land revenue, so Gandhi organised a Satyagraha for the farmers. {107}
- Kheda peasants suffered poverty, famine and British discrimination; famine had destroyed the region's agrarian economy.
- The revolt was against taxes and rents; the government threatened to seize property if taxes were unpaid.
- Kheda was against high taxes people could not pay due to crop failure and plague epidemic.
- No Permanent Settlement was proposed for Gujarat.
## Order of Gandhi's movements
| Event | Year |
| Champaran Satyagraha | 1917 {35,37,76} |
| Ahmedabad Mill Strike | February-March 1918 |
| Kheda Satyagraha | March-June 1918 |
| Satyagraha against the Rowlatt Act | 1919 |
| Non-Cooperation Movement | 1920-1922 |
| Civil Disobedience Movement | 1930 |
- The Rowlatt Act was passed to curb revolutionary activities in India.
- Gandhi led the Kheda Satyagraha (1918), Civil Disobedience Movement (1930) and Quit India Movement (1942); **Bardoli Satyagraha** (1928) was led by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel. {43}
- Gandhi was not related to the Swadeshi Movement (1905-1911, against the partition of Bengal); he was then in South Africa. {42}
- Khilafat Movement, Individual Satyagraha and Quit India Movement were led by Gandhi.
- Gandhi did not take part directly in the **Vaikom Satyagraha**. {49}
- Vaikom Satyagraha: a movement in Travancore (modern Kerala) for temple entry of the depressed classes, against untouchability and caste discrimination; Gandhi took part indirectly in 1925. {50}
- Gandhi led the Rajkot Satyagraha, Kheda Satyagraha and Non-Cooperation Movement.
- In the Rajkot Satyagraha, after a 4-day hunger strike, Gandhi agreed to settle with the ruler of Rajkot through the Viceroy's mediation.
## Boycott, economy and Harijan service
- Gandhi said "destruction is the best method of dealing with foreign clothes"; he linked it with the nation's self-respect. {33}
- His boycott of British goods was effective because the British saw India as a major market for manufactured goods. {34}
- The boycott promoted the production and sale of Indian goods such as crafts and homemade items.
- Gandhi: each country's economy should be decided by its climate, land and the nature of its people; so he never opposed mechanisation in the USA. {73}
- He stressed an economy based on non-violence, held that centralisation leads to exploitation and inequality, and did not favour mechanisation in India.
- His economics opposed all exploitation, whether of one group by another or of one country by another.
- He wanted a system where a person can freely choose work and labour, and the revival of Indian goods industries so that no one goes hungry.
- He stressed cottage industries: making jaggery, thrashing grass, extracting oil, paper making, leather work.
- He stressed rural industries such as khadi, handlooms, sericulture and handicrafts, since India lives in its villages.
- Rural industries use family labour, little capital, local raw materials and local markets; they reduce the burden on agriculture and check migration to cities.
- According to Gandhi, socio-economic improvement of untouchables can come by setting up cottage industry for them. {74}
- Gandhi opposed planned development in India; he was opposed to industrialisation and mechanisation. {89}
- He backed cottage and village industries and labour-intensive enterprises; under Gram Swaraj the village was a self-sufficient economic unit.
- 'Gandhian Innovation', a term coined by Prof. Prahlad and R.A. Mashelkar, means to produce more from less input for more people: "serving people with fewer resources and at a lower cost". {75}
- Gandhi fought hard to improve the status of untouchables, and launched the Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience Movements. {44}
- 'Service to Harijans' was his prime constructive programme; in his weekly Harijan he called it a spiritual act of soul acting upon soul.
- Gandhi: "Service of Harijan is the breath of my life without which I won't survive one single minute"; Stanley Wolpert quotes it as "the breath of life, more precious than the daily bread".
## Titles and tributes
- **Subhash Chandra Bose** was the first to call Gandhi 'Father of the Nation', in July 1944 on Azad Hind Radio from Rangoon. {45}
- Bose said: "India's last war of independence has begun… Father of our Nation, in this holy war of India's liberation, we ask for your blessings and good wishes."
- **Lord Mountbatten** called Gandhi the 'One Man Boundary Force'. {60}
- Mountbatten's wire: Punjab had 50,000 soldiers and large-scale rioting; Bengal's force was one man and there was no rioting.
- Gandhi named **Yerawada** Central Jail (Pune, Maharashtra) 'Mandir'; he spent several years there. {57}
- Gandhi resigned from the Congress in 1934 and never again became a formal member; at Independence he was not a member of the Congress. {58}
- On 30 January 1948 Nathuram Godse, a Hindu extremist, shot Gandhi.
- Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru** said: "The light has gone out of our lives and there is darkness everywhere. I do not know what to tell you and how to say it. Our beloved leader, Bapu as we called him, the Father of the Nation, is no more." {59}
## Associates
- **Pyarelal** Nayyar was Gandhi's secretary (since 1942) during the Noakhali communal riots. {48}
- His sister Dr. Sushila Nayyar was Gandhi's personal doctor; Pyarelal also played an important role in the Dandi March.
- **Jamnalal Bajaj**: cotton (cloth) merchant, banker, Congressman, social worker and close associate of Gandhi. {54}
- Bajaj was an industrialist, philanthropist and freedom fighter; his leadership as commander of the Flag Satyagraha made him a national leader.
- Bajaj was born in Sikar district (Rajasthan) and adopted by a rich businessman of Wardha.
- He met Gandhi in 1915 and stayed his follower for life; Gandhi called him his 'fifth son'.
- He took part in the Congress at the age of 30; he was treasurer of the Congress and founder of Gandhi Seva Sangh.
- Bajaj served as AICC treasurer for many years and went to jail in 1923 and 1930; unique among capitalists as a full-time Congress activist. {53}
- He joined the Non-Cooperation Movement and gave up his title 'Rai Bahadur' in 1921.
- In 1921 he brought Vinoba Bhave to Wardha to start a branch of the Satyagraha Ashram.
- He worked for abolishing untouchability, Harijans' entry into Hindu temples and promotion of khadi; he took deep interest in village industry and handloom cloth.
- In 1923 a tricolour flag procession at Nagpur in memory of Jallianwala Bagh was banned; Bajaj led the **Nagpur Flag Satyagraha** and got 18 months' jail and a Rs. 3,000 fine.
- He was jailed again in 1930 during the Civil Disobedience Movement.
- Balchand Hirachand, in an April 1930 letter to FICCI, urged businessmen to stop sitting on the fence and join those fighting for Swaraj.
- In May 1930 FICCI decided to boycott the Round Table Conference while Gandhi stayed away and until the Viceroy promised dominion status.
- **Charles Andrews** (Deenbandhu Andrews), Gandhi's close English associate, taught at St. Stephen's College, Delhi. {55}
- He loved Indians and wanted to be an Indian; he was close to Rabindranath Tagore, Gopal Krishna Gokhale and Mahatma Gandhi.
- He lived with Gandhi at Phoenix; Gandhi gave him the title 'Deenbandhu' for his devotion to the poor.
- Andrews was president of the All India Trade Union Congress in 1925 and 1927 (also given as from 1925 to 1927); he went with Gandhi to the Round Table Conference in London.
## Chhattisgarh visits
- Gandhi first came to Chhattisgarh on 20 December 1920, at Dhamtari; he came again on 24 November 1933. {51}
- **Maulana Shaukat Ali** accompanied Gandhi on the 1920 tour, which was on the invitation of Sundarlal Sharma. {56}
- Shaukat Ali was part of the Khilafat movement and attended the first and second Round Table Conferences.
