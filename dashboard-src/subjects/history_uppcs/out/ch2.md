## Name, nature and sources
- Human society depends mainly on the economy; as the economy changes, human society changes. {1}
- Indus Valley Civilization is also called **Harappa Civilization**, because the first archaeological evidence came from Harappa (the site discovered first). {2}
- With ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, it was one of the three ancient civilizations of the world.
- Archaeologists use 'culture' for a group of objects, distinctive in style, usually found together within a specific area and period of time.
- Distinctive objects of Harappan culture: seals, beads, weights, stone blades and baked bricks.
- The Harappan seal is possibly the most distinctive artefact of the civilization.
- Historical period is decided by available written literature; the period before the art of writing was known is called early historical age.
- Indus Civilization is **Proto-historic**: writing was known but is still not deciphered. {3}
- Indus Valley Civilization was the earliest known urban civilization of the Indian subcontinent; Aryan civilization was rural. {4}
- Pottery found in excavations proves Indus Civilization existed before Aryan civilization: Harappan pottery was red painted with black figures; Aryans used grey painted pottery. {5}
- Script and town planning of Indus Civilization were more developed than those of Pre-Aryans; pottery evidence comes from early settlements of Mehrgarh.
- Indus culture differed from Vedic culture: it was urban (Vedic was rural), had a pictographic script, and did not know iron (Vedic people used iron weapons widely). {6}
- Evidence from archaeological excavations is the major source of knowledge about Harappan culture. {7,8}
- First artefact uncovered at Harappa: a unique stone seal carved with a unicorn and an inscription; similar seals with other animal symbols and writing are found across the region.
- Excavation of the civilization began in 1921; tools, weapons and ornaments were found, but the main source is **seals**; more than 3000 seals were found. {9}
- Indus Valley Civilization was a **Bronze Age** civilization; people did not know iron; gold, silver, copper and brass are found, but not iron. {27,69}
- In India the Iron Age began from the later Vedic period (about 1000 B.C.).
- Order of appearance in India: urban culture (Harappan) → iron (post-Vedic period, around 1000 B.C.) → punch-marked silver coins (7th-6th century B.C.) → gold coins (Indo-Greek rulers, 2nd century B.C.). {66}
- Copper was the first metal used by humans, though the period of its use varies around the globe. {67}
- Indus Valley Civilization is known for town planning, for Mohenjodaro and Harappa, for agriculture (ploughed field at Kalibangan) and for industry/business (evidence from Lothal). {29}
- Main sites: Harappa, Mohenjodaro, Ropar, Lothal and Kalibangan; cities had roads, proper housing and drainage; Harappans knew metals. {71}
- Mohenjodaro and Harappa are extinct (dead) cities today; they were discovered through excavations, but that is not the reason they are dead. {72}
- Pataliputra (next to modern Patna) was not an Indus Valley site; Kalibangan, Ropar and Lothal are. {53}
- **Sohgaura** is not a Harappan site: village on the Rapti river, Gorakhpur district, U.P.; a Mauryan copper plate inscription found there tells about storehouses. {16}
- Chanhudaro and Kot Diji (Sindh, Pakistan) and Desalpur (Kachchh, Gujarat) are Harappan sites.
## Dating, extent and people
- Carbon-14 dating puts the Harappan period at 2600 BC to 1700 BC (widely accepted); the civilization peaked during 2300 BC to 1900 B.C.
- Mesopotamian and Egyptian civilizations were contemporary to the Indus civilization.

- Harappan culture covered parts of Afghanistan; Punjab, Sindh and Baluchistan in Pakistan; and in India Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana, fringes of western Uttar Pradesh, J&K and Western Maharashtra.
- Boundaries: north Manda (Jammu and Kashmir); south Daimabad (Maharashtra); west Sutkagen Dor (Baluchistan, Pakistan); east **Alamgirpur** (Meerut, Uttar Pradesh). {35}
- The area formed a triangle of about 13 lakh sq km: larger than Pakistan and bigger than ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia.
- The civilization spread from the Jhelum river to the Hindon river (tributary of Yamuna; Alamgirpur); it had rivers Indus, Chenab, Ravi, Sutlej and Jhelum, but not the Ganga. {56}
- Population of Mohenjodaro was a mix of at least four races: Proto-Australoids (Caucasian), Mediterraneans, Alpines and Mongoloids.

## Discovery and excavators
- The vast mounds were first reported by Charles Masson in 1826.
- **Alexander Cunningham**, head of the ASI, visited Harappa in 1853 A.D. and 1856 A.D.; he made small excavations and reported ancient pottery, stone, tools and a stone seal, but did not recognize its significance. {62}
- **Sir John Marshall** was Director-General of the ASI from 1902 to 1928; he announced the discovery of a new civilization in the Indus Valley to the world in 1921. {59}
- Marshall appointed Dayaram Sahni; Sahni's excavation found the ruins of Harappa in 1921 (survey in January 1921); Rakhal Das Banerjee dug out Mohenjodaro in 1922: the two Indians linked with the discovery. {60}
- Harappa was later excavated by Madho Sarup Vats (1926-27 to 1933-34) and Mortimer Wheeler (1946).
- Others who contributed to the discovery: K.N. Dixit, Ernest Mackay, Aurel Stein, A.N. Ghosh and J.P. Joshi.
- V.A. Smith was not linked with Harappa-Mohenjodaro excavation; he was an Irish Indologist and historian. {63}

## Harappa
- **Harappa** is in Montgomery district (now Sahiwal district) of Punjab province, Pakistan, on the left bank of the Ravi river. {41}
- Stuart Piggott called Harappa and Mohenjodaro "Twin capitals of one United Kingdom".
- Harappa had two mounds: eastern = city mound; western = Citadel mound.
- Harappa granary remains: 12 rooms in two rows of six rooms each.
- Harappa has **Cemetery R-37**; Lothal the dockyard; Kalibangan the furrowed land; Mohenjodaro the Dancing Girl statue. {20}
## Mohenjodaro
- **Mohenjodaro** is a Sindhi word meaning "Mound of the dead".
- Mohenjodaro is on the right bank of the Indus river in Larkana district, Sindh, Pakistan; discovered by Rakhal Das Banerjee in 1922.
- The **Great Bath** at Mohenjodaro is its most important public place and a well-known structure of the civilization; it seems to have been used for ritual bathing. {24,25}
- A large ruined building north-east of the Great Bath measures 70.1 × 23.77 m; perhaps the residence of a very high official, possibly the high priest or a college of priests.
- Roads were the main quality of Mohenjodaro: the main road (Rajpath) was 9.15 m wide; straight roads cut at right angles, making square and quadrangle blocks.
- Mohenjodaro finds: mother goddess idol, dancing girl statue, seated male in soapstone (Priest-King), Pashupati (Shiva) seal, skeletons in wells, teeth of horse, evidence of cotton cloth.
- Mohenjodaro also gave sculptures, seals, pottery, gold and jewellery; the most famous is the **humped bull seal**. {85}
- The **Dancing Girl** of Mohenjodaro is about 10.5 cm high, made of bronze, cast by the lost-wax technique. {86}
- Clay model boats were found at Lothal; seals with a boat model were found at Mohenjodaro. {26}
- Evidence of human sacrifice was also found in Mohenjodaro.
## Chanhudaro
- **Chanhudaro** lies about 130 km south of Mohenjodaro; discovered by N.G. Majumdar in 1931; excavated on a large scale by E.J.H. Mackay in 1935-36. {40}
- Mackay found a bead making factory and furnace at Chanhudaro; some phase cut bricks were also found.
- Remnants of Jhukar and Jhangar cultures were found at Chanhudaro.
- Chanhudaro may have been an industrial hub: shell making, bangle making and bead making.
- Chanhudaro finds: ornamented elephant, toys, vestige (trace) of a dog that followed a cat, lipsticks.
## Lothal
- **Lothal** is near Saragwala village, Dholka Taluka, Ahmedabad district, Gujarat; discovered by S.R. Rao in 1954; Harappa and Mohenjodaro are in Pakistan. {44}
- Lothal lies along the Bhogava river, a tributary of Sabarmati, in the Gulf of Cambay.
- A brick basin on the eastern side of Lothal town is identified as a dockyard by its excavator; Lothal is the only Indus site with evidence of a port. {47,48}
- Lothal, on the shores of the Gulf of Khambhat, was an ancient trading port and a major harbour for trade with Western Asia. {49}
- By planning and material goods, Lothal seems a mini Harappa or mini Mohenjodaro.
- Lothal finds: rice husk, metal workers, shell ornament makers, bead makers, shop, fire altars, terracotta figurine of a horse, terracotta model of a ship, a Persian seal, painted jar.
- An **ivory scale** was found at Lothal. {68}
- Three double burials were found at Lothal; double burials were also found at Kalibangan and Rakhigarhi.
## Kalibangan
- **Kalibangan** is in Hanumangarh district of Rajasthan (not Haryana), on the bank of the Ghaggar (Saraswati) river. {30}
- Kalibangan was discovered by Luigi Pio Tessitori, an Italian Indologist; excavated by A. Ghosh in 1950-52 as a Harappan site.
- Kalibangan excavation was started by B.B. Lal and B.K. Thapar in 1960-61.
- Kalibangan's western mound was a fortified enclosure with rectangular salients and towns (as printed).
- Kalibangan gave the earliest **ploughed (furrowed) field** ever found in excavation. {18,19}
- A terracotta plough, along with the ploughed field, comes from the early phase of Kalibangan (as per ASI); a terracotta plough also comes from Banawali. {52}
- Kalibangan finds: Mesopotamian seals, wheels of a toy cart, fire altars.
- Kalibangan: a series of 'fire altars' on mud-brick platforms was discovered.
- A triangular terracotta cake from Kalibangan has a horned deity on one side and, on the other, an animal (goat) dragged by a rope by a human; it points to a sacrificial tradition. {92}
## Dholavira
- **Dholavira** is in the Rann of Kachchh (Kachchh district), Gujarat; second largest Harappan site in India after Rakhigarhi. {74}
- Dholavira was excavated by R.S. Bisht and colleagues in 1990-91 (also printed as 1992).
- Dholavira gives important information about a great bath and a **rock-cut reservoir**, about 79 m × 12 m and 7 m deep, with huge capacity. {73}
- Dholavira town was rectangular and divided into three parts: Citadel, Middle town and Lower town. {75,76}
- Dholavira people knew an advanced water management system: from the earliest period they built a series of dams and channelled water into connected reservoirs. {77,78}
- An inscription of ten large-sized signs of the Harappan script was found at Dholavira. {17}
## Other sites
- **Rakhigarhi** (Hisar district, Haryana; Ghaggar-Hakra river plain) is the largest Indus Valley site in India; the village lies along the bank of the Ghaggar river. {54,55}
- **Banawali** is in Fatehabad district, Haryana, in the valley of Ghaggar and its tributaries. {70}
- Banawali finds: clay figures of the mother goddess and a toy plough (terracotta replica of a plough).
- **Surkotada** is in Kachchh district, Gujarat; discovered and excavated by Jagatpati (J.P.) Joshi. {64}
- Surkotada gave horse bones and pot burials.
- **Daimabad** is on the left bank of the Pravara river in Ahmednagar (now Ahilyanagar) district, Maharashtra; discovered by B.P. Bopardikar in 1958.
- Daimabad is famous for many bronze goods, some influenced by Harappan culture.
- **Ropar** (Punjab) is on the left bank of the Satluj; present name Rupnagar; discovered by B.B. Lal in 1950; excavated by Yagyadatt Sharma in 1953-55.
- **Rangpur** is in the Saurashtra region of Gujarat, on the bank of the Bhadar river (coast of the Arabian Sea). {42}
- Rangpur people grew Rice, Bajra and Sorghum, as large vegetative remains show.
- **Dadheri** (Kotla Dadheri): prehistoric excavation site of Govindgarh, district Fatehgarh Sahib (Punjab). {43}
- Dadheri is known for painted grey ware culture, thought to be linked to the arrival of the Aryans.
- **Alamgirpur** is in Meerut district, U.P., on the Hindon river.
- Alamgirpur finds: a small terracotta bead-like object coated with gold, evidence of cloth, and utensils with triangle, peacock and squirrel impressions.
- **Hulas** (Saharanpur district, U.P.) is a Late Harappan site.
- Hulas finds: hand-made and wheel-made pottery with geometric or naturalistic designs painted in black, chert blades, bone points.
- **Bhagwanpura** is in Kurukshetra district, Haryana, on the southern bank of the Saraswati; excavated by J.P. Joshi.
- **Manda** is on the bank of the Chenab in Jammu and Kashmir; excavated by J.P. Joshi and Madhubala (year printed as 1776-77).
- Manda is seen as a site used to get wood from the Himalayan regions and send it downstream to other Indus towns.
- **Sinauli** (Baraut Tehsil, Baghpat district, U.P.): 125 graves of the Indus Valley Civilization (human burials) were found. {80}
- Sinauli graves are oriented North-South; most are primary human burials; some have animal bones, gold, copper and bangles.
## Town planning and buildings
- Town planning was the unique feature of the civilization; cities and towns were well planned, most on a grid pattern.
- Drainage was advanced: drains ran along the roads and were covered with slabs (big stones and bricks).
- Both baked and raw bricks were used; burnt bricks from all Indus towns generally had the ratio 4:2:1.
- Bricks were not all of one size or kind: ornamental bricks come from Kalibangan and curved bricks from Chanhudaro. {28}
- Most houses had one storey, but some had two storeys, as stairs found in some houses show.
- Roads in Harappan cities were generally 10 metres wide.
- Use of burnt bricks in buildings was a main feature: used in plenty at Harappa and Mohenjodaro; at Lothal used for drainage. {50}
- Other main features of the civilization: well planned architecture, advanced trade and commerce.
- Remains of wells and bathrooms are found in almost every major Indus city.
## Economy, metals and crafts
- Trade and commerce were advanced; there is evidence of both internal and external trade.
- Harappans knew **silver**: the earliest evidence of silver in India; its proper use is seen at Mohenjodaro and Harappa. {12}
- Other sources of silver for Harappa and Mohenjodaro were Afghanistan and Iran.

- **Cotton** was first cultivated for clothing in India; earliest evidence of cotton cultivation came from excavations in India. {82}
- From India cotton reached Greece, where it was called "Sindon".
- Cotton cultivation began in India in 3000 BC; in Egypt in 2500 BC.
- Cotton textiles were made in the Harappan period, as artefacts show.
- Harappan pottery is bright or dark **red**, uniformly sturdy and well baked; wheel-made; both plain and painted. {13}
- Plain ware is usually of red clay, with or without a fine red slip; painted pottery was red and black; black painted ware has a fine coating of red slip.
- Polychrome pottery was rare: mainly small vases with geometric patterns, mostly in red, black and green, less often white and yellow.
- Painting colour scheme: light black, chocolate or light red over a buff or greenish-buff background.
- Art forms found at the sites include seals, pottery, gold ornaments and necklaces.
## Seals and animals
- Most Indus seals were made of steatite; a few of terracotta, agate, chert.
- Most seals were square; a few were cube, circle or cylinder shaped.
- **Pipal** tree appears many times on Indus seals, showing its sacred value. {87}
- Elephant, rhinoceros, tiger, deer and sheep are shown on Harappan seals and terracotta art; the **cow** is not shown. {15}
- Most seals carry short inscriptions with figures of the one-horned animal (most common), buffalo, tiger, rhinoceros, goat and elephant.
- Harappans knew the horse, but there is no horse on the seals. {88}
- Evidence of cow and elephant is found; evidence of **lion** is not mentioned anywhere in Harappan excavations. {89}
- Evidence of bull and elephant is found (seal of Zebu bull; seals showing elephant); the horse is disputed, though the latest excavation shows evidence of horse.
- Horses played no major role in the Indus Valley Civilization; BPSC accepted 'horse' as the animal not known to Indus people. {90}
## Religion
- Pre-Aryan period is taken as the start of idol worship; it was prevalent in Indus Civilization, as coins from Mohenjodaro with the seal of God show. {14}
- Many pieces of evidence of **Mother Goddess** worship are found; people probably worshipped Mother Goddess along with male and female deities.
- Marshall identified a figure from excavation (Mohenjodaro; also printed as Harappa) as an early form of the Hindu God **Shiva** (Rudra) or **Pashupati** (lord of animals). {58}
- Pashupati seal: three-faced male god in yogic posture, with a rhino and a buffalo on the right and an elephant and a tiger on the left.
- Cylindrical stones show that people worshipped the Lingam, symbol of Lord Shiva.
- No temple or place of worship has been found till date; people worshipped both male and female deities; they did not have great palaces and temples, and did not use horse-drawn chariots in war. {83}
- The only religious sources are statues of mud and stone, and seals.
- These show worship of female deities, Shiva or Pashupati, and animals like snake and elephant.
- Worship of plants and trees, and of stone or other objects as 'Linga' or 'Yoni' symbols, was common.
- Indus Valley Civilization is considered a **secular** civilization, with no trace of theocratic or organized religion. {84}
- Indus people may have performed Yagya (fire pits found at Rajasthan and Gujarat sites); burial rituals show belief in Atma or spirit.
- The undeciphered Indus script has attracted speculations and hypotheses.

## Other ancient civilizations
- Ancient **Egyptian** civilization flourished along the banks of the river Nile for almost 3,000 years. {93}
- Nile is called the 'lifeline of Egypt'; a major north-flowing river in north-eastern Africa, generally regarded as the longest river in the world.
- Indus Valley Civilization (India) and Mesopotamian civilization (Iraq) were contemporary civilizations.
- **Mesopotamian** civilization (present-day Iraq and nearby areas) is considered the world's oldest civilization. {95}
- Egyptian civilization (civilization of the Nile) grew in the Nile basin and is held to have emerged after the Mesopotamian.
- **Sumerians** were the first to develop the art of writing in a proper system; their original script was very simple and primitive. {96}
- The Cuneiform script of Sumeria is known as one of the earliest systems of writing.
- Civilizations from north to south: Aztec → Maya → Muisca → Inca. {94}
- Aztec civilization spread over the northern part of Mesoamerica; Mayan over the southern part of Mesoamerica.
- Mesoamerica = region from central Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua and northern Costa Rica.
- Muisca civilization was in the eastern part of Colombia (South America).
- Inca civilization spread in western South America, from north of Quito to Santiago in the south.
