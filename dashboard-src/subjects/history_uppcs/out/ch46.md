@ 46 | Revolt of 1857
## Causes and the cartridge issue
- December 1856: Company decided to replace the old iron Brown Bess (standardised musket) with the new **Enfield Rifle**. {1}
- Training and trials of the new rifle were fixed at Dum Dum, Ambala and Sialkot.
- The upper cover of the cartridge had to be bitten off by mouth while loading it into the muzzle.
- January 1857: rumour spread that cartridges were deliberately greased with cow and pig fat.
- Sepoys believed the rifle was a British attempt to destroy their religion; this suspicion of interference in religion was the immediate cause of the revolt. {2}
- The cartridge issue insulted both Hindu and Muslim religious practices.
- Anglo-Indian historians held sepoy discontent and greased cartridges to be the main cause.
- Modern historians: cartridges were only a spark that set off explosives piled up by political, social, religious and economic discontent.
- Main cause of the revolt was the (exploitative) policy of the British Empire; grievances burst out like a volcano. {7}
- The revolt had diverse political, economic, military, religious and social causes.
- Sepoys = generic term for native Indian soldiers of the Bengal Army; they had their own grievances against the Company.
- Underlying grievances included taxation and land annexations by the Company.
- Within weeks dozens of army units joined the peasant army in widespread rebellion.
- The old aristocracy, Hindu and Muslim, whose power the Company was eroding, also rebelled.
- Sepoy grievances: officers' efforts to spread Christianity, order to travel on ships, stoppage of Bhatta; inefficiency of officers was not a cause. {13}
- Hindus believed sea travel could cost them their religious status.
- A more immediate grievance: no foreign service allowance (Bhatta/Batta) for serving in Sindh or Punjab.
- **Sir William Howard Russell**, correspondent of 'The Times' (London), wrote: "Nobody in North India looks at the white man's car with a friendly view." {8}
- Russell was an Irish reporter sent to India by The Times to report on the 1857 mutiny.
## Barrackpore and Mangal Pandey
- First sepoys to refuse cartridge practice were at Berhampore in late February 1857; refusal then spread.
- 29 March 1857: soldiers at **Barrackpore** (West Bengal) refused the greased cartridges; **Mangal Pandey** attacked and wounded two officers. {3,4}
- The two officers: Sergeant Major James Thornton Hewson and Lieutenant and Adjutant Henry Baugh.
- Mangal Pandey was a sepoy of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry.
- The British disbanded the 34th Bengal Native Infantry Regiment and punished the culprits.
- Mangal Pandey was hanged on 8 April 1857, not on 29 March 1857.
- Lieutenant General Sir **John Bennet Hearsey** was commanding officer at Barrackpore when the revolt broke out. {72}
## Meerut and Delhi
- The revolt began on 10 May 1857 at **Meerut**. {9,10}
- At Meerut the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry (85 of 90 troopers) refused the new cartridges and rebelled openly.
- Also printed: the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry and the twentieth regiment of the British Indian Army at Meerut revolted on 10 May 1857.
- Meerut sepoys fired on (murdered) their officers, broke the jail, freed imprisoned comrades and marched to Delhi.
- Major General William Hewitt had 2200 European soldiers but did not try to stop them.
- The march of sepoys to Delhi's Red Fort was the first event of the 1857 war. {11}
- Lieutenant General George Dobson Percival Willoughby resisted at Delhi but was defeated.
- Delhi sepoys joined the rebels; rebels captured Delhi on 12 May 1857.
- On 11 May the sepoys proclaimed **Bahadur Shah Zafar** Emperor of Hindustan (India).
- Bahadur Shah was old and could not give able leadership.
- Success at Delhi spread the revolt to Oudh, Rohilkhand, Western Bihar and other areas.
- Bahadur Shah gave **Bakht Khan** real authority and the title 'Saheb-e-Alam Bahadur'; Bakht Khan's area was Delhi. {6,61}
- Nawab Hamid Ali Khan is matched with Delhi. {66}
- May 1857: Battle of Shamli between forces of Hazi Imdadullah and the British.
- Occupation of Delhi was short-lived; the English recaptured Delhi on 20 September 1857 (also printed 20–21 September). {15}
- Sikhs and Pathans of Punjab and North West Frontier Province helped the British recapture Delhi.
- **Chapatis (bread) and lotus** flowers carried the message; they are the symbol of the revolt. {14}
- Some historians call the mutiny a wide, well-planned conspiracy.
- They cite chapatis and lotus reaching remote North Indian villages, and prophets, hermits and plays of Madaris.
- The 1857 revolt was the first expression of organised resistance; began as a sepoy revolt, later joined by the masses.
## Jhansi
- **Rani Lakshmi Bai** (Manikarnika) was born at Kashi/Benaras (now Varanasi). {16}
- Her birth date is printed as c. 19 November 1828 in one place and 19 November 1835 in another.
- Her father Moropant went to the court of King Gangadhar Rao.
- At 14 she married Maharaja Gangadhar Rao of Jhansi, a Maratha king; she was his widow.
- 1853: Gangadhar Rao died without a biological heir; adopted son Damodar Rao was left as heir.
- Dalhousie did not recognise the adopted son and annexed Jhansi by Doctrine of Lapse in 1854.
- Doctrine of Lapse: kingdom of a ruler dying without a biological heir is annexed by the Company.
- Revolt in Jhansi began in June 1857 (4 June 1857), not 11 May; sepoys started it and Lakshmi Bai later took control. {67}
- Rani left for Gwalior after the fall of Jhansi; her revolt ended with her martyrdom after a long battle.
- Lakshmi Bai died on 17/18 June 1858 at Gwalior; her samadhi (mausoleum) is in Gwalior. {19,20}
- Her last battle was against General **Hugh Henry Rose**. {21}
- Hugh Rose: "Among the Indian revolutionaries the woman lying here is the only man."
- Another wording of Hugh Rose: "Sleeping Beauty is the only man among all Indian rebel leaders."
## Lucknow and Oudh
- Revolt at Lucknow (Oudh) began on 30 May 1857 under **Begum Hazrat Mahal**. {22,23,24}
- She declared her minor son Birjis Qadr Nawab and took charge of all affairs.
! Many reference books and official websites give 4 June 1857 as the start of the Lucknow revolt, while the NCERT class 12 book gives 30 May 1857.
- Hazrat Mahal was the wife of Wajid Ali Shah.
- 21 March 1858: Colin Campbell regained Lucknow with the help of the Gurkha Regiment.
- **Nana Saheb** and Begum Hazrat Mahal both escaped to Nepal. {12}
- Nana Saheb fled to Nepal in 1859 after the British retook Kanpur; his later fate is unclear (died there, or lived in disguise).
- Hazrat Mahal fled to Nepal in 1858 after Lucknow was retaken; she died there in 1879.
- The largest number of sepoys in the revolt came from Oudh: more than 75,000; one member of each family joined. {26}
- During the revolt 60% of Bengal Army soldiers were from Oudh and the North West Province.
- **Maulavi Ahmadullah Shah** led the revolt from Faizabad (now Ayodhya); also matched with Awadh and Lucknow. {62}
- Ahmadullah Shah was the bitterest enemy of the British. {49}
- He was native of Chinapattan (Madras) and migrated to Faizabad.
- He asked people of all religions to unite and wipe the English out of India.
- British view: 'a man of great abilities, of undaunted courage, of stern determination and by far the best soldier among the rebels'.
- A reward of Rs. 50,000 was placed on his arrest (dead or alive).
## Kanpur
- 5 June 1857: rebel sepoys halted at Kalyanpur; 6 June they returned to Kanpur and Nana Saheb (Nana Dhondhu Pant) took their lead.
- Nana Saheb was proclaimed Peshwa at Kanpur (30 June 1857 as per R.C. Majumdar; also printed: Nana Saheb revolted and was accepted as Peshwa on 5 June 1857).
- **Tatya Tope** was Nana Saheb's commander-in-chief (general military advisor). {27}
- Tatya Tope's real name: Ramchandra Pandurang Tope. {30}
- Tatya Tope was betrayed by his friend Man Singh, captured in the forest of Paron and put to death by the British. {29}
- Captured 7 April 1859; military court verdict 15 April 1859; hanged 18 April 1859 at Sipri/Shivpuri.
- Tatya Tope was born in 1814 at Yeola, Maharashtra; only child of Pandurang and Rukhmabai.
- He grew up at Bithoor, where he met Nana Saheb Peshwa; a personal adherent and great admirer of Nana Saheb.
- After British reoccupation of Kanpur he led the Gwalior contingent and forced Lieutenant General Charles Ash Windham to retreat from Kanpur.
- He later came to the rescue of Rani Lakshmibai; was defeated by General Napier's troops.
- Tatya Tope was the only person who saw the rebellion from the Kanpur revolt to his end on 18 April 1859.
- **Azimullah Khan** was advisor and secretary of Nana Saheb; called 'Krantidoot'. {28}
- Azimullah Khan is matched with the revolt at Kanpur and Fatehpur. {63}
## Bareilly and Allahabad
- **Khan Bahadur Khan** of Rohilkhand led the revolt at Bareilly. {17,18}
- Bahadur Shah II appointed him Viceroy in Rohilkhand.
- He raised an army of 40 thousand and fought hard; treated Hindus and Muslims equally.
- **Maulvi Liyaqat Ali** led the 1857 uprising at Allahabad (Prayagraj), not Lucknow. {25,64}
- Liyaqat Ali belonged to village Mahgaon, Chail Pargana, District Allahabad; a religious teacher.
- He captured Khusro Bagh and declared India's independence; Khusro Bagh became sepoy headquarters.
- He took charge as Governor of liberated Allahabad; the mutiny there was suppressed by General Neil.
- Sambhalpur – Surender Sahi; Ganjam – Radhakrishna Dandsena; Kashmir – Gulab Singh.
## Bihar and Kunwar Singh
- **Kunwar Singh** led the 1857 revolt in Bihar. {31,34,35,36,37,38}
- Kunwar Singh was zamindar (king) of **Jagdishpur** (now Bhojpur district) in Shahabad region of Bihar; born there. {32,39,40,42}
- Jagdishpur was the centre of the Bihar revolt; Kunwar Singh deposed British rule and set up his own government. {45}
- He lost his zamindari due to British policies, so he joined the revolt.
- The mutiny at Patna (Danapur) was led by Kunwar Singh, a dynamic old Rajput. {43}
- Babu Kunwar Singh was nearly eighty years old in 1857.
- 25 July 1857: he took command of soldiers who revolted at Danapur; two days later he occupied Arrah, the district headquarters. {33}
! BPSC's own key gave Patna as the place where Kunwar Singh joined the rebellion; this is called wrong, and Arrah is correct.
- August 1857: Major Vincent Eyre relieved Arrah, defeated Kunwar Singh and destroyed Jagdishpur.
- Kunwar Singh reached Lucknow in December 1857; occupied Azamgarh in March 1858 but soon left.
- Pursued by Brigadier Douglas, he retreated to Bihar.
- 23 April 1858: victory near Jagdishpur over Captain Le Grand; 26 April 1858: he died in his village.
- His brother Amar Singh continued the struggle and ran a parallel government in Shahabad district for long.
- October 1859: Amar Singh joined the rebel leaders in the Nepal Terai.
- Bihar revolt was suppressed by William Taylor, Commissioner of Patna division, and Major Vincent Eyre.
- Bihar centres of revolt: Arrah, Danapur, Gaya, Patna, Muzaffarpur, Shahabad; Munger was unaffected. {60}
## Other centres and leaders
- **Maniram Dutt Dewan**, an Assamese nobleman, led the 1857 plot in Assam. {44}
- He declared Kandarpeswar Singha, grandson of the last king, as king and planned an armed insurrection; was caught and hanged.
- **Thakur Kushal Singh of Auwa** defeated the joint army of the English and Jodhpur. {46}
- Ajmer, Neemuch and Auwa were centres of the revolt; Jaipur was not. {47}
- Saadat Ali Khan fought the British in 1857 at Indore. {48}
- Nawab **Fazil Mohammad Khan** of Bhopal led the main movement there and gave his life in 1857. {50}
- Leaders active in 1857: Rani Lakshmibai (Jhansi), Tatya Tope, Nana Saheb (Kanpur), Begum Hazrat Mahal (Lucknow), Maulavi Ahmadullah (Faizabad), Begum Zeenat Mahal (Delhi). {65}
- Bhagat Singh was not linked with 1857; Lakshmibai, Bahadur Shah Zafar and Tatya Tope were rebel leaders. {53}
- Uddham Singh was not linked with 1857; he was a revolutionary of Punjab. {54}
- Col. St. Legar, Lt. Col. Gibbs and Col. Wallace are not associated with the Revolt of 1857. {75}
- Chittor was not affected by the revolt; Jhansi, Jagdishpur and Lucknow were prominent centres. {59}
- **Mirza Ghalib** saw the revolt of 1857 with his own eyes. {51}
- Ghalib was born on 27 December 1797 in Agra and died in Delhi on 15 February 1869. {52}

## Who helped the British
- **Scindias of Gwalior** gave the most help to the British in suppressing the revolt. {55}
- Canning said: "If Scindia joins the rebels, I will pack off tomorrow."
- Sir Dinkar Rao, minister of Gwalior, and Salar Jung, minister of Hyderabad, were praised by European historians for loyalty.
- Rulers who actively helped the British: Scindia of Gwalior, Holkar of Indore, Nizam of Hyderabad, King of Jodhpur, Nawab of Bhopal.
- Also: Sikh rulers of Patiala, Nabha and Jind, other Sikh sardars of Punjab, Maharaja of Kashmir.
- Canning said these rulers acted as barriers against the high, fast-moving wave of the sea, without which the British would have been washed out.
- Nizam of Hyderabad did not rebel against the Company; Raja of Vijayanagaram, Polygar of Tamil Nadu and Dewan Velu Thampi of Travancore did. {58}
- The British suppressed the revolt in Rajput states because local Rajput rulers did not support the rebels. {79}
- The educated middle class remained neutral (or sided with the British). {56}
- They saw the rebellion as regressive, reactionary and anti-modern; believed the British would bring progress.
- Later they came to know the cruel strategy of the British behind this.
- Moneylenders (sahukars) and landlords did not take part; moneylenders and merchants opposed it for economic reasons. {57}
## Spread and reasons for failure
- The revolt spread over a wide area with public support, yet did not become an all-India revolt.
- South India stayed largely quiet; Bengal and Punjab were only marginally affected; parts of eastern and western India were involved.
- Another list of areas outside the revolt: entire South India, Punjab and land to its north and west, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh.
- Main cause of failure: lack of common strategy and central organisation (leadership). {76}
- The revolt was poorly organised, with little coordination and nominal central leadership.
- Rebels lacked a clear understanding of colonial rule, a forward-looking programme, coherent ideology, political perspective or social alternative.
- Official sources record much Hindu-Muslim and inter-regional unity among rebels, but central leaders lacked unity.
- Modern nationalism was unknown; each fought for own interest, sharing only hatred of alien rule.
- Failure causes: limited area, no unity or planning, no unified leadership and discipline, better British resources, traitors, British troops back from Crimea. {77,78}
- Also: least support of native rulers; non-cooperation of Sikhs, Punjabis and the educated middle class.
- Bahadur Shah Zafar's leadership lacked popular support and rulers of important states stayed away; so the revolt failed to win freedom. {81}
- Most important reason for failure: native kings of major territories supported the British.
## British officers and government
- **Lord Canning** (1856–62) was Governor-General during the revolt of 1857. {68,69,70,71}
- Canning was the last Governor-General appointed by the Company and the first Viceroy appointed by the Crown.
- Canning made Allahabad (Prayagraj) the emergency headquarters in 1857. {73}
- Indian High Courts Act 1861 set up High Courts at Bombay, Calcutta and Madras; formally established by Letters Patent of 1862 under Canning.
- Canning passed the Hindu Widow Remarriage Act, 1856.
- **Viscount Palmerston** (Henry John Temple, 3rd Viscount) was British Prime Minister during the revolt; tenure 1855–1858 and 1859–1865. {74}
- Sir Henry Lawrence, Major General Henry Havelock and Brigadier General Neil died at Lucknow. {80}
- Brigadier General John Nicholson was wounded leading the capture of Delhi; died nine days later on 23 September 1857.
- Sir Henry Montgomery Lawrence, Chief Commissioner of Oudh ('Lawrence of Lucknow'), was wounded on 2 July and died on 4 July 1857 defending the Residency.
- Brigadier General James George Smith Neill died in September 1857 trying to reach the Residency during the siege.
- Major-General Sir Henry Havelock died of dysentery in November 1857, a few days after the siege was lifted.
## Views and books on 1857
- The Sepoy Mutiny of 1857 is called the First War of Independence in India and the Indian Mutiny in England.
- **Sir James Outram and W. Taylor** called the revolt a result of a Hindu-Muslim conspiracy. {82}
- Outram: a Muslim conspiracy that made use of Hindu grievances.
- John Lawrence and Seeley: only a Sepoy Mutiny.
- T.R. Holmes: a war between barbarism and civilisation.
- **V.D. Savarkar**, in 'The Indian War of Independence 1857', called it a planned, organised struggle and the First War of Independence. {83,84,85,86}
- **R.C. Majumdar**: "the so-called First National War of Independence of 1857 is neither First, nor National, nor a war of Independence". {89,90,91}
- Majumdar's reason: a large part of the country was unaffected and many sections took no part.
- Government of India appointed Majumdar to write on 1857; after differences he published 'The Sepoy Mutiny and the Revolt of 1857' on his own in 1957.
- Majumdar's first proposal was turned down; later he joined the Board of Editors for the freedom movement history, then left and wrote independently.
- **Surendra Nath Sen** = official historian; his 'Eighteen Fifty-Seven' was published in 1957, commissioned by the Indian government. {87}
- S.N. Sen was the first Director of the National Archives of India.
- R.C. Majumdar and S.N. Sen held that Indian nationalism in the mid-19th century was in an embryonic stage. {92}
- **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** wrote 'Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind' (Urdu): first Indian to write in an Indian language on the causes of 1857. {88}
- It was written/submitted in 1858 and published in 1859; later translated as 'The Causes of the Indian Revolt'.
## Results of the revolt
- The revolt gave a death blow to East India Company rule; rule passed to Queen Victoria. {93}
- **Queen's Proclamation** was declared on 1 November 1858 at a Durbar in Allahabad by Lord Canning. {94}
- The Proclamation was the most important outcome of 1857; it put British India under direct control of the Crown.
- Proclamation promises: no extension of territory, no interference in religion, equal protection of law for all.
- Also: secularism and religious neutrality; no more annexation of native states; kings allowed to adopt.
- Of all assurances only the abandonment of conquest and annexation was fulfilled. {95}
- Objects of the Proclamation: disclaim annexation of Indian states and place Indian administration under the Crown; not to regulate Company trade. {96}
- The Act of 1858 is called the 'Act of Administrative Reforms in India'.
- 1858 Act: Company territories to be governed by and in the name of the Queen; Company lost its powers.
- Secretary of State for India got the powers of the Court of Directors; a Council of fifteen members assisted him as an advisory body.
- Secretary of State became the real channel of communication between Britain and India.
- The Crown could appoint the Governor-General and Governors of Presidencies.
- An Indian Civil Service was to be created under the Secretary of State.
- All Company property and assets passed to the Crown, along with its treaties and contracts.
- **Peel Commission** was set up after the revolt to reorganise the Indian Army. {97}
- The Commission divided the army on caste, religion, race and community lines.
- European soldiers rose from 45,000 (1857) to 65,000 (1863); Indian troops cut from 2,38,000 to 1,40,000.
- Ratio of European to Indian soldiers: 1:2 in Bengal; 1:3 in Madras and Bombay.
- After the revolt the British recruited Gurkhas, Sikhs and Punjabis from the north. {98}
- Soldiers of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar had played a big role in the revolt, so the British distrusted them.
- 1 November 1858: Queen Victoria declared reorganisation of the army on a policy of separation and conflict.
