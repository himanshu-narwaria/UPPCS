@ 45 | Economic Impact of British Rule on India
## Home charges and trade policy
- Indian administration was in British hands and run to serve the interests of England.
- **Home Charges** = expenditure incurred in England by the Secretary of State for India on behalf of India (Indian Government).
- Home Charges included: India Office establishment, pensions and gratuities paid in England to retired civil servants, purchase of military stores, interest on debts, interest on railway capital, non-effective army charges; funds for wars outside India were not included. {1}
- Home Charges also covered dividends to East India Company shareholders, interest on public debt raised abroad, civil and military charges, store purchases in England.
- Interest and profit on private foreign capital was another important leakage from national income.
- India made huge payments for banking, insurance and shipping services.
- **Imperial Preference** = special privileges (concessions) on British imports in India; an empire-wide preferential tariff arrangement. {2}
- Under it Britain pledged free entry for most colonial goods while imposing tariffs on foreign imports.
- Result: almost no (nominal) duty on British imports in India, but heavy duty on Indian exports in Britain.
- The rich preferred to invest in land; so industries lacked capital and had no independent development under British rule. {3}
- Mid-18th century: Bengal emerged as an essential supply source for the Company's sales in Europe.
- Company's staple exports from Bengal in mid-18th century: cotton, silk, saltpetre and opium (also cloth and sugar). {4}
- 40% of Central revenue was spent on the military force in British India. {35}
## Permanent Settlement
- **Permanent Settlement** of land revenue was introduced by Lord Cornwallis in 1793. {5,6,7,10,11}
- Other names: Istimrari, Malgujari (Maalguzari), Bishwedari.
- It was launched in Bengal, Bihar, Odisha and Varanasi of U.P.; covered about 19% of British India.
- Permanent Settlement was made with the Zamindars (landlords), recognised as proprietors (owners) of the land. {8,9}
- Zamindars gave 10/11 of total revenue to the Company and kept 1/11 for their duties.
- In the Permanent Settlement Zamindars were small capitalists.
- It recognised the rights of hereditary or lawful successors of the landlords.
- Government believed these landlords would stay faithful to the British.
- Landlords got the right to transfer or sell their land.
- Revenue payable by each landlord for his Zamindari was fixed once and for all; tax rate would not rise in future.
- All rights of landlords depended on paying the fixed revenue on the fixed date at the government treasury; rights ended on failure.
- **Sunset clause**: if the Zamindar did not pay by sunset of the fixed date, his Zamindari was taken over, auctioned and passed to a new owner. {30}
- Reasons for increased litigation after 1793: loss of all rights on non-payment, and more intermediaries between farmers and landlords. {12}
- Many Zamindars did not issue pattas to farmers because there was no official check on them; they only had to pay 10/11 of collection. {13}
- In Bihar the Permanent Settlement ensured a fixed amount of revenue for the Company. {14}
- By the 19th century demand for land rose; landlords raised rents; peasants revolted.
- **Bengal (and Bihar) Tenancy Act 1885** defined the rights of landlords and tenants in Bengal and Bihar. {15}
## Ryotwari System
- **Ryotwari System** was devised by Thomas Munro and Captain Alexander Read; Cornwallis was not linked with it. {16,19,21,22}
- Ryotwari was the second land revenue system after Permanent Settlement.
- Alexander Read first applied it in **Baramahal** district (Tamil Nadu), acquired by the Company in 1792; first settlement of Madras Presidency. {17,24}
- Read, assisted by Munro, fixed the state demand at 50% of the estimated produce of the fields.
- Munro as Governor of Madras (1820–27) extended Ryotwari to all parts of the province except permanently settled areas.
- Also printed: Munro and Read introduced the Ryotwari system in 1820.
- Ryotwari spread to Madras, some parts of Bombay, Assam and Coorg (part of modern Karnataka); one list adds Eastern Bengal. {23}
- Permanent Settlement was introduced in Bengal and Ryotwari in Madras. {18}
- Ryotwari prevailed in most of South India; it was the standard system of Madras Presidency.
- In Ryotwari the ryot (peasant) owns and occupies the land and pays revenue directly to the government. {26}
- Every registered holder of land is recognised as proprietor and is responsible for direct payment to the state.
- Ryotwari features: rent paid directly by peasants, government gave pattas to ryots, lands surveyed and assessed before being taxed. {25}
- Ryotwari covered about 51% of British India.
- Land tax rate was very high and collection very strict; this became a major cause of struggle between moneylenders and peasants.
- Ryots were not exempted from revenue for bad harvests or natural calamities; only a few remissions or reduced rents sometimes.
- Thomas Munro said: "The Ryotwari is a system which has always prevailed in India." {27}
- **Mirasidars** = village elites of South India, often high caste, who claimed ownership of the whole village land; farmers worked under them.
- Banian = Indian agent of the East India Company doing commercial work; Mirasidar = designated revenue payer to the state in Ryotwari. {51}
- **Aurang** = Persian term for a warehouse where goods are collected before sale (not in-charge of treasury).
## Mahalwari and other systems
- **Mahalwari System**: unit of revenue settlement is the village or mahal; village land belongs to the village community.
- The community was jointly responsible for paying the land revenue.
- Mahalwari covered about 30% of British India: United Provinces, Central Provinces and Punjab (with some changes).
- Mahalwari was devised by **Holt Mackenzie**, territorial secretary of the Board of Revenue, in 1822 in Bengal Presidency. {33}
- Under Mahalwari the whole village was one unit called 'Mahal'; revenue was not fixed but revised periodically.
- British used different land revenue systems (Permanent, Ryotwari, Mahalwari) in different parts; this created different classes in the peasantry. {28}
- Mahalwari is matched with the North-West Provinces.
- Bombay Presidency mainly had Ryotwari; **Taluqdari** system was chiefly in Awadh (Oudh), not Bombay. {29}
- **Punjab Land Alienation Act** passed in 1900; it stopped land transfer from farmers to non-farmers. {31}

## Drain of wealth
- **Drain of wealth** = constant flow of wealth from India to England without adequate economic, commercial or material return.
- Drain Theory: part of India's national wealth or annual product was exported to Britain for which India got no material return. {36}
- Before 1813 the Company's recurring surplus came from oppressive land revenue, monopoly trade profits and exactions by Company officials.
- The Company used this surplus as 'investment' to buy exportable goods in India and elsewhere; India got nothing in return.
- This system was ended by the Charter Act of 1813.
- From 1813 the drain took the form of 'unrequited' exports; so India generally had a favourable balance of trade till World War II. {39}
- Dadabhai Naoroji called the drain the "evil of all evils" and the main cause of Indian poverty.
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** (1825–1917), 'Grand Old Man of India', propounded the Drain of Wealth theory. {37,38}
- Naoroji's works: 'England's Duties to India' (paper), 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India', 'The Wants and Means of India', 'On the Commerce of India'.
- 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' was written by Dadabhai Naoroji and published in 1901; it set out the drain theory. {42,43,44,46}
- The theory is attributed to Naoroji partly because he decided to estimate India's national net profit.
- Naoroji's causes of drain: external rule and administration; India paid Britain's civil and army expenses; wealth sent to England without return.
- Naoroji: India bore the burden of territory building inside and outside India.
- Naoroji's most effective contribution: he exposed the economic exploitation of India by the British. {48}
- He was the first Indian nationalist to prove by research that a definite amount was sent to England every year.
- Naoroji with W.C. Bonnerjee founded the **London Indian Society** in 1865 to expose the plight and exploitation of Indians.
- Naoroji was the first Indian elected to the British House of Commons, on the Liberal Party ticket, in 1892. {45}
- **Romesh Chunder Dutt** also stressed the drain; in 'The Economic History of India' he said half of India's net revenue flowed out every year.
- Indian National Congress accepted the Drain Theory at its Calcutta session, 1896.
- Congress (1896) declared the drain the real cause of frequent famines and poverty of Indians.
- Economic critics of colonialism (1870–1905): Dadabhai Naoroji, G. Subramania Iyer, R.C. Dutt, Govind Ranade, G.V. Joshi, Gopal Krishna Gokhale. {40}
- R.C. Dutt was an ICS officer; these critics wrote India's economic history and held colonialism the main hurdle to economic growth.
- **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** did not believe in the drain theory; he was loyal to the British and felt Muslims could develop only under British rule. {41}
- **Karl Marx** called British economic policy in India disgusting. {49}
- Under British rule a vast number of Indian cultivators lived in dejection and unmitigated impoverishment.
## Deindustrialisation and commercial agriculture
- **Deindustrialisation** in India started in 1813; ending the Company's trade monopoly aggravated it. {47}
- 1813: Company's commercial monopoly ended, except tea and opium trade and trade with China.
- Saint Helena Act 1833 (Government of India Act 1833) ended the Company as a trading body; it became purely administrative.
- By the 1833 Act the Company lost its monopoly of trade with China and other parts of the Far East.
- First half of 19th century: Industrial Revolution ruined Indian handicrafts. {53}
- British policy turned India into an exporter of raw materials and a consumer of finished goods.
- Machine-made British goods were cheap and good; demand for Indian handicrafts fell even at home.
- Indian goods could not sell in Britain because of high tariffs.
- **Commercialisation of Indian agriculture** was an economic result of British rule in the 19th century. {52}
- Farmers were forced to grow indigo and other cash crops as raw material for British factories.
- Result: urban population declined and domestic industries were destroyed.
- 19th-century agriculture also became commercial through use of modern agricultural equipment; Indians began growing crops for commercial use on a large scale.
- **Assam Company**, the first tea company in Assam, was formed in England in 1839 with a capital of Rs. 5 lakh. {32}
- Assam Company's headquarters is at Nazira, Assam; oldest tea company in Assam still working.
- **Sir Arthur Cotton**, a British irrigation engineer, is the pioneer of irrigation works in South India. {50}
