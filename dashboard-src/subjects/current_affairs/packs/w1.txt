  - **Global emissions:** Greenhouse gas emissions reached **58.4 gigatonnes of CO2 equivalent (GtCO2 e) in 2025**, against a revised **58.5 GtCO2 e in 2024**; uncertainty prevents confirming a decline or peak.

  - **2035 carbon-sink target:** India aims for a **cumulative increase of 3.5–4 billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent in carbon sinks through forest and tree cover**, relative to 2005 levels. Emissions-Gap-Report-2026
  - **Progress towards 2030 commitments:** India is among **six G20 members** likely to achieve their unconditional 2030 targets under existing policies, alongside **China, Indonesia, Russia, South Africa and Türkiye**. The report also projects India to achieve its conditional 2030 target.
  - **Projected emissions under NDCs:** Under full implementation of its NDC targets, India’s annual emissions in **2035** are projected to be **1,254 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent higher than in 2030**, with per capita emissions of **3.6 tonnes**. Emissions-Gap-Report-2026

[u0526] Ch6 · The Kigali Amendment marked its 10th anniversary
  context: - · PDF p. 146
  The Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol marked its 10th anniversary on **October 15, 2026**. Adopted in Kigali, Rawanda on October 15, 2016. It seeks to phase down HFCs, which are used in cooling. Full implementation could avoid about 0.3 – 0.5°C global warming by 2100.
  **Key Highlights**
  **Background**
  - The **Montreal Protocol (1987)** was adopted to phase out CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances.
  - Countries have since eliminated nearly **99%** of controlled ozone-depleting substances, helping the ozone layer recover.
  - HFCs became widely used in refrigeration and air-conditioning because they do not damage the ozone layer.
  - However, many HFCs have **hundreds or thousands of times** the warming impact of CO2 over **100 years**.
  - As HFC use expanded, the **Federated States of Micronesia and Mauritius** proposed bringing them under the Montreal Protocol in **2009**.
  - The Kigali Amendment entered into force in **January 2019**. It establishes binding schedules to reduce HFC production and consumption, while developing countries receive longer compliance periods.
  - Since limited HFC use can continue after reductions, it is called a **phase-down** rather than a phase-out.
  **India’s Kigali Commitments**
  - India ratified the Kigali Amendment in **2021**.
    · **2028:** Freeze HFC production and consumption at the agreed baseline.
    · **2032:** Reduce HFCs by **10% below the baseline**.
    · **2047:** Achieve an **85% reduction**.
  - The limits are calculated according to the gases’ warming impact.
  - Equipment purchased before the first reductions may remain in use well into the **2030s**.

[u0536] Ch6 · Ethanol Blending in India
  context: - · PDF p. 150
  Ethanol is a renewable alcohol fuel. India’s Ethanol Blended Petrol (EBP) Programme mixes domestic ethanol with petrol to reduce crude-oil dependence, support farmers and rural industry, and lower road transport’s environmental impact.
  **Meaning and Present Status**
  - E5, E10 and E20 contain about 5%, 10% and 20% ethanol by volume. E20 combines anhydrous ethanol with 80% petrol. Since November 2023, the Ethanol Supply Year (ESY) runs from 1 November to 31 October. India first achieved 20% blending in 2025; the November 2025 – August 2026 average was 20%. Public-sector outlets normally supply E20; premium grades XP100, poWer100 and Speed100 remain ethanol-free. From 1 April 2026, notified blended petrol requires a minimum Research Octane Number of 95.
  **Evolution of the Programme**
  - Pilots began in 2001, and the EBP Programme launched in 2003 in nine States and four Union Territories. The 2018 National Policy on Biofuels targeted E20 by 2030; the 2022 amendment advanced it to ESY 2025–26. E10 was achieved in June 2022, and phased E20 rollout began on 6 February 2023.
  **Scale and Measurable Outcomes**
  - Capacity grew from 421 crore litres in 2014 to about 2,000 crore litres in 2026. Procurement was about 38 crore litres in ESY 2013–14 and was projected to exceed 1,200 crore litres in ESY 2025–26. The 10 July 2026 update reported cumulative gains since ESY 2014–15: foreign-exchange savings exceeding ₹1.97 lakh crore, nearly 316 lakh tonnes of crude substitution, around 952 lakh tonnes of CO2 reduction and farmer payments exceeding ₹1.66 lakh crore.
  - **Why Ethanol Blending Matters**
    · Energy Security
    · Farm and Rural Economy
    · Environment and Fuel Quality
    · Major Government Measures
    · Vehicle Compatibility and Mileage
  **International Perspective**
  - E10 predominates in the United States; E85 contains about 51–83% ethanol and is intended for flex-fuel vehicles. Brazil mandated E30 from 1 August 2025. Brazil temporarily introduced E32 in ordinary and additivated petrol from August 1, 2026 for 180 days with one possible equal extension. Japan targets direct E10 by FY 2030 and E20 by FY 2040. India helped launch the Global Biofuels Alliance in September 2023 to promote sustainable biofuels, standards and best practices.

  - Unlike earlier COPs that leaned heavily toward announcing new pledges, COP30 marked a clear shift toward **implementation**.
  - The central political outcome was widely described as the **Belém Package**—around **29 decisions** functioning less as a new “target set” and more as an **implementation toolkit** to deliver the Paris Agreement.
  - The package’s conceptual foundation was the **Global Mutirão decision.**
  - “Mutirão” (a Brazilian term) implies the community coming together for shared work—similar in spirit to India’s concept of **“Shramdaan.”**
  **Accelerating Action: Mitigation, Adaptation, and Finance (with Finance the Most Contested)**
  **(1) Climate Finance: reinforcing obligations + tracking and delivery tools**
  - A major achievement attributed to COP30 was strengthening the interpretation of **Paris Agreement Article 9.1** (developed countries “shall provide” financial resources to developing countries).
  - A **two-year “Climate Finance Work Programme”** was launched.
  - **Tropical Forest Forever Facility (TFFF):** Brazil-led blended finance mechanism targeting **USD 125 billion**, designed like a “scholarship for forests,” delivering performance-based payments for protecting tropical forests (monitored via satellite data). **(2) Adaptation: operationalizing the GGA and scaling resilience delivery**
  - COP30 decisions treat the Baku Adaptation Road Map as an ongoing work stream with a defined first phase (2026-28). **(3) Mitigation & Transition: the Belém 4x Pledge and sectoral scaling**
  - To curb emissions and accelerate system transitions, COP30 advanced the implementation of the **Belém 4x Pledge.** The pledge was launched at the Pre-COP.
    · Target: **quadruple the use of sustainable fuels by 2035** relative to 2024 (including green hydrogen, advanced biofuels, and e-fuels).
    · Tracking: **IEA** to track progress.
  - **23 countries officially endorsed** the pledge, with broad non-state support (including major industry actors).
  **Just Transition**
  - **Belém Action Mechanism (BAM):** adopted to support a socially fair transition away from fossil fuels—focused on worker reskilling and social dialogue, though without a large dedicated fund.
  - The **Fossil Fuel Treaty Initiative** announced its **18th** nation-state supporter (Cambodia) and presented a PAS with next steps through the Climate Action Agenda.
  **Health**
  - **Belém Health Action Plan:** launched to build climate-resilient health systems to tackle heatwaves and vector-borne diseases.
  - The Belém Health Action Plan is described as the **world’s first international climate adaptation plan dedicated entirely to health**, launched with an initial **USD 300 million** investment from a coalition of **35+ philanthropies**, funding research, policy, solutions for extreme heat/air pollution/ infectious diseases, and strengthening health systems via climate-data integration.
  - COP30 also marked “Race to Zero for health care hits 100”: The campaign has reached **100 institutions** representing **14,000+ hospitals/ health centers** across **28 countries**.
  **India’s position at COP30 (as reflected in negotiations and blocs)**
    · **Finance is an obligation:** India emphasized that under Article 9.1, climate finance is a **legal obligation**, not charity, and demanded **USD 1.3 trillion/year**.
    · **Opposition to CBAM:** India strongly opposed unilateral trade measures like the EU’s CBAM, arguing they violate CBDR-RC and resemble “green protectionism.”
  - **Looking ahead: COP31 (Türkiye, 2026) and the “Belém Mission to 1.5°C”**

    · **Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM):** Bihar ranked #1 state in India with a 92% online OPD registration rate via Scan-and-Share QR codes.
    · Accounts for 2.94 crore out of 11.38 crore national registrations since 2021, ahead of UP (2.25 cr) and AP (1.70 cr).

[u0709] Ch8 · Bihar Budget 2026–27
  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 174
  Bihar’s Budget for 2026–27 was presented by Finance Minister **Bijendra Prasad Yadav** on **February 3, 2026**.

[u0710] Ch8 · Key Highlights
  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 174
  - The size of Bihar’s budget has increased from ₹**23,885 crore in 2004–05** to ₹**3,47,589.76 crore (including debt repayment) in 2026–27**.
  - This reflects the state government’s increased fiscal capacity to spend on social sectors, infrastructure and welfare schemes.
  - After **Saat Nischay-1** and **Saat Nischay-2**, the government has now framed **Saat Nischay-3 for 2025–2030**, with the aim of taking Bihar towards the category of developed states.

[u0711] Ch8 · Overall Budget Size and Fiscal Framework
  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 174
  - For 2026–27, Bihar’s **net budget expenditure** is estimated at ₹**3,47,589.76 crore**, which is ₹**30,694.74 crore higher** than the Budget Estimate of ₹**3,16,895.02 crore** for 2025–26.
  - The annual scheme outlay is ₹**1,22,155.42 crore**, while establishment and committed expenditure is ₹**2,25,434.34 crore**.
  - Thus, scheme expenditure forms **35.14%** of total expenditure and establishment/committed expenditure forms **64.86%**.
  - The **Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP)** for 2026–27 is projected at about ₹**13.09 lakh crore**, showing an estimated growth of about **15%** over 2025–26.
  - The Budget targets a **revenue surplus of** ₹**1,143.19 crore** and a **fiscal deficit of** ₹**39,111.80 crore**, equal to **2.99% of GSDP**, which is within the 3% limit set under the Bihar Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management framework.
  - The expenditure excluding debt repayment is estimated at ₹**3,24,925 crore**, while debt repayment is about ₹**22,665 crore**.
  - In the Appropriation Bill, the state proposes **gross expenditure of** ₹**3,53,045.15 crore**, out of which ₹**3,02,242.13 crore** is voted expenditure and ₹**50,803.02 crore** is charged expenditure.
  - The net expenditure figure used in the budget summary is ₹**3,47,589.76 crore**.

[u0712] Ch8 · Revenue Receipts and Borrowings
  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 174
  - Total **revenue receipts** for 2026–27 are estimated at ₹**2,85,277.12 crore**. Of this, the state is expected to raise ₹**75,202.99 crore** from its own tax and non-tax sources, while about ₹**2,10,074 crore** will come from the Centre through tax devolution and grants.
  - This shows Bihar’s continuing dependence on central transfers.
  - The state’s own tax revenue is estimated at ₹**65,800 crore**.
  - Important components include **commercial taxes of** ₹**50,000 crore**, **stamp and registration fees of** ₹**10,000 crore**, **transport tax of** ₹**5,000 crore**, and **land revenue of** ₹**800 crore**.
  - State GST as the largest source of Bihar’s own tax revenue, with an estimated ₹**38,000 crore** in 2026–27.
  - The state’s own non-tax revenue is estimated at ₹**9,402.99 crore**, including ₹**4,200 crore from mining** and ₹**2,570.08 crore from interest receipts**.
  - Bihar’s share in central taxes is estimated at ₹**1,58,178.32 crore**, while grants-in-aid from the Centre are estimated at ₹**51,895.81 crore**.
  - These grants include ₹**44,002.81 crore for Centrally Sponsored Schemes** and ₹**7,893 crore linked to Finance Commission-related grants**, including disaster response and local body grants.
  - Borrowing of ₹**61,939.48 crore** is proposed for 2026–27.
  - The state also proposes to provide **loans and advances of** ₹**1,413.94 crore**.
  - Debt repayment is estimated at ₹**22,664.83 crore**, including repayment of loans taken earlier from the Centre and internal loans.

[u0713] Ch8 · Capital and Revenue Expenditure
  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 174
  - Total **capital expenditure** is estimated at ₹**63,455.84 crore**, which is **18.26%** of total expenditure.
  - This includes capital outlay of ₹**39,377.06 crore**, with ₹**6,117.30 crore** for general services, ₹**7,434.80 crore** for social services and ₹**25,824.96 crore** for economic services.
  - Total **revenue expenditure** is estimated at ₹**2,84,133.92 crore**, or **81.74%** of total expenditure.
  - This includes expenditure on salaries, pensions, interest payments, subsidies, grants and welfare schemes.
  - Under the state’s broader budget classification, spending on salaries, pensions, interest payments and debt repayment together is estimated at ₹**1,79,327.48 crore**.
  - Using the comparable committed-expenditure classification of salaries, pensions and interest payments, estimates committed expenditure at ₹**1,33,494 crore**, or **47% of revenue receipts**.

[u0714] Ch8 · Bihar’s Economy: Key Indicators
  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 174, 175
  - Bihar’s economy is projected to grow strongly in nominal terms. For 2026–27, GSDP at current prices is projected at ₹**13.09 lakh crore**.
  - In 2024–25, Bihar’s GSDP at constant prices is estimated to have grown by **8.6%**, compared to India’s GDP growth of **6.5%**.
  - At current prices, agriculture, manufacturing and services are estimated to contribute **22.37%**, **23.57%** and **54.07%** respectively to Bihar’s economy in 2024–25.
  - Bihar’s per capita GSDP at current prices for 2024– 25 is estimated at ₹**76,490**, while India’s per capita GDP is estimated at ₹**2,34,859**.
  - The state’s per capita NSDP for 2024–25 is estimated at ₹**69,321**, showing an increase of **11.45%** over the previous year.

  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 175
  - The Budget gives major importance to **Saat Nischay-3**, which is planned for the period **2025–2030**.

[u0716] Ch8 · Progress in Agriculture
  context: Bihar Budget 2026–27 · PDF p. 175
  - Three roadmaps have already been implemented, and work on the **Fourth Agriculture Roadmap, 2023–2028** will be accelerated.
  - A new state scheme, **Jannayak Karpoori Thakur Kisan Samman Nidhi**, will provide farmers ₹**3,000 per year** in addition to the ₹**6,000 per year** provided under the central PM-Kisan Samman Nidhi.
  - Foodgrain production has increased more than three times since 2005. According to the final estimates for 2024–25, total foodgrain production reached **326.62 lakh metric tonnes**, the highest ever for the state.
  - Bihar holds an important position in several agricultural products. In 2024–25, it ranked **first in makhana and litchi production**, **second in maize**, **fourth in honey**, **fifth in rice** and **sixth in wheat**.
  - The state produces about **85% of India’s makhana** and **23% of India’s litchi**.
  - The Government of India has also established a **National Makhana Board** in Bihar to support makhana farmers and improve production.
  - Bihar is also a leading state in mushroom production, with current production of about **44,000 metric tonnes**.
  - The government is working on an **AI-enabled Mechanised Agriculture Mission**, a **Bihar Agri Infrastructure Mission** with an initial investment vision of ₹**1 lakh crore**, and a **Bihar Agriculture Acceleration Mission** to promote agri-startups, GI-tagged products and rural agricultural markets.
  - In allied sectors, Bihar’s per capita annual fish availability is **12.21 kg**, calculated on the basis of 60% population coverage.
  - Women’s empowerment is one of the central themes of the Budget. Under **Jeevika**, about **1.40 crore families** have been connected through **11.45 lakh self-help groups**, and **91 lakh members** have received insurance coverage. At present, **31.71 lakh Jeevika Didis** have been declared **Lakhpati Didis**.
  - The **Mukhyamantri Mahila Rojgar Yojana** has been one of the most important welfare-cum-employment measures.
  - It aims to support self-employment among women, with initial financial help of ₹**10,000** and further assistance of up to ₹**2 lakh** for enterprise expansion.
  - Under the **Chief Minister Divyangjan Entrepreneur Scheme**, approved beneficiaries will receive loans and grants in a 50:50 ratio up to a maximum project cost of ₹**10 lakh per unit**.
  - For social justice, ₹**19,603.02 crore** has been allocated for Scheduled Castes and ₹**1,648.42 crore** for Scheduled Tribes in 2026–27.
  - According to Sample Registration System data, Bihar’s **Maternal Mortality Ratio** for 2021–23 is **104**.
  - In child health indicators, Bihar’s **Infant Mortality Rate** is **23**, **Neonatal Mortality Rate** is **18**, and **Under-5 Mortality Rate** is **27**.
  - Bihar’s IMR, NMR and Under-5 mortality rate are better than the national averages of **25**, **19** and **29** respectively, though its MMR remains above the national figure of **88**. These are based on the SRS 2023 values.
  - Bihar has maintained the first position on the Government of India’s **DVDMS Central Dashboard** for supply and distribution of medicines since September 2024.
  - Bihar has made major progress in basic infrastructure. The length of rural roads increased from **64,205 km in 2015–16** to about **1.19 lakh km in 2025–26**, making Bihar one of the states with high road density.
  - Per capita electricity consumption increased from **203 kWh in 2015** to **374 kWh in 2025**.

[u0718] Ch8 · Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26
  context: Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26 · PDF p. 176
  - According to the Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26, Bihar’s Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) at constant (2011–12) prices grew by 8.6 percent in 2024–25 (Q.E.), and at current prices by 13.1 percent, reaching ₹5,31,372 crore and ₹9,91,997 crore respectively.
  - In the last three years, Bihar’s economic growth rate, both at constant and current prices, has been higher than the Gross Domestic Product growth rate of India.
  - At current prices, Bihar’s GSDP growth rates were 17.9 percent in 2022–23 and 14.9 percent in 2023– 24, compared to 14.0 percent and 12.0 percent respectively for the country.
  - The quick estimates for 2024–25 show that Bihar’s GSDP growth rate at current prices is estimated at 13.1 percent compared to 9.8 percent for India. At constant (2011–12) prices, Bihar’s growth rate is estimated at 8.6 percent, which is higher than India’s 6.5 percent. These figures place Bihar among the fastest-growing states in the country.
  - For 2024–25, quick estimates show that the Gross State Value Added (GSVA) of the secondary sector grew by 11.1 percent at constant (2011–12) prices and 15.5 percent at current prices.
  - In 2024–25, the tertiary sector grew by 8.9 percent at constant prices and 13.5 percent at current prices, while the primary sector recorded growth of 4.1 percent at constant prices and 9.6 percent at current prices.
  - Between 2020–21 and 2024–25, the share of the secondary sector in GSVA increased significantly from 21.1 percent to 26.8 percent at constant (2011– 12) prices, mainly due to growth in construction and manufacturing.
  - In contrast, the share of the primary sector declined from 21.9 percent to 18.3 percent, and the share of the tertiary sector also declined from 57.0 percent to 54.8 percent, indicating increasing diversification in the state’s economic activities.
  - Per capita GSDP at current prices increased from ₹46,412 in 2020–21 to ₹76,490 in 2024–25, indicating a consistent upward trend.
  - At constant (2011–12) prices, per capita GSDP increased from ₹30,159 in 2020–21 to ₹40,973 in 2024–25.
  - Within Bihar, Patna district recorded the highest Gross District Domestic Product (GDDP), followed by Begusarai and Munger districts.
  - At current prices, per capita GDDP was ₹2,41,220 for Patna, ₹1,05,600 for Begusarai, and ₹93,921 for Munger.

[u0719] Ch8 · Public Financial Management
  context: Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26 · PDF p. 176, 177
  - Bihar’s fiscal development path reflects disciplined financial management, as the state government’s total expenditure increased from ₹1.66 lakh crore in 2020–21 to ₹2.82 lakh crore in 2024–25. The state government maintained fiscal prudence through moderate borrowing and effective debt servicing while consistently prioritising developmental expenditure in sectors such as education, health, energy, transport, irrigation, and agriculture.
  - This structural change reflects the government’s focus on long-term investment, infrastructure development, and asset creation.
  - Revenue receipts of the state government increased from ₹1.28 lakh crore in 2020–21 to ₹2.18 lakh crore in 2024–25, with tax revenue as the main source. The share of tax revenue increased from 70 percent to 84 percent, while the share of non-tax revenue declined from 5 percent to 2 percent and the share of grants-in-aid declined from 25 percent to 14 percent.
  - The structure of revenue expenditure remained largely stable over the last five years, with social services having the highest share at 46 percent. The share of economic services increased slightly from 21 percent in 2020-21 to 22 percent in 2024-25, while the share of general services declined slightly from 33 percent in 2020-21 to 32 percent in 2024- 25.
  - This distribution reflects the government’s commitment to both welfare expansion and strengthening productive sectors.
  - The share of plan expenditure in total expenditure increased from 38.3 percent in 2020–21 to 39.9 percent in 2024–25, while the share of establishment and committed expenditure declined from 61.7 percent to 60.1 percent.
  - This trend indicates a gradual shift towards welfare-oriented activities and policy-driven development priorities.
  - Developmental expenditure remained around two-thirds of the total budget, reaching 67.4 percent in 2024–25. It increased from ₹1,12,122 crore in 2020– 21 to ₹1,89,892 crore in 2024–25, indicating a stronger focus on development and welfare expenditure.
  - Receipts from the central government increased from ₹91,625 crore in 2020–21 to ₹1,59,298 crore in 2024–25, mainly due to transfers from the divisible tax pool, which increased from ₹59,861 crore in 2020-21 to ₹1,29,435 crore in 2024-25.
  - In 2024–25, grants-in-aid from the central government amounted to ₹29,863 crore.
  - The state’s own revenue increased from ₹36,543 crore in 2020–21 to ₹59,360 crore in 2024–25, with tax revenue as the major contributor.
  - As of 31 March 2025, the outstanding debt of the state government was ₹3,74,134 crore, which was 37.7 percent of the GSDP.
  - At the end of 2024–25, 84.6 percent of the total outstanding debt consisted of public debt. Out of total public debt, 83.8 percent came from internal borrowings, while 16.2 percent came from loans and advances from the central government.
  - Market loans and loans from financial institutions are the main components of internal debt.

[u0720] Ch8 · Agriculture and Allied Sector
  context: Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26 · PDF p. 177
  - According to provisional estimates released by the Directorate of Economics and Statistics for 2023– 24, agriculture, forestry, and fisheries together accounted for 23.1 percent of GSVA. Within this, crop production contributed 11.5 percent and livestock contributed 8.1 percent.
  - In 2023–24, rice, wheat, and maize together contributed 99.9 percent of total cereal production in Bihar. In 2024–25, rice accounted for 40.7 percent, wheat for 32.1 percent, and maize for 27.1 percent of total cereal production.
  - Between 2023–24 and 2024–25, production of rice increased by 4.3 percent, wheat by 7.1 percent, and maize by 12.6 percent.
  - Bihar is among the leading fruit and vegetable producing states in the country and is a major producer of litchi, makhana, and mushrooms. In 2023–24, total horticulture production in the state was estimated at about 230 lakh tonnes, which is higher than 206 lakh tonnes in 2019–20, with a major share coming from fruits and vegetables.
  - Between 2023–24 and 2024–25, milk production increased by 4.2 percent, egg production by 10.0 percent, and fish production by 9.9 percent.
  - The share of electricity consumption in agriculture increased significantly from 5.3 percent in 2020–21 to 14.9 percent in 2024–25.
  - In 2024–25, the number of Kisan Credit Card holders increased by 16.3 percent compared to 2023–24, while total credit disbursed increased by 32.8 percent from ₹7,080.07 crore in 2023–24 to ₹9,399.24 crore in 2024–25.
  - Total expenditure on irrigation in 2024–25 was ₹2,729.83 crore, of which 74.3 percent was capital expenditure. Between 2023–24 and 2024–25, total irrigation expenditure increased by 63.1 percent. This increase in public expenditure is reflected in the extensive expansion of major, medium, and minor irrigation projects.

  context: Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26 · PDF p. 177
  - Bihar’s secondary sector has experienced fluctuations over the past five years. After a sharp decline in 2020–21 due to the pandemic, it recovered in 2021– 22 and recorded rapid growth in 2022–23, mainly driven by mining, utilities, and construction.
  - In 2024–25, 37.8 lakh tourists visited 28 eco-tourism sites, which is 24.0 percent higher than the previous year. Total outlay for eco-tourism and park development in 2024–25 was ₹100.00 crore.

[u0722] Ch8 · Human Development
  context: Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26 · PDF p. 177
  - Over the past 19 years, expenditure on social services has increased rapidly, rising fivefold in the first decade and 15-fold between 2005–06 and 2024–25. Expenditure on health and education increased by 14.8 times and 13.2 times respectively.
  - Life expectancy at birth (LEB) in Bihar during 2019–2023 was 68.9 years for males and 69.7 years for females.
  - It is also notable that between 2017 and 2023, the infant mortality rate (IMR) declined by 12 points from 35 to 23.
  - Significant progress has been made in reducing maternal mortality Ratio (MMR), which declined from 312 deaths per lakh live births in 2004–2006 by 208 points to 104 in 2021–2023. Under the Janani Suraksha Yojana, institutional deliveries increased from 15.77 lakh in 2020–21 to 16.17 lakh in 2024–25 (an increase of 2.54 percent).
  - In 2024–25, total enrolment at the elementary level school is 159.22 lakh, including 80.87 lakh boys and 78.35 lakh girls. Total enrolment was 27.54 lakh at the secondary level and 20.33 lakh at the higher secondary level.

[u0723] Ch8 · Environment, Climate Change and Disaster Management
  context: Bihar Economic Survey, 2025–26 · PDF p. 177
  - Between 2011–12 and 2024–25, Bihar’s forestry and logging sector achieved a compound annual growth rate of 10.4 percent at current prices. The total value increased from ₹4,187 crore to ₹15,139 crore during this period.
  - By 2024–25, this sector accounted for 7.0 percent of GSVA in agriculture and 2.0 percent of the state’s total GSVA.
  - Since 2011, Bihar’s ecological footprint has expanded significantly. The total area under forest and tree cover increased by 689 sq km from 9,214 sq km to 9,903 sq km by 2023.
  - The total forest cover increased from 6,845 sq km to 7,532 sq km during this period. Notably, the area under very dense forests (VDF) increased significantly from 231 sq km to 387 sq km, indicating successful conservation within protected areas.
  - Bihar’s commissioned power capacity is 11,764 MW, with the major share coming from thermal power (7,743 MW). Solar energy is the largest renewable source with a contribution of 1,922 MW, followed by hydropower at 1,356 MW and wind energy at 699 MW.
  - In 2024–25, the total budget allocation for the Urban Development and Housing Department was ₹5,274.9 crore and total expenditure was ₹3,613.8 crore, with a utilisation rate of 68.5 percent.
  - The credit-to-GSDP ratio in Bihar increased steadily over the last five years from 30.9 percent in 2020–21 to 37.9 percent in 2024–25, indicating increased availability of credit in the state’s economy.
  - The percentage of non-performing assets (NPAs) in banks in Bihar declined significantly over the last five years from 11.8 percent in 2020–21 to 7.6 percent in 2024–25.
