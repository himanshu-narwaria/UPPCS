  context: A. Recent Astronomical Activities · PDF p. 121
  The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) launched the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission on **20 October 2026** aboard **H3 Flight No. 10** from **Tanegashima Space Center, Japan**. It aims to achieve the world’s first sample return from the Martian system. Its planned duration is approximately five years.
  **Key Highlights**
  - **Mission objectives:** Study Phobos and Deimos, Mars’s two moons, and return **at least 10 grams of material from Phobos**.
  - **Timeline:** Arrival at Mars is expected in **2027**, and sample delivery to Earth in **2031**.
  - **Scientific significance:** Determine whether the moons are **captured asteroids** or debris from a **giant impact on Mars**, improving understanding of planetary formation and the delivery of water and organic compounds.
  **Technical Features**
  - **Spacecraft:** Three modules—propulsion, exploration and return—use solar panels for electricity and chemical propulsion for manoeuvres.
  - **Orbit:** A **quasi-satellite orbit** around Mars near Phobos supports surface mapping and landing-site selection.
  - **Instruments:** The spacecraft carries **13 mission instruments**. **TENGOO** provides approximately **40 cm imaging resolution**; **OROCHI** captures images at multiple wavelengths; **LIDAR** measures surface distance using laser pulses.
  - **Spectrometers:** NASA’s **MEGANE** (Mars-moon Exploration with Gamma rays and Neutrons) measures gamma rays and neutrons to determine elemental composition. **MIRS** (MMX InfraRed Spectrometer) operates at **0.9–3.6 micrometres**, investigating minerals, water-related substances and organic matter.
  - **Rover: IDEFIX**, jointly developed by France’s CNES and Germany’s DLR, will assess surface conditions before the spacecraft lands.
  - **Sampling:** JAXA’s **C-Sampler** collects subsurface material, while NASA’s **P-Sampler** uses high-pressure nitrogen gas to gather surface particles.
  - **Communication and return:** NASA’s **Deep Space Network** supports communication and navigation. A **Sample Return Capsule** will bring samples through Earth’s atmosphere; NASA will receive about **15%** for research and curation.

[u0455] Ch5 · Chandrayaan-3 Hop Experiment
  context: A. Recent Astronomical Activities · PDF p. 122
  A team of scientists from the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) on **May 18, 2026** shared a report of the findings of the ‘Hop’ experiment conducted by the Vikram Lander of Chandrayaan-3 and the Chandra’s Surface Thermophysical Experiment (ChaSTE) on the Moon.
  - A new study conducted during the final phase of the Chandrayaan-3 missions when the Vikram lander performed a successful hop experiment, has offered fresh insights into the Moon surface.
  - During the final phase of its mission, Chandrayaan-3’s Vikram lander reignited its engines to perform a controlled “hop” experiment (primarily to test ascent capabilities for future sample-return missions) and landed nearby, nearly 50 cm away from its initial position.
  - ChaSTE (Chandra’s Surface Thermophysical Experiment), a lunar thermometer was re-penetrated into the lunar regolith (soil) at the new location.
  - By analysing the ChaSTE data following this historic “hop” monoeuvre by the Vikram lander, scientists have discovered that the post-hop location at the Moon’s southern polar region possesses a complex, layered soil structure that was significantly altered by the lander’s brief flight.
  **Key findings from the study have emerged, which include:**
  - **Layered Stratigraphy:** analysis revealed a distinct two-layer structure within the top 6.5 cm of the post-hop site which means that the top few centimetres of the Moon’s surface aren’t just a uniform pile of dust; they are made of distinct layers with different properties,
  - **Geotechnical Variability:** the soil’s cohesion and density were found to increase significantly with depth which means that the Moon’s soil at the surface becomes more packed and sticky as we go deeper and **Twilight Thermal Profiles:** the data highlighted a “shadowing effect” where local craters and depressions caused temperatures to plunge faster than global models predicted.
  - **Surface Erosion and Compaction:** The engine firing blew away the top 3 cm of regolith, exposing and slightly compacting the material underneath.

[u0457] Ch5 · Artemis II Mission
  context: A. Recent Astronomical Activities · PDF p. 123
  Artemis II was not a Moon-landing mission; it was a **crewed lunar flyby mission** meant to prove that NASA’s Space Launch System, Orion spacecraft, ground systems, recovery teams and mission procedures can safely carry humans beyond low Earth orbit and bring them back. NASA describes it as the first crewed Artemis flight and a key step toward long-term human return to the Moon and future crewed missions to Mars.
  - Artemis II lifted off from the Kennedy Space Center on April 1, 2026, sending the first humans to the Moon since 1972. (Note: The Apollo 17 mission in 1972 sent humans to land on the Moon, whereas Artemis II sent humans toward the Moon.)
  - During the mission, the crew completed a lunar flyby, returning humans to the Moon’s vicinity for the first time in 53 years after the final Apollo Mission to the Moon.
  **Engineering Focus of the Mission**
  - The Artemis II Orion heat shield is the critical thermal protection system on the base of Orion Spacecraft. Orion heat shield protect the Capsule and Astronauts from searing temperatures and Extreme friction during high speed atmospheric re-entry.
  - During the flight, the astronauts tested Orion’s life-support, communications, control, navigation and manual operation systems in a deep-space environment.
  - The mission also tested how well astronauts could live and work inside Orion during a lunar-return flight.
  - This is important because Artemis is not only about reaching the Moon; it is about building the systems needed for repeated journeys, lunar surface work, and eventually Mars missions.
  - The Orion Spacecraft used in Artemis II was named Integrity by the crew.
  **Orion’s Heat Shield and Re-entry Lesson**
  - One of the most important engineering lessons came from Orion’s heat shield. During Artemis I, NASA found unexpected cracking and shedding of heat-shield material.
  - NASA later explained that heat and gases had accumulated inside the Avcoat material, creating internal pressure and uneven shedding during re-entry, although the spacecraft would still have kept a crew safe.
  - For Artemis II, NASA closely monitored re-entry and the thermal protection system.
  - The spacecraft returned at extremely high lunar-return speed, and NASA’s initial inspection found that the heat shield performed as expected and that heat-shield char loss was significantly reduced compared with Artemis I.
  - This result is important for astronaut safety in future Moon-landing missions.
  - Artemis III is now listed by NASA as a 2027 low-Earth orbit demonstration, mission to test one or both commerical landers from SpaceX and Blue Origin.

[u0478] Ch5 · JAMA Blood Group in Humans
  context: C. Body and Health Related Scientific Activities · PDF p. 130
  The International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT) has recognised JAMA as the 49th human blood group system, numbered ISBT 049. The discovery followed a joint investigation by researchers in the United Kingdom and Israel into unusual blood samples from a woman and her brother. ISBT ratified the system at its Kuala Lumpur Congress in 2026 and announced it on **September 14, 2026.** The finding adds to the understanding of rare blood characteristics and their possible importance in transfusion medicine.
  **Key Highlights**
  - Blood plays a vital role in the human body, carrying oxygen and essential nutrients to cells. For years, A, B, AB and O have been the blood groups most familiar to people.
  - Now, scientists have identified a new blood group system called JAMA, taking the total number of recognised blood group systems to 49.
  - The International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT) has officially recognised JAMA as the 49th blood group system.
  - The discovery came from a joint investigation by researchers from the UK and Israel. It began when scientists examined the blood of an Israeli woman and her brother.
  - The woman was found to have an antibody that reacted with the red blood cells of most people.
  - However, the same reaction did not occur with her own red blood cells or those of her brother.
  - This unusual result caught researchers’ attention because the antibody did not correspond to any previously identified blood group antigen.
  - JAMA should not be viewed as another category alongside A, B, AB and O. Instead, it is a separate blood group system involving a specific antigen.
  - This antigen is found on the red blood cells of most people. In the woman and her brother, however, it was absent.
  - Their blood also contained a corresponding antibody, helping scientists identify the unusual blood group characteristics.

  **Key Highlights**
  - **High Thrust FFSC Rocket Engines –** FFSC engines route fuel and oxidiser through separate pre-burners before the main combustion chamber.   - **Efficiency and Reusability –** Unlike conventional engines, which use only part of the propellants to drive turbopumps, FFSC engines improve turbopump and propulsion efficiency. However, the cycle is complex to design, develop and execute.
  - **Significance of EVEREST –** EVEREST is presented as India’s first LOX-methane high-thrust engine and the fourth commercial FFSC development globally, after SpaceX, Stoke Space and LandSpace. Advanced manufacturing includes India’s largest industrial 3D printer.
